## 3. Charles's commentary, separated from translation

- **90:1:** Thirty-six is judged a likely error for thirty-five; 12 + 23 gives the first two periods. The third period's count closes with 12 + 23 + 23 = 58.
- **90:2–4:** Eagles = Greeks/Macedonians; ravens = Seleucid Syrians; vultures/kites = Ptolemaic Egyptians. The shepherd of 90:3 may be Ptolemy I (Ptolemaeus Lagi). Dogs in 90:4 are connected to the Philistines by reference to 89:42, 46–47.
- **90:5:** Twenty-three shepherds correspond to the Graeco-Egyptian period; Charles dates its transition to Graeco-Syrian supremacy around 200 BCE.
- **90:6–7:** Lambs = Chasids/Asideans, a party Charles argues existed before the Maccabean rising; white sheep = faithful adherents of the Theocracy.
- **90:8:** The lamb seized and killed is Onias III, whom Charles dates to 171 BCE; he acknowledges the symbol is not a perfect fit.
- **90:9–14:** Horned lambs = Maccabean family; great horn = Judas Maccabaeus. The enemies in 90:11–13 are primarily Syrian forces; Charles admits that the symbols for vultures and kites may shift, and tentatively suggests Ammon and Edom in 90:13.
- **90:15–19:** Charles regards 90:15 as an interpolation and would interpret 90:19 before 90:16. The hostile coalition in 90:16 includes Gentile nations and apostate Jews; Judas remains the great horn. The sword in 90:19 signifies national vengeance against Israel's oppressors.
- **90:20–27:** The pleasant land is Palestine and the throne is near Jerusalem. Seven white ones are seven archangels; the seventy shepherds are judged with the fallen Watchers; the blinded sheep are apostates cast into Gehenna.
- **90:28–29:** Old Jerusalem is removed and the New Jerusalem established.
- **90:30–34:** Surviving Gentiles submit to Israel; the righteous dead and dispersed Israel are gathered into the kingdom. The ram in 90:31 is the sheep saved in 89:52 and brought to Enoch.
- **90:35–36:** Clean, abundant wool symbolizes the righteousness and its abundance among the kingdom's members.
- **90:37–38:** The white bull is the Messiah in Charles's interpretation; transformation of the creatures signifies restoration to primordial/Edenic righteousness.
- **90:39–42:** The vision closes; Enoch's tears arise from the woes disclosed in his two dream-visions.

These are Charles's nineteenth-century interpretations, not neutral facts or words in the translated text. They are preserved as attributed commentary.
