## 2. Translation witness: repository comparison

- **Chapter C (100):** The repository's C.2 contains a duplicated phrase: “slaying his sons, and his sons, and the sinner.” The 1893 text reads “slaying his sons and his sons’ sons, and the sinner.” This is a clear transcription error and is corrected in the formatted derivative. At C.4, “those who brought down sin” is the printed reading; Charles's apparatus records “who aided sin” as a variant. Do not replace the printed reading.
- **C.5:** Charles explicitly interprets the guarded righteous as departed souls, not living people. This is commentary, not a translation gloss. The repository's translation should remain unexpanded.
- **Chapter CI (101):** Charles's printed English uses “sailors of the ships” at CI.4 and CI.9. He explains that he accepts Hallévi's emendation of a reading meaning “kings of the ships” to “sailors of the ships.” The repository follows the 1893 English and should not revert to the literal disputed reading.
- **Chapter CII (102):** The 1893 OCR shows a square bracket opening at CII.4 (“[4 Fear ye not, ye souls of the righteous…”). The repository does not mark CII.4–11 as bracketed. The OCR excerpt does not make the closing extent of the bracket secure, and Charles's commentary discusses CII.4–5 as part of the address to the righteous. Do not add or remove brackets until the page image is checked.
- **CIII.1–8:** The repository follows the printed text in substance. At CIII.8, the 1893 printing includes “chains (lit. ‘net’)”; the repository has “chains” without the explanatory literal note. This is a minor omission from the translation note, not grounds to rewrite the verse.
- **CIII.9–15:** The repository uses third-person forms (“they are…”, “they hoped…”), matching Charles's adopted translation. Charles explains at length that the manuscripts diverge between first and third person; he deliberately adopts third person throughout these verses. This editorial choice must be stated rather than presented as unanimous manuscript evidence.
- **CIV.1–13:** The repository substantially follows the printed translation. Preserve the distinction between the righteous being “companions of the hosts of heaven” (CIV.6) and Charles's commentary that the author's final hope is heavenly rather than a temporary earthly kingdom.

- **CV.1–2:** Charles prints CV as a distinct unit after CIV, outside the literary unity of XCI–CIV. The translation includes the address to the “children of earth” and the promise that “I and My Son will unite with them”; the text closes “Amen.” The OCR places the bracketed opening “[CV. 1.” and shows CV.2 ending “Amen.” Keep this chapter in the 1893 p.301 transcription, but label its separate literary status.\n\n## 3. - **CV:** Charles says this chapter does not seem to belong to XCI–CIV: “children of earth” has a positive ethical sense here, the Messiah (“My Son”) appears without parallel in XCI–CIV, and the chapter emphasizes finite earthly life rather than the heavenly immortality central to XCI–CIV. He treats CV as a literary revival of Old Testament thought.\n\n## 4. Charles's commentary, separately attributed

- **C.1–3:** Charles sees C.1 as an expansion of the bloodshed in XCIX.6. He tentatively connects C.2 with the murder of Antigonus by Aristobulus I, citing Josephus, *Antiquities* 13.11.1–2. This is a historical conjecture, not an identification stated in the verse.
- **C.4:** Charles interprets “those who brought down sin” as the fallen angels, temporarily confined in earthly abysses or “secret places.”
- **C.5:** Charles argues that the guarded righteous are departed souls, not living people. He compares the “chambers” or repositories of souls in 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch, and reads their “long sleep” as lasting until the final judgment at the close of the tenth week. On this reading, resurrection follows the final judgment, not the beginning of the temporary Messianic kingdom.
- **C.6:** Charles understands “the children of earth” as the wicked, apostates, and paganizers, in contrast with the “children of heaven.”
- **CI:** Charles says the heavenly bodies and natural elements testify against sinners. He treats CI.12 (“give presents to the rain”) ironically. His acceptance of “sailors” is an explicit emendation, not an unmarked literal reading.
- **CII.1–3:** Charles reads the passage as a future terror at the day of the Lord, when even the heavenly powers are afraid. “A grievous fire” is interpreted as the fire of hell.
- **CII.4–11:** Charles presents the righteous as facing the same outward suffering and death as sinners, while the wicked use this to argue that no advantage exists beyond death. The author replies by contrasting the material aims of the wicked with the spiritual and eternal aims of the righteous.
- **CIII.1–4:** Charles reads the oath and heavenly books as assurance that reward is prepared for the righteous dead.
- **CIII.5–8:** The wicked are portrayed as having enjoyed worldly prosperity but descending to Sheol and punishment. Charles distinguishes the use of Sheol here from its use for the righteous in other passages.
- **CIII.9–15:** Charles argues these verses are spoken sarcastically by the wicked about the righteous' earthly suffering. He adopts third-person grammar because G overwhelmingly supports it and because the literary speakers are the wicked; he rejects Dillmann's first-person interpretation.
- **CIV.1–6:** Charles says the author responds by turning back to the righteous, assuring them that angels remember them and that their destiny is heavenly. He explicitly says the hope is not fulfilled in the temporary earthly Messianic kingdom.
- **CIV.7–13:** Charles reads this as a warning that sins are recorded daily and that Enoch's writings will be transmitted through languages and generations, despite attempts to corrupt them.
