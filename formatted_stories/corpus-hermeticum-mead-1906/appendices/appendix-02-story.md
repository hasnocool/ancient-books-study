# The Encomium of Kings — Story Adaptation

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), supplementary text, “About the Soul’s Being Hindered by the Passion of the Body.”
> **Coverage:** All 16 numbered paragraphs in the repository transcription.
> **Status:** Provisional adaptation; not facsimile-collated or independently human-reviewed.

The speaker begins with an analogy between a musician and the instrument through which music is performed. When an instrument is too weak or out of tune, the failure should not be blamed on the musician's art. The same distinction is applied to divine activity and the human body: the weakness belongs to the instrument, not to the unwearied divine source.

The speaker develops the image through examples of musical performance. A broken or inadequate instrument can obstruct excellent music, while the skill of the artist remains. One story tells of a harper whose broken string is replaced, in the contest, by a grasshopper that completes the melody. The speaker turns this into an image of his own situation: despite weakness, the strength of a superior power enables him to continue his song.

The song now rises first to the highest King, God, from whom victories descend. It then turns to earthly kings, whose authority is praised for securing peace and restraining conflict. The speaker presents praise of rulers as an exercise that should begin from reverence toward God and return ultimately to the divine source.

The discourse closes by linking blessing, gratitude, and peace. Human beings cannot adequately praise God, just as children cannot fully express a parent's greatness, but they can offer thanks according to their capacity. The king's name and image are celebrated as signs of protection and peaceful order.
