# Tractate XIV — A Letter of Thrice-Greatest Hermes to Asclepius
## Story adaptation

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate XIV.
>
> **Editorial status:** Provisional adaptation of the repository's formatted Mead translation. It has not been collated against the 1906 facsimile or independently human-reviewed. This is a prose retelling, not a translation.

### The letter begins

Hermes writes to Asclepius because, while Asclepius was away, Tat had asked further questions about the nature of things. Tat was eager to understand each point and would not allow Hermes to postpone the explanation. Hermes therefore gave him a fuller account, then chose its chief arguments and sent them to Asclepius in a concise, more mystical form, assuming that Asclepius's greater age and familiarity with Nature would help him follow it.

### The Maker and what is made

Hermes begins from the visible world. Things that appear and come into being do not make themselves. They are many, diverse, and unlike one another; their making therefore points beyond them to a maker. That maker cannot itself be another made thing, for the source of all made things must be more ancient than what depends upon it.

This source is supreme, one, and alone. It governs the number, magnitude, difference, and continuing production of things. Made things can be seen; the Maker cannot. Hermes presents this invisibility not as evidence of absence, but as bound up with the Maker's unceasing act of making. To understand this is to wonder, and to wonder is to count oneself blessed for learning to know one's Father.

### God, Maker, and Father

Hermes asks what could be sweeter than one's true Father, and how that Father may be known. He gives three names that should be understood together: God for Power, Maker for Energy, and Father for Good. Power is distinguished from the things made, while Energy is present in the process and reality of making. Hermes insists that there is no middle term between Maker and made.

The reader is urged to remember this pair in everything perceived or heard. All things belong to the relation of Maker and made; neither can be considered apart from the other. Their union is so close that Hermes describes them as inseparable, like a thing from itself.

He then addresses a possible objection: the world's variety and changing conditions should not be taken as dishonour to God. God's glory is precisely the making of all things. The defects and passions that accompany the process are compared with rust on brass or filth on a body: they are not the work or intention of the maker. Change serves as a cleansing of the process of becoming.

### The charge of impiety

Hermes challenges the idea that a human painter can depict heaven, gods, earth, sea, people, animals, plants, and lifeless things, while God could somehow be unable to make all things. To deny God's universal making is, in his argument, a failure to know God. It wrongly attributes arrogance, incapacity, or other passions to the divine.

God has one passion only, Hermes says: Good. The Good has the power to make all, and everything capable of being made is made through that Good. Hermes then offers to explain how this making occurs.

### The seed and the fourfold pattern

The explanation begins with a farmer. One person scatters different seeds in the ground and plants different kinds of trees. In a corresponding way, God sows Immortality in Heaven, Change on Earth, and Life and Motion in the universe. Hermes closes by naming Genesis and God in connection with all that exists. The passage's final enumeration is compressed and should be read with care rather than expanded into a scheme the source does not explicitly spell out.

The letter thus returns to its central claim: the many forms and processes of the world depend upon the one Maker, and the visible variety of what is made should lead the learner toward knowledge and wonder at its source.
