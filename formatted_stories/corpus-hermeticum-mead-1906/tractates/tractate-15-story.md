# Tractate XV — The Definitions of Asclepius unto King Ammon
## Story adaptation

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate XV.
>
> **Editorial status:** Provisional prose adaptation of the repository's formatted Mead translation. It has not been collated against the 1906 facsimile or independently human-reviewed. This is not a translation.

### A teaching meant to be guarded

Asclepius sends King Ammon a teaching that gathers and condenses earlier instruction. He warns that it is not shaped to please the many: it challenges common assumptions and may even appear to contradict other Hermetic sermons. Hermes had explained that the writing can look simple while concealing an inner meaning. If it is translated into Greek, Asclepius fears, the sound and force of the Egyptian words may be lost beneath Greek verbal display.

He therefore urges the king, as far as possible, to preserve the teaching in its native tongue. In this account, sacred names are not merely labels or ornaments of argument. Their sounds are presented as active, carrying the power of what they name.

### The One and the fullness of all

Asclepius begins with an invocation to God, the Lord, Maker, Father, and Encompasser of all. God is All and One: the fullness of everything is in the One, not as a second thing added beside it. To divide the All from the One would destroy the fullness that makes it All.

He asks Ammon to keep this unity in mind throughout the teaching. The many are not to be torn away from their source and treated as a separate ultimate reality.

### Earth, the Sun, and the circulation of life

The teaching turns to Earth, where fire, water, and earth appear together, issuing from one root. Earth receives what comes from above and gives forth from its abundance. Asclepius then identifies the Sun as the Demiurge: the ordering power that draws things toward itself, gives substance, and sustains the movements of Heaven and Earth.

The Sun's activity reaches through Heaven and Air down to Earth and the depths. A further passage speaks of a subtle essence grasped by mind alone, whose reservoir is Light. Its origin is known only to the Sun. Although the Sun is not seen directly in the manner of ordinary objects, the teaching says that its presence may be approached through conjecture and its rays.

### The cosmic charioteer

The Sun's rays illuminate the Cosmos above and below. Asclepius compares the Sun to a skilled charioteer standing at the centre and keeping the cosmic team in order. Life, Soul, Spirit, Deathlessness, and Genesis are named as the reins. The Sun's activity nourishes the deathless regions and gives life to the changing beings of the lower world through birth and transformation.

Beings change in a spiral movement, passing from genus to genus and species to species. For bodies, permanence is described through transformation. The immortal body is distinguished from the mortal by the absence of dissolution; mortal bodies undergo dissolution.

### Daimons and the human condition

The Sun's life-giving power is continuous. Around it are many kinds of daimons, who dwell among mortals while remaining near the immortals. They oversee human affairs and carry out divine allotments through storms, winds, earthquakes, famine, and war. The sermon connects such requital particularly with human impiety.

The duties are distinguished: the Gods give benefits, human beings give worship, and daimons administer requital. Human errors arising from ignorance, necessity, or rashness are not treated in the same way as impiety.

The Sun is also described as preserver and nurse of every class of life. Beneath it are many orders of daimons, associated with the stars. Their nature is discussed in terms of their activities, which may be good, bad, or mixed. They influence earthly affairs, communities, individuals, and the bodily life of human beings.

At birth, daimons associated with the configuration of Genesis take hold of people and incline the soul toward their activities. Yet the rational part of the soul is described as standing above their rule and as capable of receiving God. A ray of God may shine through this rational part; the text says that those reached by it are few and that no daimon or God has power against that ray. The broader human economy governed by daimons is named Heimarmenē, or Fate.

### The chain of dependence

The teaching sets out a hierarchy: the Intelligible World depends on God; the Sensible World depends on the Intelligible; the Sun mediates divine Good through both; the eight spheres depend on the Sun; daimons depend on the spheres; and human beings depend on the daimons. In this way, all things are said to depend on God.

God is the Father of all, the Sun its Demiurge, and the Cosmos the instrument of demiurgic operation. The closing claim returns to unity: all things are parts of God, and if all are parts, God is All. In making all things, God makes Himself; because God is without beginning or end, His making likewise has no beginning or end.
