# Tractate VI — In God Alone Is Good, and Elsewhere Nowhere
## Story adaptation

**Source:** G. R. S. Mead's 1906 English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 6, “In God Alone Is Good, and Elsewhere Nowhere.”
**Status:** Provisional adaptation; the source transcription has not been collated against the 1906 facsimile.

### The Good that needs nothing

Hermes tells Asclepius that Good is found in God alone; indeed, Good is God eternally. The Good is not subject to motion or becoming and does not depend on anything outside itself. It is complete, inexhaustible, and the source of all. God lacks nothing, cannot be deprived of anything, and has no rival capable of overpowering or injuring Him. Envy, anger, need, and pain have no place in this transcendent being.

### Why the world is not the Good itself

Hermes argues that the other things found in the cosmos are mixed with conditions that do not belong to the perfect Good. Created things are subject to birth, change, motion, and passion. Where there is passion, he says, the Good in its absolute sense is absent.

The cosmos nevertheless shares in Good insofar as it makes and sustains things. But it is also moving, changeable, and the maker of things that can suffer. It is therefore not Good in the same unqualified way as God.

### Human judgments and the turning toward appetite

Among human beings, Hermes says, good is judged comparatively: what seems less bad may be called good. Yet anything mixed with bad cannot remain perfectly good. Human beings often give the name “good” to things that are bound up with bodily labor, pain, desire, passion, error, and foolish thought.

Hermes identifies belly-lust—appetite and bodily indulgence—as a leading error that draws people away from the Good. The difficulty is that people become attached to the very things they cannot easily avoid and mistake their necessity or pleasure for the highest good.

### The Beautiful and the Good

Hermes gives thanks for understanding that the perfect Good is not simply one of the objects found in the world. The world is described as full of bad, while God is full of Good. The Beautiful is closely joined to the essence of the Good and to God.

Visible things are described as images or pictures, while the realities that do not meet the eye—especially the Beautiful and the Good—cannot be grasped by ordinary sight. Just as the eye cannot see God, it cannot directly behold the Beautiful and the Good. They belong inseparably to God.

### The path of Devotion and Gnosis

Hermes tells Asclepius that if he can conceive God, he can also conceive the Beautiful and the Good. These cannot be separated from God or joined to other living things as independent possessions. The search for God is therefore also a search for the Beautiful.

Hermes names one path: Devotion joined with Gnosis. Those who do not know and do not follow this path mistake human beings and worldly things for the true Beautiful and Good. They may even fear losing the things that they wrongly regard as the greatest goods, and strive to preserve or increase them.

The discourse closes on this difficult human condition: people depend on many things they cannot simply flee, yet they are warned not to confuse those necessities and attachments with the Good itself.

## Adaptation notes

- This is a prose retelling of the six numbered sections, not a new translation.
- The distinction between absolute Good, the changing cosmos, and human judgments is retained.
- “Devotion” and “Gnosis” are kept as the source's key terms; the adaptation does not reduce them to generic belief or information.
- The tractate's criticism of appetite is reported as the text's argument, not offered as medical or nutritional advice.
- The source's philosophical hierarchy is not presented as a modern scientific account.
