## Story

While reflecting on existence, the narrator's thought rises beyond ordinary bodily awareness. A vast Being calls him by name and asks what he wishes to understand. The narrator says that he wants to know what exists, how things are constituted, and how God may be known. The Being identifies himself as Poemandres, the Shepherd of Men and Mind of supreme authority, and promises instruction.

A vision opens before the narrator. At first he sees boundless Light. Darkness then gathers, taking on a twisting, serpent-like appearance. It becomes a turbulent, moist Nature, filled with smoke, groaning, and an inarticulate cry like fire. From the Light, a holy Word descends upon this Nature. Pure Fire rises upward; Air follows; Earth and Water remain mingled below, moved by the Word that permeates them.

Poemandres explains the vision. The Light is God, Mind, and the source that precedes the dark and moist Nature. The Word that appears from Mind is the Son of God. Mind and Word are not separate in the life they express. The narrator then sees the Light within Mind, innumerable Powers, and a vast ordered Cosmos. Fire is surrounded and brought into a settled order.

The guide describes the archetypal pattern behind the visible world. Nature receives the Word and looks toward the beautiful Cosmos, then forms a cosmos through her elements and the generation of souls. God, described as both male and female and as Light and Life, brings forth another Mind. This formative Mind establishes seven Rulers around the perceptible cosmos, whose rule human beings call Fate.

The formative Mind sets the celestial spheres in motion. Nature, meanwhile, produces living things from the lower elements: winged creatures from Air, swimmers from Water, and land animals from Earth and Water. God, the All-Father Mind, also brings forth Man as an image of the divine, beautiful and sharing in the Father's formations.

Man looks upon the created order and wishes to take part in forming. He receives authority to enter the formative sphere and learns the nature of the Rulers and their domains. He then passes beyond the boundary of the spheres and looks down toward Nature. Nature sees the divine form reflected in her waters and earth; Man, in turn, sees a form like himself in Nature and desires to dwell in it. Through this mutual attraction, the human being becomes joined to material Nature.

The text therefore describes humanity as twofold: mortal through the body, yet immortal in its essential nature. Human beings possess a divine inheritance but are also subject to bodily conditions and Fate. Nature gives birth to seven corresponding human forms, associated with the seven Rulers. The account then describes the separation of male and female among living creatures and the multiplication of life. Humanity is told to increase and to learn that it is deathless, while also being warned about the danger of attachment to bodily existence.

The narrator asks why people who are ignorant should lose the possibility of immortality. Poemandres answers that material embodiment is rooted in Darkness and Moist Nature, and that death belongs to the bodily order. The way back to Life begins when a person recognizes that their true origin is Light and Life. The divine Mind is present with those who are holy, good, pure, and merciful, guiding them toward knowledge of the Father. Those ruled by destructive appetites and impiety turn away from this guidance and remain bound to disorder.

Poemandres then describes the soul's ascent after bodily dissolution. The body returns to change; the senses return to their sources; passion and desire return to the irrational nature. As the person passes through the seven zones, the powers and faults associated with each are left behind: growth and decline, deceit, desire, arrogance, rashness, wrongful greed, and falsehood. Freed from these influences, the person reaches the eighth Nature and joins in praise of the Father. The final goal is union with God.

Having delivered this teaching, Poemandres mingles with the Powers. The narrator gives thanks and turns to other people, urging them to leave ignorance and destructive excess and to seek knowledge of God. Some reject the message; others ask to be taught. The narrator guides those who respond, teaches them the way of salvation, and leads them in thanksgiving.

At the close, the narrator reflects that bodily sleep has become the awakening of the soul, silence has become fruitful, and true vision has opened through the teaching he received. He ends with a hymn praising the Father, the source of all things, whose Word gives being to the world. He asks to remain in knowledge and to carry Light to those who remain in ignorance.

## Source notes and adaptation choices

- This is a prose summary of Mead's 32 numbered sections, not a new translation.
- The sequence of vision, cosmological explanation, human formation, ethical teaching, ascent, and hymn is retained.
- “Man,” “Nature,” “Mind,” “Word,” “Fate,” and the seven Rulers follow Mead's terminology. The adaptation does not replace them with modern scientific or technological terms.
- The account's descriptions of cosmic formation and human origins are presented as claims within the tractate's revelatory cosmology, not as independently established historical or scientific events.
- Several bracketed readings and ellipses in the formatted source remain unresolved; no missing wording has been reconstructed.
- Printed page boundaries are not supplied in the repository extract and have not been inferred.
