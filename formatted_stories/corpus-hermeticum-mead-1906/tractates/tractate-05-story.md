# Tractate V — Though Unmanifest, God Is Most Manifest
## Story adaptation

**Source:** G. R. S. Mead's 1906 English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 5, “Though Unmanifest, God Is Most Manifest.”
**Status:** Provisional adaptation; the source transcription has not yet been collated against the 1906 facsimile.

### The lesson to Tat

Hermes tells Tat that he will explain a further mystery: how the divine reality that seems hidden to most people can become most evident to a mind prepared to understand it. What becomes visible or manifest belongs to the realm of becoming; the Unmanifest, by contrast, simply is. It does not come into existence by being displayed. Rather, it is the enduring source through which other things are brought into manifestation.

Hermes says that the unmade God makes all things manifest through thought. Tat is urged first to pray to the One and Only Father for the ability to receive even a single ray of understanding. The Unmanifest is not grasped by ordinary outward sight. Thought, itself unseen, is the means by which the mind may apprehend what the eyes cannot. Yet Tat is also challenged to recognize what remains hidden within himself before expecting to perceive the divine through external eyes.

### Reading the order of the cosmos

Hermes directs Tat to consider the sun, the moon, and the ordered courses of the stars. Their movements differ, and each follows boundaries of place and number. The sun, though pre-eminent among the heavenly gods in this account, still moves within a larger order. Hermes asks who establishes these courses and measures.

He extends the questions to the Bear constellation, the turning cosmos, the boundaries of the sea, and the earth's place. Number, measure, and order cannot maintain themselves without a maker and lord. Even what is described as disorder is not independent of a higher ordering power.

Hermes then imagines Tat rising above the earth, between earth and heaven, to behold the solidity of the land, the movement of waters, the expanse of air, the swiftness of fire, the stars, and the circuit of heaven. The vision would reveal many motions held within one governance: the motionless source expressed through motion, and the Unmanifest made manifest in the ordered cosmos.

### The work within the human body

The lesson turns from the heavens to living bodies. Hermes asks Tat to consider the formation of a human being in the womb. He names the many parts and processes that make the body: eyes, nostrils, ears, mouth, nerves, veins, bones, skin, fingers, joints, feet, internal passages, organs, heart, ribs, liver, lungs, and belly. Their varied forms and functions appear as coordinated work performed upon one material.

Hermes asks who carries out this work. Just as people do not suppose that a statue or painting appears without a sculptor or painter, he argues that the intricate human body should not be regarded as workmanship without a worker. The maker is God, unmanifest and acting by will. God is greater than every name and is described as the Father of all.

### God as source and all-encompassing reality

Hermes presses the point further: God's being is expressed as conceiving and making all things. Nothing in the cosmos is outside this divine relation. Things that exist are made manifest; things that do not exist are held within God. God is therefore described as both unmanifest and most manifest, beyond bodily limitation yet present through every body, and as the source of all names while having no single name that contains Him.

The discourse becomes a hymn. Tat asks when and how the Father can be praised, since no one can seize the divine hour or find a place outside God from which to look. Whatever Tat is, does, or says is already within the all-encompassing divine reality. God is named Mind when thinking, Father when making, God when acting, and Good and Maker of all things. The tractate closes with a descending sequence—matter, air, soul, mind, God—that places God at the highest point of the account.
