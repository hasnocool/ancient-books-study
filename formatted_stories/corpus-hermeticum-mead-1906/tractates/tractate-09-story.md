# Tractate IX — On Thought and Sense

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead's English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 9.
>
> **Status:** Provisional story adaptation based on the repository's formatted transcription. The source has not yet been collated against the 1906 printed edition. This is a summary, not a new translation.

## The discourse

Hermes tells Asclepius that, after the Perfect Sermon, he will now explain the discourse on Sense. At first, sense and thought seem different: sense is associated with matter, thought with substance. Yet in human beings they work together. Other living creatures are joined to Nature through sense, while human beings are joined through thought. Hermes also distinguishes mind from thought, and God from divinity, while describing Logos and thought as mutually dependent: thought finds expression through Logos, and Logos is not manifested without thought.

In human life, sense and thought are intertwined. One cannot ordinarily think without sensing or sense without thinking. Even dream-images, which might seem to be thought without sense, are treated by Hermes as involving both activities. Human beings are divided into soul and body; when the two sides of sense agree, thought conceived by mind can be expressed.

Hermes then explains that mind conceives different kinds of thoughts according to the seeds it receives. Seeds from God produce good thoughts. Seeds from daimons can produce destructive impulses and actions: adultery, murder, sacrilege, impiety, and other harms. The cosmos contains daimons, and Hermes describes an evil daimon as approaching the soul illuminated by God's light and sowing its own influence there.

The seeds from God are few but great and beautiful: virtue, self-control, and devotion. Hermes identifies devotion with knowledge of God. Those who know God are filled with good and think godly thoughts. They may be misunderstood, ridiculed, hated, or even killed by the many, because their understanding and conduct differ from ordinary expectations. Hermes says evil has its place on earth, not as the proper character of the cosmos. The devotee who has attained Gnosis can bear what happens and refer all things to that knowledge; in this sense, the devotee makes bad things good.

Hermes returns to the relation between sense and thought. Together they constitute human nature, but not every person benefits from thought in the same way. He distinguishes the material person, whose thoughts are influenced by daimons, from the substantial person, who is joined to the Good and saved by God. God makes all things and directs them toward likeness to Himself, while the Cosmic Course shapes their becoming: some are made foul with bad, others clean with good. The cosmos itself has a simpler, less manifold sense-and-thought than humanity.

The cosmos's single activity is to make all things and take them back into itself, then make them new again. As an instrument of God's will, it receives seeds from God, brings them into manifestation, dissolves them, and renews them. Hermes compares it to a good gardener of life: the cosmos is both the place of life and its creator.

Bodies are made from different elements—earth, water, air, and fire—and vary in complexity. The speed of the cosmic course produces the diversity of births and gives bodies their qualities together with the fullness of life.

Hermes summarizes the order: God is Father of the cosmos, and the cosmos is father of everything within it. The cosmos is God's Son; things within the cosmos come through the cosmos. It is rightly called Order because it arranges births, sustains life, and orders elements and creatures. Individual living beings receive sense-and-thought from outside themselves, from what contains them, while the cosmos receives its own sense-and-thought with its coming into being and keeps it as God's gift.

Finally, Hermes rejects the claim that God is beyond sense-and-thought. All things are in God, come to be through God, and depend on Him. God does not receive these powers from outside; He gives them forth and is all things Himself. His sense-and-thought continually moves all things. Nothing that is can be wholly outside God, nor can God be without anything that is.

Asclepius is told that understanding and belief belong together. Hermes's Logos leads the listener toward truth, while mind can go further and recognize the truth when it has considered the argument. Those who understand find rest in faith; those who do not understand find the teaching incredible. Hermes closes the discourse on thought and sense.

## Section guide

- **Section 1:** Sense, thought, mind, God, divinity, and Logos are distinguished and related.
- **Section 2:** Sense and thought operate together in human beings, including in dreams.
- **Section 3:** Mind receives good seeds from God or destructive seeds from daimons.
- **Section 4:** Virtue, self-control, devotion, and Gnosis distinguish the devotee, who can endure adversity.
- **Section 5:** Material and substantial people are contrasted; the cosmos has its own simpler sense-and-thought.
- **Section 6:** The cosmos manifests, dissolves, and renews life as an instrument of God's will.
- **Section 7:** Elemental bodies differ in composition and complexity; cosmic motion shapes their births.
- **Section 8:** God, cosmos, and the beings within the cosmos are arranged in a hierarchy of source and generation.
- **Section 9:** All things are in God; God gives forth and sustains the powers by which all things move and live.
- **Section 10:** Understanding and belief are connected; Logos leads mind toward truth.

## Editorial cautions

- “Sense,” “thought,” “mind,” “Logos,” “daimon,” and “Gnosis” are technical terms in Mead's translation. They should not be collapsed into modern psychological or scientific categories.
- The tractate's account of good and evil thoughts is presented as its own theological explanation, not as an empirical theory of cognition.
- The phrase “he alone who maketh bad things good” is retained as a claim within the discourse; it should not be expanded into a claim that wrongdoing itself becomes harmless.
- No additional dialogue, historical scene, or missing passage has been invented.
