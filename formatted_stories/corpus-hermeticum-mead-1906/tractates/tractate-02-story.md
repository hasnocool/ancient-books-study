## Story

Hermes begins by asking Asclepius to consider motion. Whatever moves, he says, must move in something and by means of something. The space that contains a moving thing must be greater than the thing itself, and the mover must have greater power than what is moved. Asclepius agrees.

Hermes applies this reasoning to the cosmos. The cosmos is immense, filled with bodies, and itself a moving body. Therefore, the space in which it moves must be greater still. Asclepius accepts that this space must be immense. Hermes then argues that it cannot itself be bodily in the same way as the cosmos. He distinguishes space from God while describing both as beyond ordinary material generation.

The discussion turns to how space and God may be understood. Hermes says that space should be thought of as space, not confused with God. God, by contrast, is the power that can contain all space. He explains that motion depends on stability: what moves something else cannot simply be carried along in the same movement. Asclepius asks how the heavenly spheres move in relation to one another. Hermes answers that their movements are contrary rather than identical, and that this opposition provides a kind of stability.

To make the point visible, Hermes offers the example of a person swimming. The water moves, but the swimmer's hands and feet press against it and create resistance. Through this resistance the swimmer remains stable rather than being carried helplessly along. Hermes concludes that motion is caused through something stationary and that the cosmos is moved by an incorporeal principle, such as soul or spirit, rather than by an external material body.

Asclepius asks about empty space and apparently empty vessels. Hermes rejects the idea that anything which exists is truly void. What people call an empty jar or container is filled with air and spirit. The truly void, he says, belongs only to what is not. Asclepius recognizes that air is bodily and pervades what people call empty spaces.

Hermes identifies the bodiless as Mind and Word: whole, self-containing, free from bodily error, and preserving what exists. God is not simply Mind, Spirit, or Light; God is the cause that makes Mind, Spirit, and Light possible. Hermes says that God should be honoured as the Good and as Father. The Good gives all things and receives nothing, and its nature is one with God's.

The dialogue then turns to fatherhood and generation. Hermes says that God is called Father because God brings all things into being. He applies this principle to human procreation, describing the begetting of children as a great and pious act. The tractate ends with this teaching as an introduction to knowledge of the nature of all things.

## Source notes and adaptation choices

- This is a prose summary of the 17 numbered sections, not a new translation.
- The dialogue sequence and its argument from motion, space, and stability to Mind and the Good are retained.
- “Bodiless,” “Mind,” “Word,” “Good,” and “Father” follow Mead's English terminology.
- The example of the swimmer is retained as the text's own analogy; it is not expanded into a modern physical theory.
- The final remarks on procreation are reported as the tractate's teaching, not endorsed as a universal ethical or medical claim.
