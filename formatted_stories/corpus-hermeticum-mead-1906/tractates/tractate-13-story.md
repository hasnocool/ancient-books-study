# Tractate XIII — The Secret Sermon on the Mountain
## Story adaptation

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), Tractate XIII, as represented in the repository's formatted transcription.
>
> **Status:** Provisional derivative. This is a structured narrative summary, not a translation, and has not been collated against the 1906 facsimile. The source contains explicit lacunae; none is reconstructed here.

Tat asks Hermes to explain the teaching on Rebirth. In earlier conversations Hermes had spoken obscurely about Divinity and said that no one could be saved before being reborn. He had promised to disclose the teaching when Tat became a stranger to the world's illusion. Tat says he has prepared himself by turning his thought away from that illusion, but still does not understand the matter, womb, or seed from which the new human being comes.

Hermes answers that the womb is Wisdom understood in silence and that the seed is the True Good. The sower is God's Will. The one begotten is described as composed of all powers and as coming from God. Hermes warns that this spiritual race cannot simply be taught: God restores its memory when God wills.

Tat presses for a direct explanation. Hermes can describe the change only from experience. When he beholds the Simple Vision born within him through God's mercy, he passes beyond his former condition into a body that cannot die. He is no longer as he was: he is born in Mind. This transformation cannot be taught as an ordinary technique or perceived by the composite bodily faculties. Hermes says his former composed form has been dismembered; he is no longer touched in the old way, though he has touch and dimension, and Tat's bodily sight cannot understand what he now is.

Tat is overwhelmed and says he can no longer see himself. Hermes wishes that Tat might pass through himself as one who is awake within sleep. Asked who brings about Rebirth, Hermes names the Son of God, the One Man, by God's Will. Tat is stunned and says that his senses have been arrested; the printed transcription contains a lacuna at this point. Hermes distinguishes the changing mortal form from what is true: the mortal body waxes and wanes with time.

The true, Hermes explains, is not troubled, defined, coloured, figured, turned, clothed, or contained by a body. It gives light and is comprehensible to itself. Tat finds his understanding blocked. Hermes asks how something that is at once beyond the ordinary properties of fire, earth, water, and air could be grasped by sense. It is neither solid nor moist, cannot be bound or loosed, and is known only through power and energy—and even then only by one capable of perceiving birth in God.

Hermes tells Tat not to despair. Tat must withdraw into himself, will the transformation, and put the body's senses out of action. The brutish torments belonging to matter must be purged. Hermes names twelve: Not-knowing, Grief, Intemperance, Concupiscence, Unrighteousness, Avarice, Error, Envy, Guile, Anger, Rashness, and Malice. They inhabit the prison of the body and trouble the senses. They depart from the person who receives God's mercy, though not all at once. The source has a lacuna in its account of this departure and the articulation of the Logos.

Hermes then calls for silence. He announces that Tat is being purified by divine powers for the articulation of the Logos. As Gnosis comes, Not-knowing is driven out; Joy displaces Sorrow; Self-control drives away Intemperance; Continence opposes Desire; Righteousness expels Unrighteousness; Sharing-with-all counters Avarice; and Truth drives away Error. With Truth comes the Good, joined with Life and Light. Envy departs, and the torments of Darkness flee.

Hermes explains that when the Ten comes and drives out the Twelve, intellectual birth is complete and those who receive it are made gods. The one who gains this birth through divine mercy, abandoning bodily sense, knows himself as belonging to Light and Life and is filled with bliss.

Tat says that he now sees through the Mind's energy rather than through ordinary eyesight. He experiences himself as present in Heaven, earth, water, air, animals, plants, and at every stage of gestation. He asks how the twelve torments are driven out by the ten powers.

Hermes explains through the image of the human dwelling-place and its twelvefold constitution. The twelve types of life are composed of elements and form one nature, though human delusion makes them appear divided. In action they are one; some, such as Rashness and Wrath, cannot be separated or clearly distinguished. The Ten drives out the Twelve and gives birth to souls. Life and Light are unified where the One has being from Spirit; the One contains the Ten, and the Ten the One.

Tat declares that he sees the All and himself in Mind. Hermes identifies this as Rebirth: no longer viewing things from the body's spatial perspective, but seeing in Mind. He adds that he had not explained every aspect of the sermon, lest they be accused of speaking against the All before the multitude, since God does not will the teaching to be disclosed indiscriminately.

Tat asks whether the body made from the powers can ever be dissolved. Hermes rebukes the question as impossible and distinguishes the natural body perceived by sense from the essential birth. The natural body is dissolved and dies; the essential birth cannot be touched by death. Hermes tells Tat that he has been born a god, a son of the One.

Tat asks to hear the hymn Hermes said he heard when he reached the Eight, or Ogdoad, of Powers. Hermes says the Shepherd, the Mind of all mastery, had foretold this stage but left him to learn and perceive further things himself. The powers within Hermes, as in all, now break into song. Hermes instructs Tat to stand beneath the open sky, facing the southern wind at sunset and the eastern wind at sunrise, and to worship in silence before hearing the Secret Hymnody.

In the hymn, the speaker calls on every nature of the world to receive the utterance. Earth is opened, the Abyss unbolted, trees are told to be still, the heavens to open, and the winds to cease. The speaker praises the Lord who founded all, fixed the Earth, suspended Heaven, provided Ocean's waters for inhabited and uninhabited regions, and made Fire shine for gods and humans. The Lord is praised as sublime above the heavens and as the Eye of Mind.

The powers within the speaker are then summoned to hymn the One and All. Gnosis illumines the speaker; Mind brings joy; Self-control, Righteousness, Sharing-all, Truth, the Good, Life, and Light each join the praise. The speaker thanks God as the Energy and Power of all the powers and energies.

The hymn continues by asking that the All be taken back through the Logos as a reasonable offering. Life is asked to preserve what is within, Light to illumine it, and God to inspire it. God's Mind is described as shepherding the Word, and God as Creator and giver of Spirit. The speaker praises God through Fire, Air, Earth, Water, Spirit, and all creatures, and finds rest in God's Will and Aeon.

Tat says he has placed this praise in his own Cosmos. Hermes clarifies that he means the Cosmos seen by Mind alone. Tat agrees that the hymn has illumined his mind and asks to offer praise from his natural mind as well. Hermes cautions him not to do so carelessly. Tat offers reasonable offerings to God, the Parent of his birth, and Hermes instructs him to add “through the Word” (Logos).

At the close, Hermes rejoices that Tat has brought forth the imperishable fruits of Truth. He asks Tat to keep silence about his attainment and not to disclose the manner of Rebirth to others, lest they be accused of speaking against the All. Both teacher and hearer have attended to the lesson; Tat has become, in Mind, a Knower of himself and their common Sire.

## Editorial and structural notes

- The formatted source contains **22 numbered sections**. This adaptation follows that sequence.
- The source explicitly marks lacunae in sections 5, 7, 9, and 13. They remain gaps; no missing wording has been supplied.
- The Secret Hymnody begins in section 16 and continues through section 20.
- The terms Rebirth, Mind, Logos, Gnosis, the Twelve, the Ten, the Eight/Ogdoad, and the One are retained as source concepts rather than replaced with modern equivalents.
- This derivative is based on the repository transcription, not an independently checked facsimile.
