# Tractate XI — Mind unto Hermes

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead's English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 11.
>
> **Status:** Provisional story adaptation based on the repository's formatted transcription. It has not been collated against the 1906 printed edition. This is a structured summary, not a new translation.

## The discourse

Mind asks Hermes to master the teaching and hold its words in memory. Hermes admits that he has heard many conflicting accounts of the All and the Good, and asks Mind to explain them clearly, trusting the teaching that comes from Mind alone.

Mind sets out an ordered sequence: God, Aeon, Cosmos, Time, and Becoming. God makes Aeon; Aeon makes Cosmos; Cosmos gives order to Time; and Time gives rise to Becoming. Each level has its own characteristic: the Good and Beautiful belong to God's essence; Sameness to Aeon; Order to Cosmos; Change to Time; and Life and Death to Becoming. Their energies also differ. Mind and Soul are God's energies; lastingness and deathlessness belong to Aeon; restoration and its opposite to Cosmos; increase and decrease to Time; and quality to Becoming. Each level is contained within the one before it.

God is the source of all; Aeon is their enduring essence; Cosmos is their matter. God's power is Aeon, and Aeon's work is Cosmos, which never simply came into being once and for all but is continually becoming through Aeon. Because Aeon surrounds it, the cosmos is not destroyed, and its contents do not simply perish. Mind explains that God's Wisdom is the Good, the Beautiful, Blessedness, Virtue, and Aeon. Aeon orders the cosmos and gives lastingness to matter.

The discourse then distinguishes heavenly and earthly becoming. In heaven, becoming and time are unchanging and indestructible; on earth, they are subject to change and destruction. Mind describes a chain of soul and containment: God's soul is Aeon; the cosmos's soul is God; the Earth's soul is Heaven. God is in Mind, Mind in Soul, Soul in Matter, and all are through Aeon. The cosmic body is filled with Soul, Soul with Mind, and Mind with God. Life surrounds the cosmos from without and fills it from within, remaining constant in heaven while becoming changes on earth.

Aeon preserves the cosmos, whatever name one gives to the means—Necessity, Foreknowledge, Nature, or another principle. All of it is God's activity. Mind warns Hermes not to compare anything above or below with God, who is without likeness and alone. God cannot be inactive: all things are full of God, and nothing in the cosmos is truly without activity. Everything is continually made, and the maker is present in all that is made, not confined to one place or one product.

Mind invites Hermes to look at the cosmos: its beauty, its living body, its seven ordered worlds, the light filling all, the Moon's role in natural change, and the Earth set at the centre as foundation and nourisher. Hermes is asked to contemplate the multitude of deathless and mortal lives, with the Moon moving between the two. All are full of Soul and move according to their proper courses. Their different motions and bodies nevertheless belong to one order. Such unity requires one maker, wholly One.

Mind argues that a single order cannot be maintained by competing makers. If one maker produced mortal lives and another immortal ones, each would have reason to make the other's kind too. If matter and Soul are one, there must be one authority distributing them. All living bodies, whether immortal, mortal, or irrational, consist of Soul and matter; what does not live is matter alone. Soul is a cause of life, but the ultimate maker of deathless life is God.

Hermes asks why the maker of life does not make all animals immortal. Mind answers that the one God is the maker. Just as there is one cosmos, one Sun, one Moon, and one Godhead, there is not a plurality of rival gods. God makes all things in many ways. Hermes himself sees, speaks, hears, smells, tastes, touches, walks, thinks, and breathes through one living agency; these powers are not divided among separate persons. All activity depends on God.

Mind presses the point: if God did not make all things, God would be imperfect; but God is perfect and never inactive. God's single work is that all things be—those that are, those that have been, and those yet to be. This work is Life, the Beautiful, the Good, and God. Mind compares divine activity with human generation, while stressing that God does not work for pleasure or depend on another. If God separated Himself from what He makes, all things would collapse and Life would cease.

Life is the unifying of Mind and Soul. Death, therefore, is not the destruction of what has been joined, but the dissolution of their union. The cosmos itself undergoes change: portions pass daily into the unmanifest, yet the cosmos is not thereby destroyed. Its revolutions and concealments are forms of conversion and renewal.

Hermes asks what form the maker of the all-formed cosmos could have. Mind replies that God has a unique, bodiless idea, not visible in itself, through which all forms are manifested by means of bodies. A painted mountain may appear to stand out while remaining flat; similarly, what is bodiless can be made manifest through bodies without itself becoming a body. God's life and motion consist in moving all things and making them live.

Mind explains that all things are in God, but not as objects occupy a physical place. Place is bodily and immovable; the bodiless contains in another way. Nothing is more comprehensive, swift, or powerful than the bodiless. Hermes is asked to test this by imagining his soul travelling instantly to distant lands, across the ocean, or into heaven. Thought can range beyond bodily obstacles and, in imagination, even beyond the cosmos.

From this capacity Mind draws a lesson: Hermes must make himself like God in order to know God, because like is known by like. He must transcend bodily limitation and ordinary measures of time, imagine himself as deathless and capable of knowing all forms of life, and gather the perspectives of all places, times, qualities, and quantities. This is a demand for an expansive act of contemplation, not a claim that a human body can physically perform these journeys.

If Hermes confines his soul within his body and declares that he knows nothing and can go nowhere, he separates himself from the path to God. The greatest evil is ignorance of God's Good. The ability and desire to know the Good open a direct path, which can be encountered in every circumstance—awake or asleep, travelling or still, speaking or silent. Everything is an image of the Good.

Hermes asks whether God is unseen. Mind tells him to be silent: nothing is more manifest than God, because all things have been made so that God may be seen through them. Nothing is wholly unseen, even among bodiless things; mind sees itself in thinking, and God is seen in making. Hermes is urged to reflect on the rest in the same way and not be led astray.

## Section guide

- **Section 1:** Mind asks Hermes to retain the teaching; Hermes seeks clarity about the All and the Good.
- **Section 2:** The sequence God, Aeon, Cosmos, Time, and Becoming is defined, with each level's essence and activity.
- **Section 3:** God is the source, Aeon the enduring essence, and Cosmos the matter; Aeon orders and preserves the cosmos.
- **Section 4:** Heavenly and earthly becoming differ; the cosmic body is filled with Soul, Mind, and God.
- **Section 5:** All activity is God's energy; nothing is comparable to the One God, and nothing is truly inactive.
- **Section 6:** God is active in all things and the cosmos is presented as a living, beautiful whole.
- **Section 7:** The seven worlds, Moon, Earth, and the many kinds of life are contemplated within cosmic order.
- **Section 8:** The differing motions of living bodies require a single unifying maker.
- **Section 9:** Rival makers would produce conflict; one maker governs the distribution of life and matter.
- **Section 10:** Living bodies consist of Soul and matter; Hermes asks why all lives are not immortal.
- **Section 11:** God alone is the One maker of Soul and Life.
- **Section 12:** Human faculties act as one coordinated life, and all activity depends on God.
- **Section 13:** God's one work is that all things be; this work is Life, Beauty, Good, and God.
- **Section 14:** Life unites Mind and Soul; death dissolves their union rather than annihilating them.
- **Section 15:** The cosmos changes and renews without being destroyed.
- **Section 16:** God has a unique bodiless idea through which all forms become manifest.
- **Section 17:** God is living activity, moving all things and making them live.
- **Section 18:** All things are in God, but not as objects occupy physical space.
- **Section 19:** The soul's imagined movement demonstrates the reach of mind beyond bodily limits.
- **Section 20:** To know God, Hermes must become like God through expansive contemplation.
- **Section 21:** Ignorance of the Good is the greatest evil; the Good can be sought in every circumstance.
- **Section 22:** God is manifest through all things; mind sees itself in thinking and God in making.

## Editorial cautions

- “God,” “Aeon,” “Cosmos,” “Time,” “Becoming,” “Mind,” and “Soul” are technical terms in Mead's translation. Their roles should not be collapsed into modern scientific categories.
- The sequence of cosmic levels and the account of the seven worlds are presented as the tractate's cosmology, not as astronomy verified by modern observation.
- The claim that death dissolves the union of Mind and Soul is a metaphysical statement in this discourse, not a biological account of death.
- The soul's imagined travel is an analogy for the reach of thought, not a claim of physical travel or empirical astral projection.
- No missing dialogue or unrecorded section has been invented.
