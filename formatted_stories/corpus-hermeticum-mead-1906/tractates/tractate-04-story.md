# Tractate IV — The Cup or Monad
## Story adaptation

**Source:** G. R. S. Mead's English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 4, “The Cup or Monad,” in *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906).

**Status:** Provisional adaptation. The repository transcription has not been collated against the 1906 printed facsimile. This is a summary, not a new translation.

### 1. The unseen Maker and the world
Hermes tells Tat that the Maker formed the universe through Reason (Logos), not by physical hands. The Maker is everywhere, the source of all beings, and unlike any body that can be touched, seen, or measured. Fire, water, air, and breath come from this transcendent source. Being Good, the Maker ordered and adorned the world for Himself.

### 2. Humanity's place
The divine Cosmos sends humanity down to Earth: a being described paradoxically as both deathless and mortal. Humanity is distinguished from other lives by Reason and Mind. People can contemplate the works of God, wonder at them, and seek to know their Author.

### 3. Reason and Mind
Hermes distinguishes Reason, which God has distributed among human beings, from Mind, which is not yet possessed by everyone. Tat asks why Mind was not given equally to all. Hermes answers that Mind was set in the midst of human souls like a prize to be sought.

### 4. The Cup and the Herald
Tat asks where Mind has been placed. Hermes says God filled a mighty Cup with Mind, sent it down, and appointed a Herald to summon human hearts. The proclamation invites those with faith and understanding to undergo the Cup's baptism and seek the One who sent it. Those who understand and immerse themselves in Mind share in Gnosis and become “perfect men.” Those who do not understand have Reason but lack Mind's illumination and remain ignorant of their origin and purpose.

### 5. Two ways of living
Hermes compares people governed by sensation and impulse to irrational creatures. They focus on bodily pleasures and assume that human existence is for the body's sake. Those who receive God's gift of Mind contemplate earthly, heavenly, and even higher realities. They glimpse the Good, recognize their earthly sojourn as a mischance, and turn toward the One and Only.

### 6. The choice between perishing and divine things
Hermes calls this knowledge the Gnosis of Mind and the vision of divine things. Tat asks to receive the baptism. Hermes replies that Tat must first cease identifying himself with his perishable body if he is to love his true Self. A person cannot devote themselves wholly to both perishing and divine things. As one choice wanes, the other can grow.

### 7. Choosing the Better
Choosing the Better is described as a beautiful lot: it makes the chooser godlike and demonstrates piety toward God. Choosing the Worse destroys the “man” and disturbs divine harmony. Hermes likens those ruled by bodily pleasures to processions that pass along a road, taking the way from others without changing its course.

### 8. Responsibility and the path
What comes from God is and will be available to humanity; the responsibility to press onward belongs to people themselves. Hermes says that evil arises when people prefer it to good, not because God causes it. The path toward the One and Only God passes through many bodies, daimones, and the vast system of star-courses. The Good has no boundary or end and no beginning in itself, though Gnosis appears as a beginning to human knowers.

### 9. Turning from appearances
Gnosis is not the beginning of the Good; it is the first beginning of knowing it for human beings. Hermes urges Tat to take hold of this beginning and hasten along the path. The familiar visible world is difficult to leave, while invisible things are hard to believe. Evils are more apparent to the senses, but the Good has no bodily form or figure. It cannot be made visible to a body as though it were another visible object.

### 10. The One as source
Hermes explains the superiority of the Like to the Unlike through the Oneness, which is the Source and Root of all. Nothing exists without this Source, while the Source depends on nothing beyond itself. The One contains and engenders every number, yet is neither contained nor engendered by another.

### 11. The image as guide
Created things are imperfect, divisible, and subject to increase and decrease. What grows does so from the One, but can fail through its own weakness when it can no longer contain the One. Hermes says he has sketched an image of God as far as words can. Tat is urged to contemplate it inwardly: the divine vision can become a guide that draws the attentive soul upward, as a magnet draws iron.

## Adaptation notes

- The dialogue remains between Hermes and Tat; the adaptation does not add scenes or characters.
- “Cup,” “baptism,” “Mind,” “Reason (Logos),” “Gnosis,” “the Good,” and “the One” retain their place in Mead's account.
- The contrast between body and Self is summarized without turning it into a claim that the physical body is intrinsically evil.
- The text's account of star-courses and daimones is reported as its own cosmological language, not as a modern scientific description.
- The source transcription contains apparent OCR or transcription irregularities, including “thoudidst,” an isolated “26” in section 9, and punctuation/backtick anomalies. These have not been silently repaired in the source edition. Consult the source transcription and facsimile before making textual corrections.
