# Tractate XII — About the Common Mind
## Story adaptation

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), Tractate XII.
> **Coverage:** All 23 numbered sections.
> **Status:** Provisional adaptation of the repository's formatted transcription; not a translation, facsimile-collated text, or independently approved document.

Hermes tells Tat that Mind belongs to God's very essence, if “essence of God” can properly be said. Divine Mind is not detached from God but united to the divine as light is to the sun. The Mind present in human beings is therefore a divine gift, and the text describes some human beings as “gods” in a qualified theological sense. It quotes the Good Daimon: “Gods are immortal men, and men are mortal gods.”

Hermes distinguishes the operation of Mind in rational and irrational life. Where there is soul, there is Mind, and where there is life, soul is also present; but irrational creatures are described as having soul without the guiding Mind that works for human good. In human beings, embodied life brings pleasure and pain, which can overwhelm the soul. When Mind governs, it acts like a physician: it challenges harmful habits and may cause discomfort in order to free the soul from destructive pleasure. The greatest illness is godlessness, followed by attraction to evil and indifference to good.

Tat asks how this account can be reconciled with Fate. If a person is fated to commit a wrong, why should that person be punished? Hermes replies that bodily events occur within Fate, but suffering the consequences is also within Fate. He sets this question aside temporarily to explain the different effects of Mind. Human beings may be guided by reason or live without its guidance; all undergo birth, change, and fated events, but the Mind-led person can be freed from vice. Such a person does not escape ordinary change, yet is not morally defined by the wrongdoing that Mind has helped overcome.

Hermes recalls the Good Daimon's teaching that all things are one, especially the bodies perceived by Mind. Life depends on divine Energy, Power, and Aeon. Intelligible things are not separated from one another, and divine Mind, as ruler and God's Soul, can act according to its will. Hermes uses this teaching to answer Tat's question about Fate: God's Mind rules Fate, Law, and all things. It can place a soul above Fate or leave a soul subject to Fate, according to the text's theological argument.

Tat next asks whether Mind becomes passionate when it works alongside the impulses of irrational creatures. Hermes distinguishes the mover from what is moved and discusses incorporeal things, bodies, action, and passibility. The terminology is difficult: the passage says that incorporeal things are moved by Mind, while bodies are acted upon, and warns Tat not to be confused by the terms “action” and “passion.” This is the tractate's philosophical account, not a modern scientific description.

Hermes says that God has given human beings two gifts beyond those of mortal creatures: Mind and speech. Mind enables knowledge of God; speech enables praise. Used properly, these gifts guide a person toward the company of the gods and blessed ones after bodily life. Other creatures have voice, Hermes says, but human speech is distinct. Although languages differ among peoples, he argues that human speech and Reason share a common nature. He gives a chain of relations: Soul is in Body, Mind in Soul, Reason (Logos) in Mind, and Mind in God.

The tractate presents a hierarchy of images and principles: Reason is the image of Mind, Mind the image of God, and body and form are related through form and soul. It describes Matter, Air or vital spirit, Soul, Mind, and God in ascending order, while saying that God surrounds and permeates all. Necessity, Providence, and Nature order the Cosmos and Matter. The text distinguishes the intelligible realm's Sameness from the multiplicity of cosmic bodies, which change while retaining continuity.

Hermes explains number and composition, then describes Matter and the Cosmos as filled with Life. Nothing in the Cosmos is truly dead: what appears to die is dissolved as a compound and becomes renewed. Tat asks whether the lives within the Cosmos die. Hermes corrects the terminology: they are dissolved, not annihilated. Since motion is the activity of life, nothing in the Cosmos is wholly without motion. Even Earth, which appears stable, is in motion through its power to nourish, engender, and bring forth.

All things in the Cosmos move through increase or decrease. The whole Cosmos is not changed in the same way as its parts, and the text denies that its parts are simply destroyed. It distinguishes life from mere generation and death from mere change, associating death instead with forgetfulness. Matter, Life, Spirit, Mind, and Soul are included in the tractate's account of immortal cosmic reality.

Human beings receive a special place because they can receive God and are described as co-essential with Him. God communicates through visions, signs, birds, inward parts, wind, and trees, enabling human beings to seek knowledge of past, present, and future. Other living creatures inhabit particular regions or elements, while human beings make use of earth, water, air, and fire, and can contemplate and sense the heavens.

Tat asks how God may be contemplated. Hermes directs him to the ordered Cosmos: its arrangement, Necessity, Providence, living Matter, and the activity of gods, daimones, and human beings. These are energies, Hermes argues, and their activity ultimately depends on God. Life, Immortality, Energy, Spirit, Necessity, Providence, Nature, Soul, Mind, and Aeon are named as divine aspects or powers; nothing that has become or is becoming is outside God.

Finally, Tat asks whether God is in Matter. Hermes distinguishes Matter from God so that Matter may be understood as having the quality of space, but insists that Matter, Body, and Essence are also energies of God. God is not contained by size, space, quality, form, or time. Rather, God is the All that surrounds and permeates all. Hermes concludes with an ethical instruction: worship of God has one way—“not to be bad.”

## Adaptation notes

- This is a continuous prose retelling of the tractate's argument, not a new translation or historical reportage.
- The claims about divine Mind, Fate, the soul, cosmic motion, and God's relation to Matter are presented as claims within Mead's translation.
- The account of immortality and dissolution is not converted into a modern physical theory.
- Apparent transcription or typographical anomalies remain flagged in the source and are not silently repaired here.
- All 23 numbered sections are represented. The underlying transcription remains uncollated against the 1906 printed facsimile; independent human review is pending.
