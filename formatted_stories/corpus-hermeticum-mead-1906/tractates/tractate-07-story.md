# Tractate VII — The Greatest Ill Among Men Is Ignorance of God
## Story adaptation

**Source:** G. R. S. Mead's 1906 English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 7.
**Status:** Provisional adaptation; the source transcription has not been collated against the 1906 facsimile.

### A call to wakefulness

Hermes addresses those who have become intoxicated by ignorance. He urges them to stop, become sober, and look upward with the true eyes of the heart. The flood of ignorance, he says, spreads across the earth and overwhelms the soul confined within the body, keeping it from reaching the harbors of Salvation.

He does not assume that everyone can respond. His appeal is directed especially to those who are able to turn back and seek another course.

### Finding the harbor and the guide

Hermes tells those who can to resist the flood and use its counter-current to make for the harbor of Salvation. Once there, they should seek someone who can take them by the hand and lead them to the gates of Gnosis.

There, Light shines without darkness. The souls who have arrived are sober and contemplate with the eyes of the heart the One who wills to be seen. This vision is not available to the ear, eye, or tongue; it belongs to mind and heart.

Before reaching it, the seeker must tear away a covering: ignorance, corruption, darkness, and the deadening hold of sensation. Hermes portrays this covering as a hostile presence within the household, one that turns a person's attachments against that person.

### Removing the cloak of ignorance

Hermes explains that this cloak holds the soul down so that it cannot look upward. It prevents the seeker from seeing the Beauty of Truth and the Good within it, and from recognizing the harm hidden in what people mistakenly regard as their senses.

The cloak works by filling the soul with material concerns and disordered desire. As a result, people fail to hear what they should hear and see what they should see. The discourse therefore ends as an urgent call to recover discernment: the seeker must recognize ignorance's deception, turn toward Gnosis, and learn to perceive with the heart.

## Adaptation notes

- This adaptation follows the three numbered sections in the repository transcription.
- The images of intoxication, flood, harbor, guide, light, and cloak are retained as the discourse's own metaphors.
- “Salvation” and “Gnosis” remain source terms; the adaptation does not impose a later doctrinal system upon them.
- The text's contrast between bodily sensation and contemplative knowledge is summarized without treating the body as literally a prison or corpse.
- This is a prose retelling, not a new translation.
