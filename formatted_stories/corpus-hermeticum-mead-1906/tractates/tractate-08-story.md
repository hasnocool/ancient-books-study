# Tractate VIII — Story Adaptation

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead's English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 8, “That No One of Existing Things Doth Perish.”
>
> **Status:** Provisional story adaptation. The formatted source transcription has not yet been collated against the 1906 facsimile. This is a summary, not a new translation.

## The lesson about life and change

Hermes tells his son Tat that they must examine the relation between Soul and Body: how Soul is deathless, and how bodies are composed and dissolved. He challenges the ordinary meaning of death. Nothing in the cosmos, he says, is simply destroyed. The word “death” is treated as a mistaken name for a process that does not amount to the extinction of life. Since the cosmos is a living, deathless whole, its parts—including humanity, the rational animal—belong to that living whole.

Hermes then distinguishes the eternal God from the cosmos. God is the first maker and is eternal in and of Himself. The cosmos is second: brought into being by God, sustained by God, and made deathless through its divine source. It is therefore everlasting by the Father's sustaining power, but it is not eternal in precisely the same way as the Father.

The Father forms a universal body from the matter beneath the cosmos, makes it spherical, and surrounds it with life. Filled with divine ideas, He places living beings within this sphere and orders life into its many kinds. The universal body's deathlessness holds matter together so that it does not fall back into its former disorder. Yet traces of disorder remain among earthly living things, appearing as increase and decrease—the changes people call death.

Hermes contrasts earthly bodies with heavenly bodies. The heavenly bodies preserve the order assigned to them by the Father. On earth, bodies are composed and dissolved; dissolution returns their material to forms that are not themselves subject to death. What is lost in this process is sensation, not the existence of bodies as such.

Finally, Hermes describes humanity as a third life, made after the image of the cosmos and endowed with mind according to the Father's will. Human beings can sense the cosmos as a body and conceive the first God as bodiless and as the Good Mind. When Tat asks whether this human life therefore does not perish, Hermes tells him to understand the distinctions among God, cosmos, deathless life, and dissolving life. The cosmos exists by God and in God; humanity exists by the cosmos and in the cosmos. God is the source, limit, and constitution of all things.

## Section guide

- **Section 1:** Hermes introduces Soul and Body and challenges the assumption that death means destruction.
- **Section 2:** God is the eternal first maker; the cosmos is the second, made and sustained by God.
- **Section 3:** The Father orders matter into a living cosmic body and encloses its many forms of life within it.
- **Section 4:** Heavenly order is preserved; earthly bodily dissolution is a change of composition, not simple annihilation.
- **Section 5:** Humanity's mind can apprehend both the cosmos and the bodiless divine source; Hermes summarizes the relation of God, cosmos, and humanity.

## Editorial cautions

- “Death” is discussed within the tractate's metaphysical account of life, body, and cosmic order. This adaptation does not treat it as a modern biological or scientific explanation.
- The distinction between the Father's eternity and the cosmos's derived immortality is central and should not be erased.
- The source includes explanatory angle-bracket glosses, such as “<i.e., beneath the cosmos>,” as well as bracketed wording. Their editorial status should be checked against the printed page before any transcription changes.
- No missing material or additional dialogue has been supplied.
