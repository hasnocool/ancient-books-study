THE SEKHMET-HEKA HYPOTHESIS
Magic, Medicine & Memory: Shemsu Hor Predynastic Encoding
Zep Tepi Series — Supplementary Hypothesis to Paper III (Shemsu Hor Hypothesis)
© Sefy Levy | DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.21466636 | ORCID: 0009-0002-0309-4420 | 2026
Abstract
“Magic is effective together with medicine. Medicine is effective together with magic.”
— Ebers Papyrus, Eb 3 (c. 1550 BCE); Leitz 2005
The oldest surviving Egyptian medical texts are the most rational. The newest are the most 
magical. This is not what any developmental model of medicine predicts, and it is the organizing 
anomaly this paper addresses. The Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus (c. 1825 BCE) contains 34 
purely empirical cases with no incantations. The Edwin Smith source text (c. 3000–2500 BCE) 
is rational in 47 of 48 surgical cases. The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) contains only 12 
magical entries across 877 rubrics under minimal classification (rising to approximately 10–12% 
under broader Egyptological classification schemes; the inversion pattern holds under all 
schemes). The London Medical Papyrus (c. 1300 BCE) reverses this proportion: 36 of 61 
prescriptions are classified as magical. If the unified Dynastic state invented systematic 
medicine, the newest texts would be the most sophisticated. The corpus runs in the opposite 
direction.
This paper proposes that the inversion is explained by inheritance rather than invention. A pre-
literate pastoral knowledge system — gathered along the Saharan Highland Corridor during the 
terminal African Humid Period — was absorbed by the Dynastic state and progressively 
encoded within the theological framework of Heka. On this hypothesis, the botanical 
pharmacopeia was not lost but preserved within the theological framing; analysis of the 
‘magical’ prescriptions confirms that plant ingredients are present intact throughout the entire 
corpus. What changed, on this reading, was the epistemological framing, not the medicine itself. 
The word Heka held empirical knowledge and divine explanation together without contradiction, 
because for the ancient Egyptians there was no contradiction to hold.
The knowledge trail follows corridor plants with confirmed or probable pre-unification presence 
in Egypt – the entire known Saharan/sub-Saharan component of the early Egyptian botanical 
record. All appear along the corridor; seven occur in the living Mauritanian pharmacopeia (six in 
Yebouk/Ehel Adja; Lawsonia inermis additionally in Thouzery 1997). Commiphora africana 
(myrrh), non-native to Egypt but native to the Sahel corridor, appears in both rational medical 
and magical ritual texts, mythologically encoded as Ra’s tears (Salt Papyrus 825; Derchain 
1964–1965) – a Late Period retrojection of a plant used for over two millennia. A peer-reviewed 
obstetrics study (Haimov-Kochman et al. 2005) independently identifies the knowledge source 
as ‘herders of domesticated animals.’ The ‘magical’ content in later texts represents progressive 
textualization of oral heka protocols that always accompanied pastoral treatment – not 
epistemological decline. All claims are bounded by explicit confidence classifications and 
falsification conditions.
Keywords: ancient Egyptian medicine, Heka, predynastic Egypt, Shemsu Hor, Saharan 
Highland Corridor, archaeobotany, ethnobotany, traditional ecological knowledge, 
Hierakonpolis, chronological inversion, Richat Structure

Series Context
The Zep Tepi Series documents a proposed northward migration by a Saharan pastoralist 
population — the Shemsu Hor — through a seven-node Highland Corridor Refugia during the 
terminal African Humid Period (c. 3800–3100 BCE). Nodes: the Richat Structure (Mauritania), 
Tassili n’Ajjer (Algeria), Takarkori/Tadrart Acacus (Libya), Messak Settafet (Libya), Gilf Kebir 
(Egypt), Nabta Playa (Egypt), and Hierakonpolis (Egypt). Papers I–III establish the geographic 
anchor, cultural grammar, and candidate population. The Sphinx Hypothesis (Levy 2026e) asks 
what this population built. This paper asks whether the botanical distribution of the oldest 
Egyptian medical pharmacopeia is structurally consistent with the corridor network. The paper 
follows the corridor’s geographic sequence, from the proposed western terminus to the Egyptian 
destination.
The chronological alignment between the Shemsu Hor integration window and the Egyptian 
medical record is the organizing connection this paper makes explicit. Paper III (Levy 2026c) 
proposes the Shemsu Hor integration at Hierakonpolis within the 3800–3100 BCE window. The 
pre-papyrus archaeobotanical record documented in this paper places the corridor’s medicinal 
plants in Egypt across exactly that range: castor oil in tombs at 4000 BCE; colocynth seeds at 
Naqada at 3800 BCE; Chemically Attested senna residue in pottery at 3150 BCE (compound-
class identification, McGovern et al. 2009); Moringa from Old Kingdom tomb contexts (c. 2700–
2181 BCE, immediately post-unification). All confirmed and probable pre-unification corridor 
plants tracked in this paper are present in Egypt before writing exists, before the state exists, 
and before any documented internal Egyptian mechanism could account for their presence in a 
systematic pharmacopeia — a pattern consistent with a model in which the pharmacopeia 
preceded the state rather than being invented by it. The Edwin Smith Papyrus source text — the 
oldest surviving Egyptian medical document and the most rational — is conventionally 
linguistically reconstructed to c. 3000–2500 BCE (Breasted 1930), based on hieroglyphic and 
hieratic word-forms more characteristic of earlier periods than the text’s c. 1600 BCE copy date; 
an independent paleographic study of word-spellings across the 12th to 18th Dynasties reached 
a comparable early dating using a separate methodology (Dévaud 1924). However, the 
proposed range for the underlying composition is genuinely debated among Egyptologists: more 
recent analyses place the original anywhere from the end of the Old Kingdom to the early 
Middle Kingdom (Allen 2005; Sanchez and Meltzer 2012). Across this entire debated range, the 
source text substantially predates any documented internal Egyptian mechanism for inventing 
systematic rational medicine from scratch, and this paper does not rest on any single point 
within that range. Independent corroboration that Egypt’s rational medical tradition has genuine 
Old Kingdom depth comes from two sources unconnected to Edwin Smith’s own dating debate. 
Pommerening’s (2014) classification of the broader Egyptian medical ‘teaching text’ genre, 
based on internal lexical and grammatical staging across the full corpus, dates the oldest 
stratum of that genre to the 5th/6th Dynasty, c. 2400 BCE. Separately, a skull excavated at Qau 
and dated to the very end of the Old Kingdom (7th–8th Dynasty, c. 2181–2160 BCE) shows a 
highly probable sign of therapeutic trepanation with a healed margin, consistent with 
sophisticated, survivable cranial surgery — the same domain Edwin Smith describes — being 
practised by this period (Fujita, Lee, Hong and Shin 2025). Before this broader window, no 
systematic Egyptian medical tradition is documented. After it, Egypt possesses the most 
sophisticated rational pharmacopeia in the ancient world, appearing fully formed in its oldest 
texts with no developmental record of invention. This paper proposes that this is consistent with 
knowledge arriving alongside this population rather than being invented from nothing. 
What the Shemsu Hor built at Hierakonpolis, Paper III proposes. What the hypothesis proposes 
they may have carried — the botanical pharmacopeia of seven ecological zones traversed over 
generations — this paper traces. The paper’s original contribution is twofold. First, building on 
the pattern noted by earlier scholarship (Reeves 2008 proposes differential preservation as one 
explanation for it; see Section 11), it proposes a specific mechanism for the chronological 
inversion — the progressive textualization of oral heka protocols — that this paper argues 
accounts for the pattern more fully than differential preservation does. Second, it demonstrates 
that the same corridor plants appear simultaneously across all Egyptian body-knowledge 
domains (medicine, perfumery, soap, hair care, textile cleaning, and embalming), and, applying 
discriminating tests from the cultural-evolution literature (linguistic, ancestry, and use) to this 
multi-domain simultaneity, argues that it is better explained by a unified pastoral knowledge 
system arriving intact than by separate institutional discoveries. This reframes the study of 
Egyptian medicine from a history of discovery to a history of preservation, and proposes that the 
oldest Egyptian medical texts are consistent with being the earliest documented record of an 
older pastoral pharmacopeia rather than the origin of systematic medicine. Given the paper’s 
interdisciplinary scope, readers may find the following navigation useful: the chronological 
inversion and Heka encoding mechanism are documented in Sections 1–2; the pastoral 
knowledge source and corridor network in Sections 4–5; the archaeobotanical and statistical 
evidence in Section 6; the ethnobotanical and ethnomusicological corridor record in Section 7; 
the One Medicine integration in Section 9; and the Equifinality Matrix and Falsification 
Conditions in Sections 11–12. Tables 1, 4, and 5 synthesise the pharmacological, 
archaeobotanical, and domain-mapping evidence respectively.
1. The Organizing Anomaly: The Chronological Inversion of the 
Medical Corpus
The oldest surviving Egyptian medical texts are the most empirically rational. The newest are 
the most theologically encoded. This chronological pattern is the opposite of what any model of 
progressive state-led medical development would predict, and it constitutes the organizing 
anomaly this paper addresses.
The evidence is direct. The Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus (c. 1825 BCE, UC32057, Petrie 
Museum; Griffith 1898) contains 34 cases that are entirely empirical, with no magical formulas 
or ritual instructions. The Edwin Smith Papyrus source text (linguistically reconstructed to c. 
3000–2500 BCE; Breasted 1930) is rational in 47 of 48 surgical cases, describing the brain, 
meninges, cerebrospinal fluid, and heart-pulse relationship in clinical terms. The Ebers Papyrus 
(c. 1550 BCE), the largest surviving medical document with 877 rubrics, contains only 12 
magical entries — approximately 1.4% magical content across the entire text. This count follows 
a minimal classification approach; Egyptologists disagree on what constitutes a ‘magical’ rubric. 
Grapow’s classification (Grundriss der Medizin der Alten Ägypter, 1954–1973) identifies a larger 
number of heka-mediated entries depending on whether invocations accompanying botanical 
prescriptions are classified as magical content. Under broader classification schemes, the 
magical proportion of Ebers rises to approximately 10–12%. However, even at 12%, the 
inversion pattern holds: the London Medical Papyrus at 59% magical substantially post-dates 
Ebers, and the oldest texts (Edwin Smith source c. 3000–2500 BCE, Kahun 1825 BCE) remain 
near-zero magical under all classification schemes. The inversion is a robust pattern across the 
range of plausible classifications, not an artefact of minimal counting. Expressed as a 
Rationality Score (rational prescriptions ÷ total prescriptions × 100), the corpus-level trajectory 
runs from approximately 98–100% in the oldest texts (Edwin Smith source, Kahun) to 
approximately 41% in the London Medical Papyrus — a decline of approximately 57 percentage 
points. This quantified inversion is robust: even under the broadest magical classification 
scheme (Grapow 1954–1973, which raises Ebers to ~88–90%), the trajectory from oldest to 
youngest texts still shows a decline of approximately 47–49 percentage points. The pattern is 
not dependent on any particular classification methodology. A note on a divergent figure in 
adjacent literature: some general medical-history reviews outside Egyptology (e.g., Sallam 
2010, citing Joachim 1890, Ebbell 1937, and Cunha 1949) report that Ebers contains “some 700 
magical formulas.” This figure is not derived from the Egyptological classification apparatus 
used here (von Deines, Grapow and Westendorf 1954–1973) and appears to conflate the 
papyrus’s total prescription count — independently and consistently reported elsewhere as 
approximately 700–877 remedies — with its specifically magical-content subset; none of the 
three sources cited for that figure engages with Grapow’s rubric-level classification. The 12–
105-entry range used in this paper (1.4–12%, depending on classification breadth) is the one 
derived from primary Egyptological sources and is used throughout. A methodological note on 
the use of the rational/magical distinction: this distinction is used here instrumentally — not 
because the ancient Egyptians recognised it, but because it tracks the progressive textualization 
of oral heka protocols across the documented corpus. The distinction is a modern analytical 
heuristic, not an ontological claim about Egyptian epistemology, and the percentage figures 
reported throughout this section should be read as approximate indicators of a real trend rather 
than precise measurements. 
The Heka encoding model accepts, as its starting point, the Grundriss school’s foundational 
insight that Egyptian medicine embedded empirical and divine explanation within a single 
cosmological framework; it uses the modern classificatory pattern as a proxy for the 
completeness of oral heka transcription — a tool that is valid precisely because it is 
anachronistic: earlier texts show the botanical core stripped of oral framing; later texts show the 
framing restored. The distinction measures how much oral content made it into the written 
record at different periods, not what the Egyptians themselves understood themselves to be 
doing. This observation was already noted in 1952: Leake, reviewing the full papyrus corpus, 
wrote of the Ebers Papyrus that ‘it is remarkable that there is not more indication of magic and 
supernaturalism in the text’ (Leake 1952, The Old Egyptian Medical Papyri, University of 
Kansas Press, p. 13) — an independent recognition of the same inversion pattern this paper 
formalises. Independent confirmation of the corpus’s underlying medical validity, distinct from 
the rational/magical framing question, comes from a 2026 pharmacological review of the papyri 
corpus, which found that approximately 28% of described remedies retain therapeutic value by 
modern pharmacological standards (Ghalioungui 1987, The Ebers Papyrus: A New English 
Translation, Commentaries and Glossaries, Academy of Scientific Research and Technology, 
Cairo; cited in Khalpey, Amer and Abraham 2026, ‘Decoding the Past, Informing the Future: 
Artificial Intelligence as a Bridge Between Ancient Egyptian Medical Knowledge and Modern 
Surgical Practice,’ Cureus 18(3), DOI: 10.7759/cureus.104493). This figure measures genuine 
pharmacological efficacy rather than rational classification, and is therefore a separate, 
corroborating line: it confirms that real therapeutic content runs through the corpus 
independently of whether a given prescription happens to be framed in rational or magical 
terms. The inversion pattern documented here is independently consistent with the most recent 
comprehensive treatment of Egyptian medicine: David and Forshaw (2023, Medicine and 
Healing Practices in Ancient Egypt, Liverpool University Press, DOI: 
10.3828/liverpool/9781837644292.001.0001) explicitly state that ancient Egyptians ‘did not 
recognise a distinction between empirical-rational therapy and the use of incantations’ and that 
‘both approaches were valid but had different applications for particular circumstances.’ This is 
precisely the framework the present paper formalises: the Heka encoding model does not 
impose a modern rational/magical dichotomy but instead uses the modern classificatory pattern 
instrumentally to track the progressive textualization of what was, from the Egyptian 
perspective, a single unified therapeutic system. David and Forshaw further note that the Edwin 
Smith Papyrus is ‘the oldest known’ medical text and presents ‘predominately rational therapies’ 
in contrast to other papyri which ‘are mainly lists of recipes or prescriptions, often incorporating 
a magical incantation within the text’ — an independent confirmation of the chronological pattern 
this paper documents. The Grundriss der Medizin der alten Ägypter (von Deines, Grapow, and 
Westendorf 1954–1973) demonstrated that Egyptian medicine was embedded within a 
fundamentally magical-religious worldview, and that separating ‘rational’ from ‘magical’ content 
imposes an anachronistic distinction. The Heka encoding model accepts this as its starting 
point. Where the Grundriss school describes the end-state of integration — the fully encoded 
medical corpus — the Heka encoding model asks how that integration came to be. It proposes 
that the botanical core was inherited from a pastoral knowledge system, and that the theological 
framing was added progressively over centuries as the state absorbed and institutionalised that 
knowledge. The paper therefore does not challenge the Grundriss school’s conclusions; it asks 
a question the Grundriss school did not ask: what was the source of the botanical knowledge 
that the theological framework preserved?
Alternative Explanation: Multiple Coexisting Traditions and the Grundriss School  A significant 
body of Egyptological scholarship argues that Egyptian medicine comprised multiple coexisting 
traditions — temple medicine, magical medicine, and what we might call surgical or rational 
medicine — that produced different types of texts for different purposes and audiences. The 
foundational statement of this view is the Grundriss der Medizin der alten Ägypter (von Deines, 
Grapow, and Westendorf 1954–1973), whose argument against separating ‘rational’ from 
‘magical’ content is set out above; this section tests that premise against a specific rival 
account. More recently, Reeves (2008, Egyptian Medicine, Shire Publications) has argued that 
the apparent chronological inversion may reflect differential preservation: older rational texts 
survive because they were copied as core scribal curriculum in the Houses of Life, while 
magical texts were always produced in parallel but were preserved in temple contexts less likely 
to survive archaeologically.  The Grundriss school’s fundamental insight — that Heka united 
empirical and divine explanation within a single cosmological framework — is the starting point 
for this paper, not a position it argues against. The paper cites Grapow’s classification counts 
directly. What the Heka encoding model adds is a mechanism: the Grundriss school describes 
the end-state of integration; this paper proposes how the integration occurred and why the 
botanical knowledge survived inside it.
The so-called magical prescriptions do not represent the loss of botanical knowledge. Analysis 
of the London Medical Papyrus confirms that ‘spells are used together with the cures. Some of 
the texts outline what should be recited when a particular cure is being applied’ (British 
Museum, London Medical Papyrus EA 10059). The botanical ingredient is preserved intact. The 
incantation specifies what to recite while the plant treatment is administered — not instead of it. 
The pharmacopeia survives inside the theological encoding.
The mechanism pattern within the corpus is instructive. Conditions with observable cause-and-
effect relationships — anorectal diseases, surgical wounds, fractures — retained their rational 
character even in later texts. Chester Beatty VI (c. 1200 BCE), a specialist proctological treatise 
with 41 paragraphs, contains zero magical elements throughout, centuries after the London 
Medical Papyrus had become predominantly magical (Jonckheere 1947). Jonckheere’s editor 
Jean Capart describes Chester Beatty VI in these terms: ‘C’est vraiment de la doctrine, du 
raisonnement, c’est la transmission, à travers les générations, des résultats d’observations 
groupées suivant des principes’ — ‘This is truly doctrine, reasoning, the transmission across 
generations of observations grouped according to principles’ (Jonckheere 1947, p. 9). 
Jonckheere himself classifies the London Medical Papyrus in footnote 6 as belonging to papyri 
whose medical content ‘sont noyés dans des textes relevant de la magie’ — ‘are drowned in 
texts of magic’ (p. 12) — a characterization entirely consistent with the inversion pattern this 
paper documents. Chester Beatty V (BM 10685), by contrast, uses magical formulae including a 
clay crocodile treatment for migraines — a condition whose mechanism was entirely invisible to 
pre-scientific observation (Gardiner 1935, Hieratic Papyri in the British Museum, Third Series). 
Chester Beatty VII (BM 10687) similarly relies on magical formulae against scorpion bites 
(Jonckheere 1947). The pattern is consistent: conditions with observable mechanisms retained 
rational treatment; conditions with invisible mechanisms attracted theological explanation. The 
knowledge was not lost. It was encoded in proportion to how mysterious the mechanism 
appeared.

2. The Explanation: Heka and the Encoding of Empirical Knowledge
Before examining the corridor evidence, the mechanism of the transformation documented in 
Table 1 must be established. Understanding how empirical knowledge becomes theological 
encoding is prerequisite to understanding why the botanical evidence looks the way it does.
The practical consequence is decisive. Jonckheere (1947, p. 39, footnote 2) documented four 
modes of magical infiltration in Egyptian medical texts: (1) magical formula joined as appendix 
to a complete botanical prescription; (2) magical formula inserted within the prescription; (3) 
magical formula preceding the treatment; (4) isolated magical formula with no botanical 
component. The first three modes preserve the botanical pharmacopeia entirely intact — only 
mode 4 represents pure incantation without physical treatment. The London Medical Papyrus 
(BM EA 10059), the most magical text in the corpus, contains 61 prescriptions of which 25 
(41%) are classified as rational and 36 (59%) as magical (Wreszinski 1912; Ghalioungui 1963, 
Magic and Medical Science in Ancient Egypt). Of those 36 magical prescriptions, the majority 
involve botanical ingredients combined with incantation (modes 1–3); only a minority are pure 
incantation with no physical treatment (mode 4). The plants never left. The theological framing 
arrived around them.
The mechanism for why incantations were added is documented in the texts themselves. The 
Ebers Papyrus states explicitly: ‘Magic is effective together with medicine. Medicine is effective 
together with magic.’ The Sais inscription, cited in multiple histories of ancient Egyptian 
medicine including Nunn (1996, Ancient Egyptian Medicine, British Museum Press), records a 
physician stating: ‘I have come from the school of medicine at Heliopolis, and have studied at 
the woman’s school at Sais, where the divine mothers have taught me how to cure diseases.’ 
Medicine and divine teaching are the same activity, described in the same breath.
The integration of botanical knowledge into theology is most clearly illustrated by the Salt 
Papyrus 825 (BM 10051; critical edition: Derchain 1964–1965), a Late Period text (c. 700–300 
BCE). The text records that when Ra wept during creation, his tears fell to earth and became 
myrrh — Commiphora resin, with confirmed antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic 
properties. This is not evidence of the original encoding moment; Commiphora was in Egyptian 
use by at least 1825 BCE (Kahun Papyrus) and in Chemically Attested pre-papyrus 
archaeobotanical contexts from c. 3000 BCE (compound-class identification; species-specific 
identification pending, see Section 6 Statistical Summary). What the Salt Papyrus documents is 
the end-state of a process that had been running for millennia: a plant whose healing worked 
was given a divine explanatory origin, because the biochemical mechanism was invisible to pre-
scientific observation. The theological retrojection confirms that the encoding mechanism 
operated continuously across Egyptian history — not that it was invented at the Late Period. 
The plant healed. The god got the explanatory credit. The encoding mechanism documents the 
pattern; the Late Period text shows it still operating thousands of years after the original 
integration.
The implication for reading the inversion: the oldest papyri — Kahun, Edwin Smith — preserve 
the botanical pharmacopeia before the institutional theological encoding was complete. The 
later papyri preserve the same pharmacopeia with the theological framing fully developed 
around it. The Brooklyn Papyrus, at its most ‘magical,’ still documents the botanical treatments 
used alongside the ritual protocols. The knowledge was never lost. The Heka framework is not 
evidence of decline. It is evidence of institutionalization.
Sekhmet is a lioness. This connects the medical tradition to the iconographic argument of the 
Zep Tepi Series. The Sphinx Hypothesis (Levy 2026e) proposes that the same corridor 
population carried the lion iconographic tradition. If that hypothesis is accepted, the medical and 
iconographic arguments share a common proposed origin population, though each paper 
stands independently on its own evidence. The lioness who heals is iconographically consistent 
with the same lion tradition — a convergence documented as a series-level observation in 
Phase Zero (Levy 2026d). Different domain, the same proposed origin population — each paper 
in this Series stands independently on its own evidence; the convergence is noted across the 
Series, not claimed within this paper.
Sekhmet’s domain is both plague and cure. Bestetti et al. (2024) argue that her priests 
performed the first documented recognition of zoonotic disease transmission, though this 
interpretation is not consensus Egyptology and may equally reflect ritual purity concerns. She is 
associated with Lower Egypt — the corridor’s geographic destination. Her priests were the first 
formally identified physicians. This is a suggestive reading consistent with the corridor 
hypothesis; the symbolic system and the epidemiological reality are distinct: the iconographic 
connection is noted as a series-level observation consistent with the corridor hypothesis; the 
paper does not claim it as primary evidence, and the companion Sphinx Hypothesis (Levy 
2026e) addresses the iconographic argument independently. Heka is the word that names the 
transformation: what was botanical knowledge became religious practice; what was pastoral 
observation became institutional medicine.
Heka — Magic and Medicine as One
The word ḥk (Heka) is simultaneously the Egyptian word for magic, the name of the god of 
ꜣ
magic and medicine, and the name of the primordial creative force, existing before the gods 
themselves and the source of their power (Coffin Texts, Spell 261), through which ritual and 
healing action was believed to work. This triple identity is the most direct evidence that the 
ancient Egyptians never separated what modern scholars have called ‘rational medicine’ from 
‘magical medicine.’ They were the same thing, described by the same word, governed by the 
same god and the same force. As peer-reviewed scholarship states: ‘A priest treating a sick 
person might recite an incantation, apply an herbal remedy, and make an offering to a god, all 
as part of the same treatment. None of these steps would have seemed more rational or 
superstitious than the others’ (Ritner 1993, The Mechanics of Ancient Egyptian Magical 
Practice, SAOC 54). This integration is confirmed independently in peer-reviewed medical 
history: ‘The border between magic and medicine is a modern invention; such distinctions did 
not exist for ancient healers’ (Karenberg A. and Leitz C. 2001, ‘Headache in magical and 
medical papyri of ancient Egypt,’ Cephalalgia 21(9): 911–916). The incantation and the herb 
were not in competition. The incantation explained the mechanism — divine force — while the 
herb enacted it. When this paper’s evidence base shows corridor plants appearing in both the 
oldest empirical papyri and the later ‘magical’ papyri, it is because the botanical pharmacopeia 
was never displaced. What changed was the institutional theological framing applied on top of it. 
The Heka hypothesis — encoded in this paper’s title — names exactly this: the word Heka held 
the empirical and the divine together without contradiction, because for the ancient Egyptians, 
there was no contradiction to hold.

distinct, and the distinction matters for what this paper can and cannot claim. The crook-sceptre 
carried by Egyptian rulers and gods (Gardiner sign S38) represents the word ḥq , ‘ruler,’ the root

documented ideological pairing in royal theology. Ancient Egyptian texts make frequent use of 
paronomasia — deliberate wordplay between phonetically similar terms — and the near-

culture (Naqada II, c. 3500–3200 BCE) and was recovered from tomb U-547 at Abydos, 
documenting the evolution of a functional pastoral implement into elite ceremonial regalia 
across the predynastic integration window. The pastoral origin of the physical object is 
independent of the etymological question: a corridor population that herded with the crook is 
consistent with the artefact’s later adoption as royal regalia, regardless of whether its name 
shares a root with the word for magic. This paper’s argument therefore rests on two separable 
claims — the documented pastoral-to-regalia trajectory of the crook itself, and the well-
established single root of heka as magic, medicine, and divine name (Ritner 1993; Karenberg 
and Leitz 2001; Zucconi 2007, all cited above) — not on a claimed shared etymology between 
the two. Zucconi (2007, ‘Medicine and Religion in Ancient Egypt,’ Religion Compass 1: 26–37, 
DOI: 10.1111/j.1749-8171.2006.00004.x) independently frames heka not as superstition but as 
a technical term for effective force — ‘power’ — the activating principle that makes a remedy 
work, with heka functioning as ‘power’ required to restore maat (balance). Under this reading, 
heka in medicine is not the opposite of empirical knowledge; it is its mechanism-name, applied 
to effects whose biochemical basis was unavailable to pre-scientific observation. The pastoralist 
knew the plant healed. The word heka named why it healed.
The so-called ‘magical’ prescriptions are therefore more precisely understood as oral protocols 
— structured speech acts encoding the remedy, the condition, the application sequence, and 
the attribution of mechanism in a single utterance designed to survive transmission intact across 
pre-literate generations. Egyptological scholarship has recognised that two types of practice 
coexisted in ancient Egypt: an intuitive, improvised knowledge transmitted orally, and a literary 
tradition in which formulae became fixed so that their exactness and antiquity were thought to 
give them power (Pinch, G. 1994, Magic in Ancient Egypt, British Museum Press, London, p. 9). 
The first — oral, improvised, transmitted — is the functional description of how a pastoral 
pharmacopeia survives across pre-literate generations. The second — fixed, exact, ancient — is 
what the scribes of the Houses of Life codified when they absorbed that oral tradition into the 
institutional record. This reading extends the corpus-level inversion documented in Section 2. 
The oldest surviving papyri are the most rational because the earliest scribes transcribed the 
botanical knowledge stripped of its oral framing: the herb, the dose, the condition, without the 
incantation-wrapper that the pastoral knowledge-holder always recited alongside the treatment. 
The later texts, with proportionally more ‘magical’ content, may represent a fuller transcription of 
the original oral protocols, complete with the spoken formula. The knowledge was the same 
throughout. What changed was the completeness of transcription. The pharmacopeia did not 
become more magical across time. The scribal record of it became more complete.
A convergence that falls outside the primary scope of this paper but is documented here for 
series context: the curved shape of the crook-sceptre — a hook on a handle — belongs to the 
same functional shape class as the ceremonial adze used in the Opening of the Mouth 
ceremony, whose form independently matches the Meskhetyu asterism (Ursa Major, the Bull’s 
Foreleg). The peer-reviewed literature confirms that the Egyptian concept of Ursa Major as a 
rejuvenation symbol and a potent force associated with the Opening of the Mouth ceremony 
may date to the beginning of the Predynastic period, c. 4000 BCE (Relke, J. and Ernest, A. 
2002–2003, ‘Ancient Egyptian Astronomy: Ursa Major — Symbol of Rejuvenation,’ 
Archaeoastronomy: Supplement to the Journal for the History of Astronomy Vol. XVII: 64–80). 
The Phase Zero paper of this Series (Levy 2026d) proposes the full chain: the corridor 
pastoralists who watched the hook-shaped circumpolar stars from Nabta Playa (c. 4600–3400 
BCE) named them Meskhetyu — the Bull’s Foreleg — for the most symbolically charged animal 
part in their pastoral world; the ritual adze was subsequently shaped to match those stars; and 
the architectural orientation of the provincial pyramid at el-Kula encodes Meskhetyu at the 
precise latitude where the constellation reaches its circumpolar threshold. The shepherd’s 
crook, the ritual adze, and the hook-shaped constellation are the same shape, held by the same 
population, across three institutional domains: medicine, ceremony, and astronomy. This is a 
convergence of form and population, not of etymology: the crook-word (ḥq ) and the magic-word

Heka and the Unified Botanical Domain
The preceding sections have argued that heka unified what modern scholarship separates into 
medicine, magic, ritual, and religion. That argument carries a methodological consequence for 
the botanical evidence base of this paper. If heka governed all botanical knowledge 
simultaneously — the prescription in the Ebers Papyrus, the incantation in the London Medical 
Papyrus, the incense formula in the temple laboratory, the funerary offering in the predynastic 
tomb — then restricting the corridor plant count to the medical papyri alone imposes exactly the 
categorical distinction this paper argues did not exist. The corridor pastoralists who carried 
botanical knowledge to Egypt did not arrive with separate inventories labelled ‘medicine’ and 
‘ritual’. They brought integrated knowledge of plant properties, encoded across multiple 
application domains within the single framework of heka. Counting only plants that appear in the 
medical papyri undercounts the evidence by the same magnitude as the distinction the paper 
argues is a modern invention.
The clearest demonstration of this principle is kyphi (Egyptian: kapet) — the sacred compound 
incense first attested in the Pyramid Texts, with its earliest surviving recipe in the Ebers Papyrus 
itself (c. 1550 BCE), and its full New Kingdom formula recorded in the Harris Papyrus 
(Ramesses III, c. 1184–1153 BCE) and inscribed on the temple walls of Edfu and Philae. Kyphi 
was simultaneously a ritual offering burned to the gods, a therapeutic preparation taken orally 
for asthma and administered by inhalation for respiratory conditions (Dioscorides, De Materia 
Medica; Plutarch, De Iside et Osiride), and an antimicrobial fumigant whose efficacy has been 
confirmed in independent peer-reviewed laboratory testing (Maravelia, Faviou and Filianos 
2022, Aegyptus et Pannonia VIII; Faviou, Magiorkinis, Maravelia and Filianos 2023, Journal of 
the Hellenic Institute of Egyptology 6). The Ebers Papyrus — the paper’s primary source for the 
rational medical corpus — contains kyphi’s earliest recipe. Temple inscriptions — explicitly 
described by Ritner (1993, SAOC 54) as the material dimension of the same heka practice that 
produced the medical papyri — contain its most complete documentation. There is no 
evidentiary or methodological reason to exclude kyphi ingredients from the corridor botanical 
inventory. They are in the Ebers Papyrus. They are in the heka tradition. They are in the same 
knowledge system.
Broadening the evidentiary base from medical papyri to the full heka botanical domain recovers 
a confirmed corridor plant whose arrival timing requires careful treatment: Cymbopogon 
schoenanthus (L.) Spreng. (camel grass, aromatic rush; syn. Andropogon schoenanthus L.), a 
core ingredient in the kyphi formula. Its native range is documented by the Royal Botanic 
Gardens, Kew (Plants of the World Online: powo.science.kew.org/taxon/397010-1) as extending 
from North Africa (Mauritania) eastward to Egypt, Sudan, and Arabia — the Mauritania-to-Egypt 
arc that defines the corridor. It is not native to Egypt: its distribution is North African and 
Sahelian, placing it in the same non-native corridor category as Commiphora africana. It was 
identified in the kyphi formula as ‘aromatic rush’ (Andropogon schoenanthus) by Manniche 
(1989, An Ancient Egyptian Herbal, pp. 159–162) and confirmed by McGovern et al. (2009, 
PNAS 106(18): 7361–7366, citing Nunn 1996, pp. 211–212, Appendix B). Physical plant 
remains of Cymbopogon schoenanthus have been identified in ancient Egyptian tombs, 
documented by Schweinfurth (1883, ‘The Flora of Ancient Egypt,’ Nature 28: 109–114; 1884, 
‘Further Discoveries in the Flora of Ancient Egypt,’ Nature 29: 312–315) and cited authoritatively 
in Burkill (I.H. 1935, p. 728). The tomb finds documented by Schweinfurth date to the 21st 
Dynasty (c. 1070–945 BCE) at Deir el-Bahari. The Ebers Papyrus kyphi recipe (c. 1550 BCE) 
constitutes the earliest textual attestation of it as a named ingredient. However, kyphi itself — as 
a compound preparation — is named in the Pyramid Texts of the Old Kingdom (5th–6th 
Dynasties, c. 2400–2300 BCE), placing its use before any surviving recipe specifies its 
ingredients (Manniche 1999, Sacred Luxuries, p. 55). 
Since camel grass is a documented core ingredient in every surviving kyphi recipe across all 
periods, and since the plant is non-native to Egypt and corridor-distributed, its presence in Egypt 
by the Old Kingdom is the most parsimonious inference — though no pre-unification physical 
find has been confirmed at current evidence levels. On this basis Cymbopogon is classified as 
an Old Kingdom corridor arrival with transmission inference: non-native to Egypt, corridor-
distributed, attested in Egypt from the Old Kingdom period via the kyphi preparation, with arrival 
most plausibly connected to the corridor population. This places it in a separate sub-category 
alongside Moringa oleifera — distinct from both the seven confirmed pre-unification taxa (which 
have physical or chemical evidence predating 3100 BCE) and the three late post-unification 
trade arrivals (Hibiscus, Acacia senegal, Tamarindus). The statistical proportions remain 7/21 = 
33% (of all 21 identified pre-unification taxa) and 7/15 = 47% (of the 15 taxa non-native to Egypt 
specifically). Both denominators are small, and no null expectation — the proportion of corridor-
distributed taxa expected by chance given Egypt’s ecological position — has been 
independently calculated; these percentages describe the current record, not a statistically 
tested departure from a baseline. Its active compounds include terpenes, flavonoids, and 
essential oils with documented spasmolytic, antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory activity 
(Pavlović, Omar et al. 2017, Archives of Biological Sciences 69(3): 409–415). In the Timbuktu-
Mali region — within the same Sahelian pastoral zone the corridor traverses — it is documented 
in gynaecological treatment, labour support, post-partum care, and digestive conditions: the 
same application domains as the seven confirmed pre-unification corridor plants. In Sudan — 
corridor Node 6-adjacent — it is documented in traditional pharmacological use from Khartoum 
state (Mokhtar et al. 2023, Life 13(7): 1451, DOI: 10.3390/life13071451). 
The Heka encoding model can be formalised as a set of testable propositions.

Proposition 1: A pastoral knowledge system, transmitted orally, encodes botanical 
properties as integrated multi-domain knowledge (medicine, hygiene, fragrance, 
preservation) rather than as separate specialised applications. 

Proposition 2: When this knowledge system is absorbed by an institutional state, the 
botanical content is preserved while the oral framing (incantations, spoken protocols) is 
progressively textualized over centuries. 

Proposition 3: The textual record will therefore show (a) early texts prioritising botanical 
content over framing, and (b) later texts including increasingly complete framing, 
producing the chronological inversion documented in Section 1. 

Proposition 4: The same botanical suite will appear across all institutional domains that 
preserve pastoral body-knowledge (medicine, perfumery, cosmetics, embalming) 
because they derive from a single knowledge tradition, not from separate institutional 
discoveries. 

Proposition 5: Corridor plants absent from any of these domains should be predictable 
from their absence in the pastoral source tradition or from ecological substitution by Nile 
Valley alternatives — not from random omission. These propositions generate the 
predictions in Section 10, all of which are testable against the existing papyrus and 
archaeobotanical record. They also generate a framework that other scholars can apply: 
any pre-literate pastoral knowledge system absorbed by an institutional state should, on 
this model, produce the same inversion pattern in its earliest textual records — a 
comparative prediction that extends the hypothesis beyond Egypt.
3. Methodological Framework
A note on the scope of genetic evidence in this paper. The genetic foundation of the corridor 
model — the chain from Taforalt (Morocco, 15,000 BP) through Takarkori (Libya Fezzan, 7,000 
BP; Salem et al. 2025) to the Nuwayrat Old Kingdom genome (2855–2570 BCE; Morez Jacobs 
et al. 2025) — is argued and defended in Papers I and II of this series (Levy 2026a, 2026b), 
building on the externally peer-reviewed genetic findings of Salem et al. (2025) and Morez 
Jacobs et al. (2025). This paper adopts the corridor model developed in Papers I and II as its 
working framework and does not re-argue the genetic case; the peer-reviewed genetic findings 
themselves are treated as established, but the corridor-transmission interpretation built on them 
remains, as in Papers I and II, a hypothesis rather than a settled conclusion. The genetic 
evidence is relevant here in one specific, bounded way: Salem et al. (2025) establish that the 
Takarkori lineage's closest ancient affinity is to Taforalt (Morocco), and its modern echoes split 
geographically into two branches: a southward branch into Fulani populations across the Sahel 
(D'Atanasio et al. 2023; Fortes-Lima et al. 2025), and a branch that remained in the 
Maghreb/western Sahara, carried most strongly by Saharawi and other Berber-speaking 
populations (Serra-Vidal et al. 2019) — the same broad ethnolinguistic population as the 
Hassaniya-speaking Bidhan/Haratine communities whose pharmacopeia this paper traces 
botanically. No study has directly compared the ancient genomes to modern Saharawi or 
Mauritanian samples, so this link remains circumstantial rather than confirmed (see Section 9). 
This paper makes no independent claim about genetic directionality; that argument belongs to 
Papers I and II.
The argument of this paper is consilient: it draws on independent evidence from Egyptology, 
archaeobotany, ethnobotany, linguistics, genetics, and pharmacology. No single line of 
evidence proves that the Shemsu Hor transmitted the corridor pharmacopeia to Egypt. The 
concept of consilience of inductions, first formalised by Whewell (1840) and revived by Wilson 
(1998), holds that when independent lines of evidence from different domains converge on a 
single explanation, that convergence itself constitutes a stronger inferential warrant than any 
single line could provide. The evidence is presented domain by domain so that readers from 
each discipline can evaluate it within their own methodological standards; the Equifinality Matrix 
(Section 11) maps whether the convergence is better explained by transmission or by the 
alternatives. The botanical and chronological evidence is the paper’s primary load-bearing 
argument. The archaeobotanical, ethnobotanical, and pharmacological lines are independent 
corroborating evidence in the sense that matters here: their documentation does not require the 
corridor model to be true. The genetic and linguistic lines corroborate aspects of that same 
corridor model as established in Papers I–III, rather than testing it fresh; each contributes what it 
can, none is asked to carry more than it can demonstrate, and the convergence across the 
independent lines, supported by the corroborating ones, is the case. A note on why 
convergence constitutes evidence and not merely coincidence. Shared plant distribution does 
not by itself imply knowledge transfer rather than repeated local discovery or parallel ecological 
access. The answer this paper gives is cumulative and structural, not singular. 
First (Egyptology), the relevant plants appear in the oldest surviving Egyptian medical texts 
(Kahun, Edwin Smith) before any state-level mechanism for acquiring them is documented, and 
the chronological inversion of the corpus itself is explained by progressive Heka encoding 
(Section 2) rather than differential preservation. 
Second (archaeobotany), the relevant plants are non-native to Egypt and corridor-distributed, 
directly attested by physical remains at the Takarkori pastoral node (di Lernia et al. 2024) and in 
Egypt itself before unification. 
Third (ethnobotany), the Commiphora africana tracking data — pastoral herders following the 
plant’s seasonal leaf cycle as a grazing guide (Le Houerou 1980) — provides a concrete 
mechanism by which accumulated knowledge would accompany population movement rather 
than remaining stationary, corroborated by the continuity of the same applications in the 
contemporary Mauritanian pharmacopeia (Yebouk et al. 2020, 2026). 
Fourth (linguistics), the adr- root persists as a cognate name across six corridor populations 
(Section 7), evidencing that the source tradition was linguistically continuous rather than 
independently coined at each node, though this evidence does not itself bridge into ancient 
Egyptian. 
Fifth (genetics), the genetic evidence (Salem et al. 2025) documents deep ancestral continuity 
from the corridor nodes into a modern population whose range includes the western terminus, 
via a two-branch pattern detailed in Section 9; this link is circumstantial rather than confirmed. 
Sixth (pharmacology), the applications match across the corridor — same plants, same 
conditions, same application domains — in a pattern more specific than general ecological 
availability would predict, and each application is independently confirmed by modern 
pharmacological review (Section 6). No single one of these lines is decisive. 
Together, across six independent disciplines, they converge on a population-transmission 
explanation that is more parsimonious than the alternatives because it requires no additional 
mechanisms beyond the documented ones: a pastoral population moving through the habitat 
zone of the plants it used, carrying that knowledge to the Nile. One limitation on this 
convergence should be stated plainly: the genetic and linguistic lines do not stand fully 
independent of the corridor model itself, since the population movement they corroborate is 
established in Papers I–III rather than argued fresh here (this paper makes no independent 
claim about genetic directionality; see Section 4). The archaeobotanical, ethnobotanical, and 
pharmacological lines are independent of that prior argument in the sense that matters here — 
they do not require the corridor model to be true to be individually documented — and it is 
primarily their convergence with each other, not with the genetic and linguistic evidence, that 
carries the weight of the consilience claim. The Equifinality Matrix (Section 11) maps each 
alternative explanation’s evidential problems in detail; this paragraph consolidates the argument 
that the matrix develops. Parsimony comparison. The transmission hypothesis requires three 
assumptions: (1) a pastoral population moved through the corridor; (2) they carried botanical 
knowledge with them; (3) that knowledge was absorbed by the Dynastic state. Each is 
independently documented: population movement (Zep Tepi Series Papers I–III; Salem et al. 
2025, Nature), botanical knowledge in the corridor zone (Hammiche and Maiza 2006; di Lernia 
et al. 2024; Thouzery 1997, 2002), and institutional absorption (Bestetti et al. 2024; Gardiner 
1938). 
The alternative hypotheses require additional unverified assumptions: independent Nilotic 
discovery requires that multiple non-native plants were independently identified with the same 
multi-domain applications across Egypt and the corridor without transmission — a series of 
coincidences for which there is no independent evidence; trade network transmission requires 
that the pre-unification medicinal suite was acquired through elite exchange channels despite its 
functional, non-prestige character; preservation bias requires systematic loss of magical content 
from the oldest texts despite the Houses of Life copying tradition. The transmission hypothesis 
requires no additional mechanisms beyond the documented ones. The alternatives each require 
at least one specific, independently unverified mechanism to explain the same evidence. 
Parsimony therefore favours the transmission hypothesis. A related alternative deserves explicit 
treatment: that the corridor pharmacopeia was transmitted through trade or cultural contact 
without any population movement. Two features of the evidence favour population movement 
over trade-only transmission. 
First, the medicinal suite is a multi-domain system — the same plants appear across medicine, 
perfumery, soap-making, hair care, and embalming simultaneously. Trade typically transmits 
individual commodities, not integrated knowledge systems. A pastoral population arriving with 
the full body-knowledge system explains the simultaneous appearance across all domains; 
trade-only transmission requires that each domain was acquired separately, which is less 
parsimonious. Second, the most efficient mechanism for transmitting the integrated knowledge 
of a plant’s multi-domain properties is the movement of the people who hold that knowledge. 
Trade can transmit goods; it cannot transmit the understanding that the same plant is 
simultaneously a soap, a perfume carrier, a molluscicide, and a medicine — unless the traders 
also hold that knowledge, in which case they are functioning as knowledge carriers rather than 
mere traders. The corridor model does not require that trade was absent; it requires that the 
primary transmission mechanism was population movement, with trade playing a secondary role 
in the post-unification period.
The argument in this paper is also a constraint-satisfaction framework over documented 
botanical co-occurrence and ecological continuity. This section states the constraints explicitly 
so that the paper’s epistemics are transparent throughout.
Definitions
Ecological Continuity Edge (E_eco): A documented shared biome, habitat connection, or 
archaeobotanical presence relationship between two nodes. Undirected. Does not imply 
transmission, diffusion, or cultural exchange.
Temporal Ordering Edge (E_seq): A chronological sequence between two nodes. Directed. 
Does not imply causal transmission; direction is temporal only.
Confidence Class: Qualitative evidentiary strength based on current Tier A literature and 
archaeological sampling density. High / Moderate / Low. Low confidence reflects sampling 
limitations, not confirmed absence. Untested: no archaeological, chemical, or genetic study has 
been conducted on this specific claim. Distinct from Low, which reflects thin but existing 
evidence; Untested reflects the absence of evidence-gathering itself, per Axiom 5.
Six Methodological Axioms
Axiom 1: Correlation does not imply transmission. Shared botanical distribution is consistent 
with, but does not establish, knowledge transmission.
Axiom 2: Ecological continuity does not imply diffusion. E_eco does not imply E_seq.
Axiom 3: Temporal ordering does not imply causal transmission. E_seq is chronological 
sequence only.
Axiom 4: No temporal ordering edges for medicinal plants in the pre-3000 BCE period. Only 
E_eco edges are claimed for this period.
Axiom 5: Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. For under-excavated regions — 
particularly Mauritania/Western Sahara — current absence of archaeobotanical evidence 
reflects sampling density, not definitive proof of absence.
Axiom 6: Edge clustering does not imply latent structure.

4. The Knowledge Source and the Western Terminus
This paper proposes that the botanical distribution in the oldest Egyptian medical texts is 
structurally consistent with a model in which a pastoral population with access to the botanical 
resources of seven ecological zones across the corridor had access to the full pharmacopeia 
documented in the corridor network (E_eco). The claim is ecological continuity, not causal 
transmission (Axioms 1–3).
The key independent confirmation: Haimov-Kochman, Sciaky-Tamir and Hurwitz (2005, 
European Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology and Reproductive Biology 123(1): 3–8) 
concluded: ‘They employed their knowledge of plants in their surroundings obtained probably by 
herders of domesticated animals who noticed that when animals grazed on certain plants, they 
failed to reproduce.’ The corridor population matches this profile: pastoral cattle-herders 
documented across major portions of the corridor where many of the relevant plants occur. This 
peer-reviewed source independently identifies the probable knowledge source as the corridor 
population’s documented profile, without reference to the Zep Tepi Series.
Two independent bodies of more recent peer-reviewed literature corroborate this mechanism, 
presented newest first. Wyse, Latif, Gurusinghe, McCormick, Weston and Stephen (2022, 
Animals 12(19): 2709, DOI: 10.3390/ani12192709) confirm that legume forage phytoestrogens 
(isoflavones, coumestans) disrupt reproductive function in grazing cattle and sheep through 
structural mimicry of mammalian estrogen — the biochemical mechanism underlying exactly the 
observation Haimov-Kochman et al. propose herders made. This does not show that ancient 
herders identified the compound class; it shows that the observable effect — animals failing to 
reproduce after grazing specific plants — is real and biochemically grounded, and one a 
pastoral population in daily contact with its herds would have had every opportunity to notice. 
Independently, ethnoveterinary fieldwork confirms that pastoralist populations generally 
maintain field-verified diagnostic knowledge of exactly this kind, including of reproductive 
disorders specifically: among South African cattle-keeping communities, reproductive disorders 
are documented as one of the most frequently treated disease categories in living 
ethnoveterinary practice (Chakale, Asong, Struwig, Mwanza and Aremu 2022, Plants 11(13): 
1784, DOI: 10.3390/plants11131784; Chakale, Mwanza and Aremu 2021, Frontiers in 
Veterinary Science 8: 710884, DOI: 10.3389/fvets.2021.710884); and Fulani pastoralists in 
West Africa — within the same broad Sahelian corridor zone this paper traces — display “high 
levels of ethnoveterinary knowledge and good clinical diagnostic abilities” independently 
validated against parasitological survey data (Majekodunmi, Dongkum, Idehen, Langs and 
Welburn 2018, One Health 5: 46–56, DOI: 10.1016/j.onehlt.2018.03.001). None of these four 
sources references ancient Egypt or this hypothesis; together they show that Haimov-Kochman 
et al.’s single-source inference describes a mechanistically real and ethnographically common 
pattern of pastoralist knowledge, not a speculative aside.
This section establishes the knowledge source and traces the botanical resources available at 
the corridor’s western terminus, before the route continues eastward toward Egypt in Section 5. 
The corridor itself is established in Papers I–III of the Zep Tepi Series; this paper inherits that 
framework and maps the knowledge trail through it. The Richat/Mauritanian zone is presented 
as the proposed western terminus of the botanical network — all facts documented, confidence 
classes stated, and the claim appropriately bounded by the methodological axioms.
The African Humid Period (AHP, c. 15,000–5,000 BP) is the ecological foundation. Geological 
surveys confirm freshwater deposits in the Richat zone dated 15,000–8,000 BP, documenting 
an ecologically connected, habitable landscape during this period. Recent climate modelling has 
now simulated the AHP with magnitude comparable to palaeoclimate observations, confirming 
that these humid periods provided vegetated corridors that allowed dispersal of species 
including early humans across North Africa (Armstrong, Tallavaara, Hopcroft and Valdes 2023, 
‘North African humid periods over the past 800,000 years,’ Nature Communications 14: 5549, 
DOI: 10.1038/s41467-023-41219-4). Crucially, the AHP termination is now documented not as a 
single clean event but as a centuries-long 'climate flickering' pattern: a 620,000-year 
palaeoenvironmental record at decadal resolution documents 20–80 year droughts recurring 
every approximately 160 years across the final millennium of the Green Sahara (Trauth, M.H., 
Asrat, A., Fischer, M.L. et al. 2024, ‘Early warning signals of the termination of the African 
Humid Period(s),’ Nature Communications 15: 3697, DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-47921-1). This 
flickering model strengthens the Highland Corridor Refugia framework: populations were not 
driven out in a single migration but experienced repeated partial retreats to highland refugia 
across multiple generations, accumulating botanical knowledge at each node across the 
flickering window before the final northward exodus toward the Nile. As the AHP collapsed, 
populations concentrated at highland refugia — the nodes of the corridor — and eventually 
migrated toward the Nile. The chronological timeline below documents what is known at each 
stage.
Node 1 — The Western Terminus: Mauritania and the Adrar Plateau (Confidence: 
Moderate Participation; Pre-3000 BCE Origin: Untested)
The doum palm (Hyphaene thebaica) requires less inferential support than any other plant in 
this paper for its ecological continuity claim: it is documented in predynastic Egyptian tombs, 
named in the oldest medical papyri, and its native range runs from Mauritania and Senegal 
across the Sahelian belt directly to Egypt and Sudan — the corridor itself, without inference or 
interpolation. This is an E_eco edge, not an E_seq edge (Section 3, Axioms 1–3): the range fact 
establishes that a corridor-native plant reaches Egypt, but a biogeographic range, however 
clean, does not by itself demonstrate that the plant travelled with a specific population rather 
than by trade, gradual ecological spread, or independent Nile Valley discovery. It is the clearest 
single case in which the ecological-continuity premise requires no inference or interpolation — 
not the clearest demonstration of transmission itself. Yebouk et al. (2026, Journal of 
Ethnobiology and Ethnomedicine 22(5)) document 133 medicinal and aromatic plants in the 
Mauritanian traditional pharmacopeia treating 17 disease categories. Mauritania is described as 
having ‘the most detailed and diverse knowledge on traditional plant remedies of medicinal plant 
use’ in the region. An ethnobotanical study of the Adrar Province and the Ehel Adja Clinic in 
Nouakchott (2025) specifically documented seven plant species in active clinical use: 
Commiphora africana, Combretum glutinosum, Hibiscus sabdariffa, Acacia senegal, 
Tamarindus indica, Senna italica Mill., and Moringa oleifera.
Six of these seven plants documented at the Ehel Adja Clinic also appear in ancient Egyptian 
medicine. The most actively used medicinal plants in the Richat zone’s contemporary 
pharmacopeia are almost entirely shared with the Egyptian medical tradition. All six represent 
E_eco edges. Two of the six — Senna italica and Commiphora africana — have Chemically 
Attested pre-unification evidence in Egypt (compound-class-level identification; species-specific 
confirmation pending); Moringa oleifera is attested from Old Kingdom tomb contexts (early post-
unification, c. 2700–2181 BCE), placing its Egyptian arrival at or immediately after unification 
rather than before it. The remaining three — Hibiscus sabdariffa, Acacia senegal, and 
Tamarindus indica — are corridor-native species that appear in Egyptian medicine but arrive 
after unification, consistent with the corridor model through post-unification trade channels. 
Whether the pre-unification continuity reflects direct corridor transmission or parallel ecological 
access is not established at current evidence levels (Axioms 1, 4); both explanations remain 
consistent with the data.
Traditional Ecological Knowledge and Oral Transmission
The confidence class of ‘Untested’ for the Richat zone applies specifically to pre-3000 BCE 
archaeobotanical evidence, reflecting the absence of any fieldwork attempt rather than weak 
evidence (see Section 3). A complementary evidentiary pathway exists through Traditional 
Ecological Knowledge (TEK). Peer-reviewed ethnobotanical literature establishes that African 
traditional medicine has a rich history ‘dating back to the dawn of humanity... preserved and 
passed down through generations via oral traditions’ (Oketch-Rabah and Katerere 2025, 
Frontiers in Pharmacology, DOI: 10.3389/fphar.2025.1616493). Pastoral knowledge systems 
specifically bear evidence of practices maintained over millennia, including plant-based 
understanding of animal nutrition, reproduction, and health outcomes that accumulate through 
direct observational learning across generations (Sharifian et al. 2022, Ecology and Society 
27(1); Chao, Li and Reyes-García 2025, Ambio, DOI: 10.1007/s13280-025-02131-x). The 
Tuareg population of the Algerian Sahara — corridor Node 2 — has an ethnobotanically 
documented pharmacopeia consistent with TEK maintained through oral transmission across 
millennia (Hammiche and Maiza 2006; Miara et al. 2019: 97 plant species treating 22 ailments 
among Tuareg Saharan pastoralists). The Amazigh/Berber pastoral traditions common to 
Mauritania and the Saharan corridor are characterised in peer-reviewed literature as 
representing ‘ancient practice’ preserving ‘a holistic ecological, social, cultural and institutional 
phenomenon’ (Fernández-Giménez et al. 2021, Mountain Research and Development, citing 
Auclair and Alifriqui 2012 on the Agdal institution). 
Within the TEK framework accepted across ethnobotany, the contemporary Mauritanian 
pharmacopeia documented by Yebouk et al. (2026) and the Ehel Adja Clinic study (2025) is 
consistent with representing orally transmitted knowledge of potentially millennial depth. The 
specific antiquity of that oral chain: Untested (see Prediction 11). The seven plants shared 
between the Mauritanian and Egyptian medical traditions — six documented in contemporary 
clinical surveys, and Lawsonia inermis in the Adrar Province ethnopharmacological record 
(Thouzery 1997) — are therefore consistent with both present-day ecological continuity AND 
ancient oral transmission — two explanations not mutually exclusive and both compatible with 
the corridor model. This raises the effective confidence class for the Mauritanian oral knowledge 
connection from ‘Untested’ (archaeobotanical) to ‘Moderate’ (TEK oral transmission framework). 
The upgrade to ‘Moderate’ reflects methodological convergence across four independent 
disciplines — not confirmed historical continuity, which awaits archaeobotanical confirmation 
(Prediction 11). It means the oral transmission hypothesis is more plausible than the 
archaeobotanical evidence alone would suggest, without establishing 5,000-year continuity as a 
demonstrated fact. The four independent lines are:
1.
Documented intergenerational transmission within specialized families in the Adrar 
region, with 46 species and seven direct correspondences to the paper’s confirmed and 
probable pre-unification corridor plants (Thouzery 1997, 2002); 
2.
An 800-year written and oral chain anchored to Ibn al-Baytar (13th century CE) through 
Hamidoun (Ould Hamidoun, 1952), Thouzery (1997), and Yebouk et al. (2020) — a 
chain now extended to 1937 through Dubié’s colonial documentation of Moorish 
medicine (Dubié 1937) and to 1953 through the first comprehensive scientific 
ethnopharmacological survey of the Mauritanian pharmacopeia (Leriche 1953, 
‘Phytothérapie maure’, Mémoires de l’IFAN 23: 267–306), both independently confirmed 
in the Yebouk series bibliography and in Thouzery and Ould Mohamed Vall (2011) — 
documenting unbroken continuity of specific medicinal uses in the Adrar zone; 
3.
A documented pastoral transmission mechanism — generational tracking of 
Commiphora leaf cycles as grazing guides — providing a concrete accumulation and 
transfer mechanism (Le Houerou 1980);
4.
aDNA evidence of ancient population continuity from Taforalt (Morocco, 15,000 BP) 
through Takarkori (Libya, 7,000 BP; Salem et al. 2025), whose modern echoes split 
geographically: southward into Fulani populations (D'Atanasio et al. 2023), and within 
the Maghreb/western Sahara into Saharawi and other Berber-speaking populations 
(Serra-Vidal et al. 2019) — the same broad population as the Mauritanian 
Bidhan/Haratine communities carrying the Adrar TEK. This establishes the deep 
antiquity of a related Saharan lineage and identifies the geographically relevant modern 
branch, but does not itself establish direct genetic continuity with the specific population 
documented here — the weakest of the four lines below, included for completeness 
rather than as independent confirmation. The specific question — whether oral 
knowledge transmission across 5,000 years is plausible in this cultural context — is 
directly addressed by two independent bodies of peer-reviewed evidence. 
First, geomythological research has demonstrated that oral traditions in pastoral and 
indigenous contexts can maintain factual accuracy across 7,000–12,000 years when supported 
by kin-based transmission structures: Nunn and Reid (2016, ‘Aboriginal Memories of Inundation 
of the Australian Coast Dating from More than 7000 Years Ago,’ Australian Geographer 47(1): 
11–47, DOI: 10.1080/00049182.2015.1077539) verified 21 Aboriginal Australian coastal stories 
against sea-floor bathymetry, dating them to 7,250–13,070 cal yr BP; Hamacher, Nunn et al. 
(2023, ‘The archaeology of orality: Dating Tasmanian Aboriginal oral traditions to the Late 
Pleistocene,’ Journal of Archaeological Science, DOI: 10.1016/j.jas.2023.105819) further 
confirmed Palawa stories at 12,000+ years using two independent verification methods (sea-
floor topography and stellar precession), demonstrating transmission across 470+ successive 
generations. The key enabling mechanism in both cases — kin-based responsibility to transmit 
accurately — is precisely the structure documented in the Adrar region by Thouzery (1997, 
2002): specialized family-based transmission, not casual oral tradition. The methodology of 
treating archaeological questions through oral tradition evidence has itself been formalised in 
the field’s authoritative reference: the Oxford Handbook of Indigenous Oral Traditions and 
Archaeology (Bruchac et al., Oxford Academic, September 2024, DOI: 
10.1093/oxfordhb/9780197557686.001.0001) positions archaeology as increasingly engaging 
with oral traditions as records of history transmitted across generations via practices of memory, 
ecological knowledge, and situated learning. 
Directly relevant to the Amazigh pharmacopeia specifically, Applequist (2025, ‘Persistence of 
Use Among Amazigh People of Medicinal Plants Documented by Ibn al-Baytar,’ Plants 14(3): 
342, DOI: 10.3390/plants14030342) independently validates the Amazigh oral pharmacopeia’s 
stability, finding that 60.9% of Ibn al-Baytar’s Berber-named plants remain in use, with 78.6% 
retaining homologous common names, in a population whose traditional knowledge ‘was largely 
transmitted orally, with little access to medieval literature’ — explicitly ruling out literary 
transmission as the source of continuity. Five-thousand-year oral continuity is documented as 
achievable in analogous traditions with comparable structural transmission mechanisms; this 
establishes that the claim is plausible for the Adrar pastoral tradition, not that it has been 
demonstrated for it specifically. One inferential assumption underlies this upgrade and should 
be stated explicitly: that documented cultural and population continuity implies knowledge 
continuity — that the same families transmitting medicine in the Adrar today carry knowledge 
whose deep structure derives from the same pastoral tradition documented archaeologically. 
This assumption is itself supported by the TEK literature cited above (Nunn and Reid 2016; 
Hamacher et al. 2023; Applequist 2025), which demonstrates that structured kin-based 
transmission systems can maintain factual accuracy across timescales far exceeding the 5,000 
years proposed here. The assumption is nonetheless an assumption, not a demonstrated fact 
for this specific tradition; its validity is precisely what Prediction 11 is designed to test. A 
methodological note on the TEK analogy: the Australian coastal flooding evidence (Nunn and 
Reid 2016; Hamacher et al. 2023) involves geographic knowledge verifiable against fixed 
bathymetric data, whereas botanical knowledge is more fluid and harder to anchor across 
generations without textual records. The relevant parallel is not the type of knowledge being 
transmitted but the transmission mechanism: structured kin-based oral practice with consistent 
daily application context. A pastoral family that transmits plant knowledge through daily herding, 
healing, and food preparation has the same mechanism — structured, repeated, socially 
enforced, application-grounded — as an Aboriginal community transmitting coastal geography 
through ceremonial narrative. It is the fidelity of the mechanism that the TEK literature 
documents, not the equivalence of the content type. Three further precedents strengthen this 
analogical case. 
Second, Pinotti et al. (2025, ‘Picuris Pueblo oral history and genomics reveal continuity in US 
Southwest,’ Nature 642: 125–132, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-025-08791-9) used ancient and 
modern genomes to confirm the Picuris Pueblo’s oral tradition of ancestral connection to the 
Ancestral Puebloan population of Chaco Canyon, a claim the community’s oral history had 
maintained for roughly a millennium. This is methodologically closer to the present argument 
than the coastal-flooding studies: it verifies population continuity and ancestral connection — 
the same category of claim this paper's genetic evidence circumstantially supports for the 
Mauritanian pharmacopeia via the two-branch pattern documented in Section 9 — and does so 
through genetic rather than geological corroboration. 
Third, the Vedic oral tradition demonstrates that entire integrated knowledge systems, not 
isolated facts, can survive high-fidelity oral transmission over comparable timescales: the 
Rigveda has been transmitted with documented phonetic precision since approximately 1500–
1200 BCE through formal mnemonic recitation techniques maintained within specialist families 
— a structure directly analogous to the specialist family-based transmission Thouzery (1997, 
2002) documents in the Adrar region. The botanical knowledge the corridor population carried 
was not abstract — it was enacted daily in wound care, water treatment, reproductive health, 
and animal husbandry. That daily enactment is precisely the application-grounded context that 
the TEK literature identifies as the primary stabilising mechanism for oral transmission fidelity. A 
further objection — that botanical knowledge is susceptible to ecological substitution over time, 
with a functionally equivalent plant replacing the original without the tradition recognising the 
change — does not apply here: the corridor evidence documents Commiphora africana 
specifically at every node from Mauritania through the Sahel to Sudan — the corridor’s 
easternmost node before Egypt itself — under variants of the same adr- name, not a pattern of 
different plants carrying the same name at different points along the route. Substitution would 
predict name-plant dissociation across nodes; the evidence shows the opposite within the 
corridor. The name does not itself continue into ancient Egyptian (Section 7), so this argument 
addresses substitution risk along the corridor, not the separate question of the transmission step 
into Egypt. 
Fourth, structured griot oral transmission provides an archaeologically verified case of the same 
fidelity, in a living hereditary tradition of the same general type as the Mauritanian iggawin. In 
2024, Canós-Donnay’s excavation of Kansala, the legendary capital of the Kaabu kingdom 
(Guinea-Bissau), reported preliminary physical evidence of fortifications, trade infrastructure, 
and a gunpowder house whose destruction is preserved in griot oral history — findings so far 
presented in the popular-scientific and news press rather than in a peer-reviewed report 
(Archaeology, May/June 2024; NPR, 27 April 2025). This does not establish 5,000-year 
continuity for the botanical knowledge proposed here; it establishes that the same structured, 
hereditary, application-grounded oral transmission mechanism this section identifies as the 
stabilising factor — and that Thouzery (1997, 2002) documents for the Adrar pharmacopeia 
specifically — can preserve historically accurate information that archaeology later confirms.
Hibiscus sabdariffa: Corridor-Native Post-Unification Arrival
Hibiscus sabdariffa (karkadeh, roselle) is documented in the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) as a 
remedy for coughs and is known in Egypt as Karkadeh, the ‘Tea of the Pharaohs.’ It was 
domesticated in Sudan — a mid-corridor node — at approximately 4000 BCE (Murdock 1959; 
Fuller and Hildebrand 2013). Its corridor origin is not in doubt; its first confirmed physical 
presence in Egypt is the Ebers Papyrus (1550 BCE), placing it firmly in the post-unification 
period. Egypt’s expanded trade networks after unification provide the most parsimonious arrival 
mechanism. Hibiscus is therefore treated in this paper as a corridor-native post-unification 
arrival: its presence in Egyptian medicine is consistent with the corridor model, but it cannot 
serve as pre-unification archaeobotanical evidence. It is documented in the Mauritanian 
pharmacopeia at the Ehel Adja Clinic and in the Adrar pharmacopeia (Thouzery 1997). 
Contemporary validation: confirmed antihypertensive, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, diuretic, 
and antioxidant properties (Da-Costa-Rocha et al. 2014, ‘Hibiscus sabdariffa L. — A 
Phytochemical and Pharmacological Review,’ Food Chemistry 165: 424–443, DOI: 
10.1016/j.foodchem.2014.05.002; Almajid, F. et al. 2023, ‘Exploring the Health Benefits and 
Therapeutic Potential of Roselle (Hibiscus sabdariffa) in Human Studies: A Comprehensive 
Review,’ Cureus 15(11): e49309, DOI: 10.7759/cureus.49309); currently used in clinical trials for 
hypertension management globally.
Doum Palm (Hyphaene thebaica) — The Corridor in a Distribution Map
The doum palm is documented in ancient Egyptian papyri and tomb paintings and its fruits have 
been found in pharaonic tombs. Its geographic range is the most striking single piece of 
evidence for the Richat connection: ‘H. thebaica grows in the west from Mauritania and Senegal 
and east to Egypt, Kenya, and Tanzania’ (Adewale 2024, Journal of Food Biochemistry). The 
World Agroforestry Centre’s species reference database confirms this distribution in detail, 
listing the doum palm’s native range as extending from Mauritania and Senegal across the 
Sahel to Egypt and Sudan (Orwa et al. 2009) — the Sahelian belt the corridor traverses. The 
doum palm requires less inferential support than any other plant in this paper. Its geographic 
distribution is non-inferential: Nunn (1996, Ancient Egyptian Medicine, Table 8.1, p. 155) lists 
Hyphaene thebaica (Egyptian names: mama, ququ) with Predynastic first recorded use. The 
plant’s native range is consistent with the corridor route. Contemporary validation: confirmed 
antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa; broad-
spectrum pharmacological profile (Adewale 2024, Journal of Food Biochemistry).
Commiphora — The Bridge Plant: Species, Documentation, and Transmission
Myrrh (Commiphora) is documented in the Ebers Papyrus for diarrhea, headaches, gum 
ailments, toothache, and backache, and was heavily used in Egyptian embalming. The critical 
detail: Commiphora africana is classified in the Sahara–Sahel–Nile Botanical Network 
framework as ‘NOT native to Egypt’ but ‘Sahel-wide.’ Egypt had to obtain Commiphora from the 
corridor zone. Its presence in the Ebers Papyrus is consistent with arrival via the corridor 
network. It is documented in the Mauritanian pharmacopeia at the Ehel Adja Clinic. Its chemistry 
has been characterised in detail: a doctoral study of Commiphora africana resin sourced 
specifically from Mauritania documents its chemical and physico-chemical composition 
(Abdellahi 2013); the volatile compounds and biological effects of Commiphora africana incense 
have since been confirmed pharmacologically (Eltigani et al. 2025); and the sesquiterpenoid, 
triterpene, and steroid chemistry of the wider genus is documented in the closely related 
species Commiphora myrrha (Unterholzner et al. 2024).
Species Presence and Habitat History in the Adrar Zone
Commiphora africana is confirmed in the Adrar region of Mauritania — the region containing the 
Richat Structure — through GBIF occurrence records, iNaturalist observations, peer-reviewed 
ethnobotanical surveys, and the earliest formal scientific botanical surveys of the region. The 
naturalist Théodore Monod — who first explored the Richat Structure scientifically on 7 July 
1934 and made it the focus of a dedicated geological excursion during the XIXème Congrès 
Géologique International in October 1952 (Monod 1956, ‘Notes sur l’excursion D10 (Adrar 
mauritanien)’, Bulletin de l’IFAN, tome XVIII, sér. A, n°2: 633–651) — simultaneously published 
the first comprehensive botanical inventory of the Adrar Plateau in the same year, documenting 
230 vascular plant species across the same plateau containing the Richat Structure (Monod 
1952, ‘Notes botaniques sur l’Adrar (Sahara occidental)’, Bulletin de l’IFAN 14A: 405–449; 
continued in Monod 1954, Bulletin de l’IFAN 16A: 1–48). Monod was therefore the founding 
scientific authority on both the Richat’s geology and the Adrar’s botanical inventory. These 
surveys constitute the earliest formal record of the Adrar’s plant diversity — the same region 
where Commiphora africana and Balanites aegyptiaca are confirmed in every subsequent 
ethnobotanical survey, including Yebouk et al. (2020) who surveyed Ouadane directly, building 
on the floristic foundation Monod established. The definitive Mauritanian flora catalogue (Lebrun 
1998) subsequently confirmed both species in the Adrar zone through peer-reviewed synthesis 
of all available herbarium and literature records, with Commiphora africana cited from Monod’s 
1954 collections between Tifrirt and Aouelloul and Balanites aegyptiaca confirmed at Atar and 
the Amojjar Pass on the Adrar Plateau. Monod's own field survey provides an even more 
precise, site-internal confirmation: in a 1974 dispersal-biology study based on 32 vegetation 
transects walked across the Adrar between 23 December 1973 and 16 January 1974, he 
recorded three individual specimens of Balanites aegyptiaca growing directly inside the Richat 
Structure itself, at Graret el-Guelb, alongside Acacia ehrenbergiana and Maerua crassifolia — 
the same Maerua species that survives at the central dome today (Monod, T. 1974, ‘Spectre de 
modes de dissémination dans l’Adrar mauritanien (Sahara occidental)’, Candollea 29: 401–425, 
relevé n° 17). A 1988 French-language biogeographic study of the Tagant massif (which 
borders the Adrar) identifies Commiphora africana as a diagnostic marker for the Sahelian 
vegetation zone and confirms that a Holocene wet period (9,630 ± 150 BP) created 'tamourt' 
(rocky shelters) providing refuges for species with 'more meridional biogeographical distribution' 
(Barry et al. 1988, ‘Note IX: le Tagant’, Ecologia Mediterranea 14: 155–183). A parallel peer-
reviewed phytosociological study of the Adrar Plateau itself — the massif containing the Richat 
Structure — documents the Acacio-Aristidion community as characteristic of the Adrar’s 
southern Saharan and Sahelian border zone, a community whose diagnostic species include 
Commiphora africana and Balanites aegyptiaca (Barry, J.-P., Jaouen, X., Musso, J. and Riser, 
J. 1987, ‘Le problème des divisions bioclimatiques et floristiques au Sahara. Note VI: entre 
Sahel et Sahara: l’Adrar mauritanien. Approches biogéographique et géomorphologique’, 
Ecologia Mediterranea 13(1–2): 131–181). This same study describes the Adrar as ‘une 
passerelle entre toutes les flores’ — a bridge between all floras — independently deploying 
corridor language for the same zone the present paper identifies as its western terminus. 
During the African Humid Period — the corridor's ecological window — the Sahelian vegetation 
zone extended hundreds of kilometres northward. Commiphora africana would have been a 
native component of the Adrar Plateau flora during the pastoral occupation window (6,000–
3,000 BCE), with the water-retaining depressions and wadis of the Richat Structure providing 
suitable habitat. Commiphora africana requires 300–800 mm annual rainfall (Le Houerou 1980, 
'Commiphora africana, browse tree of the Sahel'); the Adrar region receives approximately 11 
mm today, well below this threshold. The plant's current absence from the central depression 
reflects contemporary hyper-aridity, not past distribution. Contemporary field documentation 
confirms this: the Richat Structure's central dome is today almost entirely denuded of vegetation 
— only a small grove of Maerua crassifolia, known in Hassaniya as atil, survives at the eastern 
edge of the central massif. This is not a corridor plant. The corridor species — Commiphora 
africana (âdres), Balanites aegyptiaca (teïchot), Acacia spp. — characteristically inhabit wadi 
beds and alluvial flats, and are confirmed throughout the broader Adrar zone in every botanical 
survey from Monod (1952, 1954) onward. The Richat Structure's external ring depression 
contains the wadi systems of Akerdil and Bamouéré, whose AHP-era alluvial deposits confirm 
seasonal water presence during the pastoral occupation window (Sao, O. et al., ‘Paleo-
environmental study of Quaternary sediments of the Guelb er Richat’, XIXth INQUA Congress 
documentation, AMS dates 7,730–15,000 BP corresponding to the African Humid Period). The 
claim is not that these plants grow at the Richat today, but that they inhabited its active wadi 
systems during the AHP and remain documented in the broader Adrar pastoral territory. The 
physical evidence of Commiphora's presence during the pastoral occupation window: Untested 
(see Prediction 11). (The theological encoding of Commiphora as Ra's tears, and its 
appearance across the rational-to-ritual spectrum of the medical corpus, is discussed in Section 
2.)
The earliest formal botanical documentation of the Adrar’s plant diversity predates any 
ethnopharmacological survey by four decades. Naegèle (1958, ‘Note sur quelques plantes 
récoltés à Chinguetti (Adrar Tmar)’, Bulletin de l’IFAN sér. A, 20(2): 293–305) subsequently 
documented 26 species specifically at Chinguetti — the ancient Adrar city approximately 130 
km southwest of the Richat — with Hassaniya vernacular names, providing the first formal 
record of local nomenclature for plants in this specific pastoral territory. Adam (1962, ‘Inventaire 
des plantes signalées en Mauritanie’, Journal d’Agriculture Tropicale et de Botanique Appliquée 
IX(3–6): 85–200) then compiled the first comprehensive all-Mauritania flora inventory. Together, 
Monod (1952, 1954), Naegèle (1958), and Adam (1962) establish the formal botanical baseline 
for the Adrar zone. The definitive critical catalogue synthesising all historical and modern 
botanical records for the region — Lebrun, J.-P. (1998), Catalogue des plantes vasculaires de la 
Mauritanie et du Sahara occidental, Boissiera 55, Conservatoire et Jardin botaniques de 
Genève, 322 pp. (DOI: 10.5169/seals-895416) — provides peer-reviewed Adrar-zone 
confirmation for all six major corridor plants discussed in this paper. Senna italica is 
documented at Atar and Hamdoun; a colonial-era herbarium specimen (Schmitt in Chevalier 
28517) records it as ‘en abondance dans les oueds de l’Adrar’ — in abundance in the wadis of 
the Adrar. Citrullus colocynthis is confirmed at Atar and in the northern and north-western Adrar 
(Schmitt in Chevalier 28509). Two further corridor plants — Lawsonia inermis (henna) and 
Ricinus communis (castor bean) — are documented specifically at Ouadane by Monod’s 1977 
collections (Lebrun 1998: 6832, 9953), placing these species within approximately 40 km of the 
Richat Structure’s centre. This constitutes the closest documented botanical location to the 
Richat for any corridor plant confirmed in the peer-reviewed literature. 
Lebrun (1998) additionally records that Monod’s 1952 botanical survey included physical 
specimen collection within the Richat Structure itself — specifically from the western cliff of oued 
Bamouéré (cited as oued Bamwéré, Richat), the external ring wadi of the structure — 
documenting Rumex vesicarius, Andrachne telephioides, and Astragalus eremophilus at this 
exact location. These are not corridor plants; they are drought-adapted species characteristic of 
current hyper-arid conditions. Their documentation confirms that systematic botanical survey 
was conducted within the Richat’s own wadi system, and that the same botanist who 
established the Adrar’s corridor-plant record was simultaneously conducting surveys within the 
Richat’s internal drainage. The earliest systematic ethnopharmacological documentation of the 
Adrar pharmacopeia was conducted by Thouzery (1997), whose fieldwork specifically in the 
Adrar region produced a species list documenting the traditional medicinal use of 46 plant 
species among Moorish populations in the area containing the Richat Structure (Thouzery, M. 
1998, Phytothérapie Maure: étude ethnopharmacologique dans l'Adrar, Mémoire 
d'ethnopharmacologie appliquée, Université de Metz). Cross-referencing Thouzery's Adrar 
species list against this paper's confirmed and chemically attested pre-unification corridor plants 
reveals seven named correspondences: Commiphora africana (Hassania name: adress), 
Balanites aegyptiaca (tesot), Citrullus colocynthis, Senna italica, Cassia senna, Ricinus 
communis, and Lawsonia inermis. This is not the same list as the seven confirmed and 
chemically attested pre-unification corridor taxa tallied in Section 6: it names Senna's 
Mauritanian and Egyptian species (Senna italica and Cassia senna) separately rather than as 
the single combined corridor thread used elsewhere in this paper, and it does not include 
Hyphaene thebaica. Six of the seven confirmed and chemically attested pre-unification corridor 
plants tracked elsewhere in this paper as corridor botanical threads — all except the doum palm 
— were independently documented in the Adrar traditional pharmacopeia twenty-two years 
before the Yebouk series began. Tamarindus indica, Acacia senegal, and Hibiscus sabdariffa — 
corridor-native species also present in the Adrar pharmacopeia and in Egyptian medicine — 
arrive in Egypt after unification and are noted separately as post-unification corridor arrivals. 
The Hassania indigenous name for Commiphora africana — adress — signals deep cultural 
familiarity with this plant in the Adrar zone where the Richat sits (see Section 7 for the adr- 
name trail across the corridor).
Ethnopharmacological Documentation: The Yebouk Adrar Survey
The Yebouk et al. (2020) Adrar Province survey, which specifically included the town of 
Ouadane — the settlement nearest to the Richat Structure — identifies Balanites aegyptiaca as 
the most cited medicinal plant in the entire province, with 278 use reports and complete 
informant consensus across three pathological groups. Balanites aegyptiaca is the same plant 
documented archaeobotanically at Takarkori (Node 3) 7,000 years ago. Its emergence as the 
top-priority medicinal species in Ouadane creates a direct documented botanical thread 
between the corridor's most securely-dated archaeological node and the proposed western 
terminus zone. The presence of Balanites aegyptiaca as naturally growing populations in the 
hyper-arid zones of Mauritania — at rainfall levels below 50 mm annually, among the lowest in 
which any tree survives — is independently confirmed by a peer-reviewed phytochemical study 
that sampled 30 trees from five natural populations across Mauritanian arid and hyper-arid 
zones, including sites at the northern distributional limit of the species in Mauritania (Mint 
Abdelaziz, S. et al. 2020, ‘Phytochemicals, Antioxidant Activity and Ethnobotanical Uses of 
Balanites aegyptiaca (L.) Del. Fruits from the Arid Zone of Mauritania’, Plants 9(3): 401, DOI: 
10.3390/plants9030401). The Hassaniya name recorded in that study — Toogga/teïchot — is 
the same term used in the Adrar ethnobotanical record, confirming cultural continuity of the 
species’ identification across the Mauritanian pastoral tradition. Commiphora africana is 
documented as polyvalent across six clinical conditions from three pathological groups in the 
same study, consistent with its status as a medicinal cornerstone across the entire corridor.
The Written and Oral Knowledge Chain
The Yebouk et al. (2020) study notes that six of the 68 Adrar Province medicinal species are 
also cited in Ibn al-Baytar's thirteenth-century CE compendium of simple medicaments — the 
most authoritative Islamic medical encyclopedia of the medieval period. This creates an 800-
year documented knowledge chain for elements of the Adrar pharmacopeia: from Ibn al-Baytar 
(13th century CE) — its knowledge preserved within a living Greco-Arabic written medical 
tradition in Mauritania traced back to Hippocrates, Galen, and Avicenna (Graz and Barras 
2015), and transmitted via the oldest known Arabic medical treatise of the Moorish tradition, the 
‘Al-Umda’ medical poem of Awfa b. Babakkar (composed pre-1850; French translation: Dubié 
1943) — through Dubié’s first colonial French documentation of Moorish medicine (Dubié 1937) 
and Leriche’s systematic pharmacopeia survey (Leriche 1953), through Hamidoun (Ould 
Hamidoun, 1952) and Thouzery (1997), to the Yebouk series (2020–2023). The paper itself 
observes that traditional medicine knowledge 'is particularly important within particular families 
which have owned, practiced and transmitted it for several generations' — confirming 
structured, family-based intergenerational transmission rather than casual oral tradition. The 
IRD publication based on Thouzery's work similarly notes 'une médecine originale transmise de 
génération en génération au sein de familles spécialisées dans cette activité' — an original 
medicine transmitted from generation to generation within families specialised in this activity 
(Thouzery 2002, in IRD Éditions, Des sources du savoir aux médicaments du futur). This 
structure of family-based specialist transmission is independently documented by Graz and 
Barras (2015) in their peer-reviewed study of the Maqari family — one of three recognised 
lineages of traditional Mauritanian practitioners — whose written Arabic medical treatise 
synthesises the pharmacopeia of the western Sahara within a Hippocratic-Galenic framework. 
Graz and Barras describe explicitly ‘une continuité extraordinairement longue, de près de 2500 
ans’ — an extraordinarily long continuity of nearly 2,500 years from the first Hippocratic texts to 
the contemporary Mauritanian clinic. The same article confirms that the parallel Mauritanian 
tradition, the ‘Al-Umda’ medical poem of Awfa b. Babakkar (pre-1850), likewise cites Avicenna, 
Galen, and Hippocrates directly, establishing a documented written chain from ancient 
Mediterranean medicine into the Moorish tradition. This is consistent with the Avicennan 
Canon’s own explicit incorporation of medicine ‘unknown to the Greeks’ (Masic 2012), which on 
this hypothesis included plant knowledge from African pastoral sources transmitted via Arabian 
trade networks. Together, these sources document the Mauritanian pharmacopeia as the 
western terminus of a written and oral knowledge tradition whose earliest documented anchor 
point is the medieval Islamic reception of ancient Greek medicine, and whose contemporary 
expression continues the same family-specialist transmission structure proposed for its 
prehistoric predecessor.
Archaeological Evidence of Pastoral Occupation
The Adrar Plateau's pastoral occupation is archaeologically documented. The Agrour Amogjar 
site, located at 690 metres elevation on the Adrar Plateau within the Richat zone, houses 
Neolithic rock paintings spanning eight stylistic groups including a pastoral period phase. The 
stylistic chronology follows Mauny (1954) and is confirmed by Vernet (2006, in Hic sunt leones: 
mélanges sahariens en l'honneur d'Alfred Muzzolini, Cahiers de l'AARS 10: 199–206), who 
documented the site directly; Mauny's naturalist-to-pastoral period is dated to 5,000–2,000 BCE 
on stylistic grounds, placing the pastoral occupation of the Richat zone well within the proposed 
Shemsu Hor integration window. The Adrar Plateau features stone circles including the 
documented Atar Stone Circle, and the Adrar Region has evidence of cattle pastoralism 
continuing as late as 1,000 BCE (Mauny 1954; Vernet 1993). These are not inferences from 
ecological continuity but documented archaeological occupation of the specific landscape 
containing the Richat Structure during the proposed pastoral window. The under-excavation of 
this zone reflects modern access constraints, not absence of human presence (Axiom 5; 
introduced in Paper I of this Series).
The Adrar Province Survey: Scope and Scale
An ethnobotanical study of the Adrar Province specifically — the administrative zone containing 
the Richat Structure — documents 68 plant species across 27 botanical families used in 
traditional medicine in this zone, treating 50 health conditions grouped in 14 pathological 
categories (Yebouk et al. 2020, Journal of Ethnopharmacology 239:111927, Adrar Province 
fieldwork). This study is geographically more specific than the broader Mauritanian survey 
(Yebouk et al. 2026, covering all of Mauritania). The most-used plant families are Fabaceae (14 
species) and Poaceae, with primary concentrations on gastrointestinal and dermatological 
conditions — the same clinical emphasis documented in the oldest Egyptian medical papyri.
The Transmission Mechanism
The link between pastoral movement and Commiphora knowledge is ethnographically 
documented. Herdsmen of the northern Sahel follow the seasonal leaf cycles of Commiphora 
africana as a grazing guide: 'Herdsmen of the northern Sahel follow the rainfall patterns and the 
C. africana trees as they come into leaf, continually finding new areas for their animals to 
browse' (Le Houerou 1980, 'Commiphora africana, browse tree of the Sahel,' in Browse in 
Africa: the current state of knowledge, Nairobi). This pastoral tracking behaviour provides a 
direct mechanism for the accumulation and transmission of Commiphora's medicinal properties 
along the Sahelian pastoral network. The plant's seasonal leaf cycle gave herders repeated, 
generation-by-generation exposure to its resinous properties — its antimicrobial, antiseptic, and 
wound-healing applications directly observable from animal grazing behaviour and wound 
outcomes. This is the transmission mechanism the corridor model requires. For the Richat 
zone's AHP deposits, tumuli, stone fortresses, and rock art, see Paper I (Levy 2026a); for the 
Salem et al. 2025 genetic link to Takarkori, see Paper II (Levy 2026b).
Acacia senegal: Post-Unification Corridor Arrival
Acacia senegal is native to the Sahel corridor zone and is documented in the Mauritanian 
pharmacopeia and in Egyptian medicine (Ebers Papyrus, Kahun Papyrus contraceptive 
prescriptions; the gum’s phytochemistry and traditional medicinal uses are reviewed by Ashour 
et al. 2022, Molecules 27(4): 1171). Its earliest confirmed physical presence in Egypt is Old 
Kingdom, c. 2650 BCE — post-unification. It cannot serve as pre-unification archaeobotanical 
evidence, but its corridor origin and Egyptian medical attestation are consistent with the broader 
corridor transmission model operating through post-unification trade channels. Acacia gum, 
when fermented, releases lactic acid — a spermicide still used in modern contraceptive 
formulations (Riddle 1992, Harvard University Press; Thomas et al. 2020, Contraception: X 2: 
100031). The lactic acid mechanism documented in Egyptian acacia-gum contraceptive recipes 
is now the active ingredient of Phexxi, an FDA-approved spermicide licensed in the United 
States in May 2020 (FDA approval NDA 208088) — the exact pharmacological pathway the 
Kahun Papyrus exploited circa 1825 BCE.
Henna (Lawsonia inermis)
Henna (Lawsonia inermis) is documented in the Ebers Papyrus for use as an astringent, to stop 
diarrhea, and to close open wounds (Aboelsoud 2010, Journal of Medicinal Plants Research 
4(2): 082–086). It is native to ‘northern and eastern Africa, Asia and northern Australia, in semi-
arid zones and tropical areas’ (Migahid 1978, Flora of Saudi Arabia; distribution documented 
across North Africa and the Sahel), making it a North African corridor-zone native. Also known 
as the ‘Egyptian privet,’ it was cultivated in Egypt from the Middle Kingdom. No specific 
contemporary clinical documentation has been found in the Yebouk or Ehel Adja surveys; 
however, Lawsonia inermis appears in Thouzery’s (1997) Adrar Province ethnopharmacological 
species list among the 46 plant species documented in traditional use in the zone containing the 
Richat Structure. This constitutes historical Mauritanian corridor-zone documentation. Its North 
African distribution further establishes an E_eco edge. Confidence: Moderate.
5. The Corridor Route: From the Sahara to the Nile
This section continues the knowledge-gathering timeline from the western terminus (Section 4) 
through the remaining corridor nodes to the Egyptian endpoint, in chronological sequence. Each 
node is assessed for what botanical knowledge was available, what is documented, and how it 
is consistent with the knowledge accumulation model.
Node 2 — Tassili n’Ajjer (Confidence: High)
Hammiche, V. and Maiza, K. (2006). Journal of Ethnopharmacology 105(3): 358–367. DOI: 
10.1016/j.jep.2005.11.028. Documented species with correspondence in Egyptian medicine: 
Acacia albida Del., Acacia seyal Del. (same genus as Kahun Papyrus contraceptive); Artemisia 
campestris L. (documented for post-partum care; the Ramesseum Papyri focus on post-partum 
recovery — same plant, same application domain); Artemisia judaica L. (documented for 
helminthiases); Balanites aegyptiaca Del. (documented for digestive ailments). The Tassili rock 
art spans approximately 12,000 years (Muzzolini 2001); the Round Heads period (c. 9,000–
7,000 BCE) includes anthropomorphic figures widely interpreted as ritual or religious in 
character — dancing and apparent worship postures (Soukopova 2011, 2012).
Reverse-Pharmacology Precedent: Artemisia
The 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded jointly to Tu Youyou ‘for her 
discoveries concerning a novel therapy against Malaria’ derived from Artemisia annua, and to 
William C. Campbell and Satoshi Ōmura for ivermectin (Nobel Prize Committee, 5 October 
2015). This case is a direct illustration of reverse pharmacology in practice. Tu Youyou’s 
discovery did not come from a laboratory screen of synthesized compounds — it came from 
systematic consultation of ancient Chinese medical texts, specifically the 4th-century 
compendium of Ge Hong, where the antifebrile properties of sweet wormwood had been 
recorded 1,700 years earlier (Tu, Nobel Lecture, Karolinska Institute, 7 December 2015: 
‘Discovery of Artemisinin: A Gift from Traditional Chinese Medicine to the World’). The Nobel 
Committee’s press release states explicitly: “Tu revisited the ancient literature and discovered 
clues that guided her in her quest to successfully extract the active component from Artemisia 
annua” (Nobel Prize Committee 2015). This is the precise model this paper proposes for the 
Egyptian medical corpus: that ancient botanical knowledge, encoded in texts compiled from 
earlier oral sources, contains genuine pharmacological intelligence recoverable by modern 
analysis. The antimalarial activity of artemisinin from Artemisia annua was first published in 
English by the Qinghaosu Antimalaria Coordinating Research Group (1979, ‘Antimalarial studies 
on qinghaosu,’ Chinese Medical Journal 92: 811–816) and subsequently reviewed by Klayman 
(1985, ‘Qinghaosu (Artemisinin): An Antimalarial Drug from China,’ Science 228(4703): 1049–
1055). Artemisinin-based combination therapies are now standard treatment worldwide for P. 
falciparum malaria. The Artemisia genus documented at Tassili for post-partum care and 
antiparasitic use is the same genus whose antiparasitic activity is Nobel Prize-confirmed. The 
Egyptian Ebers Papyrus documents Artemisia in antiparasitic and gynaecological applications 
(Manniche 1989). The corridor botanical knowledge and the Egyptian corpus share not just the 
plant but the application domain — antiparasitic and reproductive health — in a pattern 
consistent with a single knowledge tradition encoding genuine biological activity that modern 
pharmacology has independently confirmed. Both Nobel Prize cases in this paper illustrate the 
same methodological point: ancient botanical pharmacopoeias — whether Egyptian or Chinese 
— can encode pharmacologically active compounds that withstand modern scientific scrutiny. 
This is a precedent for the method of reverse pharmacology, not a validation of this paper’s 
corridor-transmission hypothesis specifically. The 2015 Prize is best read as validating reverse 
pharmacology as a method — taking a specific ancient textual detail seriously as a lead for 
modern investigation, rather than as decorative color. That precedent supports treating the 
Egyptian medical corpus as a plausible repository of genuine pharmacological knowledge. It 
does not, by itself, establish where that knowledge came from; the corridor-transmission 
argument rests on the archaeobotanical, genetic, and linguistic evidence presented elsewhere 
in this paper, not on the Nobel precedent.
Contemporary Validation: Balanites aegyptiaca
Balanites aegyptiaca is the Adrar Province’s most-cited medicinal plant (Yebouk et al. 2020; see 
Section 4 for the full use-report figures). Its comprehensive pharmacological profile — 
antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antiplasmodial, and wound-healing — is 
documented in the peer-reviewed literature (Thakkar et al. 2025; Sharma and Sharma 2026; 
Tsila et al. 2025). The same Takarkori link already established (Section 4; di Lernia et al. 2024) 
applies here too. Its multi-domain applications in Egyptian medicine, perfumery, soap-making, 
and pest control are discussed in Section 9.
Node 3 — Takarkori, Tadrart Acacus (Confidence: High — Archaeobotanical)
di Lernia et al. (2024). DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0310739. Balanites aegyptiaca drupes are 
‘among the most common archaeobotanical remains’ at the Holocene pastoral site. This is an 
E_arch edge: the plant is physically in the preserved botanical record of the corridor site, not 
merely documented in ethnobotanical surveys. The Tuareg Kel Tadrart still use the same plant 
medicinally — continuous from the Holocene pastoral period to the present. Three independent 
documentation points on a single plant: Tassili pharmacopeia (Node 2), Takarkori 
archaeobotanical record (Node 3), Egyptian traditional medicine (destination).
Nodes 4–6 — Messak Settafet, Gilf Kebir, and Nabta Playa
These nodes do not currently yield specific archaeobotanical medical evidence (E_arch absent; 
E_eco only). Messak Settafet (Libya, Node 4): therianthrope rock art tradition consistent with 
shamanic healing iconography globally. Gilf Kebir/Wadi Sura (Egypt, Node 5): Cave of Beasts 
hybrid figures; no specific medical plant evidence published. Rock art documentation at Jebel 
Uweinat and Gilf Kebir confirms that all preceding hunter-gatherer cultures in this zone were 
replaced by a homogenous culture of cattle pastoralists (Zboray et al. 2025, in Förster et al. 
eds., Current Research in the Rock Art of the Eastern Sahara, IBAES 27, GHP London), 
corroborating the pastoral population profile at this corridor node. Nabta Playa (Egypt, Node 6): 
cattle-herding community with sub-Saharan African osteological origins, consistent with the 
herder profile Haimov-Kochman et al. (2005) identify as the probable knowledge source; no 
specific medical plant evidence published. Rock art from the First Cataract area — the corridor 
gateway between the eastern nodes and the Egyptian destination — provides independent 
evidence of cultural contact during the fourth millennium BCE integration window (Brémont and 
Vanhulle 2025, in Förster et al. eds., Current Research in the Rock Art of the Eastern Sahara, 
IBAES 27, GHP London), consistent with the pastoral population’s documented approach to 
Hierakonpolis across this period.
Node 7 — Hierakonpolis: The Egyptian Integration Point (Confidence: High)
Predynastic Fracture Healing
The predynastic workers' cemetery HK43 (Naqada I-IIC, c. 3750–3550 BCE) documents healed 
fractures in a well-nourished, robustly built population (Dougherty and Friedman 2008). The Old 
Kingdom Giza fracture study shows 93.3% successful fracture alignment in 2,287 long bones 
from 204 skeletons (Zaki 2013) — a tradition that requires an established practice predating the 
pyramid era.
The same integration of medical care extended to animals at Hierakonpolis before writing 
existed. The HK6 elite cemetery (c. 3800–3100 BCE) documents healed fractures in 20 of 38 
wild mammals, including baboons, a leopard, a jungle cat, a hartebeest, an aurochs, and a 
hippopotamus; a cat shows healed fractures indicating at least 4–6 weeks of sustained captive 
care (Van Neer et al. 2015; Van Neer et al. 2014). This is the archaeozoological signature of 
what Schwabe (1978) called a 'fused' society; the full One Medicine framework and its corridor 
transmission significance are developed in Section 9.
Reverse-Pharmacology Precedent: Antibiotic Grain Storage
Mills, J.O. (1992). ‘Beyond Nutrition: Antibiotics Produced through Grain Storage Practices, their 
Recognition and Implications for the Egyptian Predynastic.’ In Friedman and Adams (eds), The 
Followers of Horus. Oxbow Monograph No. 20. Oxbow Books, Oxford, pp. 27–35. The 
predynastic population at Hierakonpolis had access to conditions producing Penicillium-family 
antibiotic properties through grain storage. Published in a volume titled after the Shemsu Hor. 
The 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (Fleming, Florey, Chain) confirmed penicillin’s 
mechanism: Penicillium-family moulds on organic substrates kill bacterial pathogens. Primary 
sources: Fleming (1929, ‘On the antibacterial action of cultures of a Penicillium,’ British Journal 
of Experimental Pathology 10(3): 226–236); Chain, Florey et al. (1940, ‘Penicillin as a 
chemotherapeutic agent,’ Lancet 236(6104): 226–228). The mechanism is pharmacologically 
comparable. The Edwin Smith and Ebers papyri document the application of mouldy bread to 
infected wounds as a standard treatment.
Shamanic Precursors
Bestetti et al. (2024, Cureus, DOI: 10.7759/cureus.67195): predynastic shamans managed the 
healing process in predynastic Egypt. The Hierakonpolis population during the Naqada period 
would have included shamanic healers with access to the corridor’s botanical resources. A 2025 
chronological and stylistic analysis of the Hierakonpolis petroglyphs (Hardtke and Friedman 
2025, in Förster et al. eds., Current Research in the Rock Art of the Eastern Sahara, IBAES 27, 
GHP London, pp. 171–190) provides updated attribution and dating for the rock art record at 
this integration node, consistent with the Naqada-period occupation documented in the skeletal 
and archaeobotanical evidence above.
Contemporary Validation: Honey
Honey’s antibacterial properties are confirmed through hydrogen peroxide production, osmotic 
effect, low pH, and bee defensin-1 antibacterial peptides. Medical-grade honey is licensed as a 
medical product for professional wound care in Europe and Australia, with a 2025 
comprehensive clinical review confirming efficacy across acute wounds, burns, and antibiotic-
resistant infections (Barazesh et al. 2025, Health Science Reports, Wiley, DOI: 
10.1002/hsr2.70240). The Edwin Smith and Ebers papyri document honey as a wound 
antiseptic: the application was consistent with confirmed pharmacological activity.
The Institutional Endpoint: Sais and the Temple of Neith
At the Sais temple complex in the western Nile Delta --- where the cult of Neith is attested from 
Naqada II (c. 3600--3350 BCE, predating the state by several centuries) --- a women's medical 
teaching institution is attested in later inscriptions. The teaching tradition is Middle to New 
Kingdom in its textual attestation, but the institutional roots in the cult of Neith — a goddess 
associated with war, weaving, and creation — predate unification. The Sais inscription quoted in 
Section 2 (Nunn 1996; Strouhal, Vachala and Vymazalová 2014) documents an institution; the 
antiquity of the Neith cult documents that Sais was a centre of female religious authority before 
writing existed. Whether the medical school itself dates to the predynastic period cannot be 
confirmed from current evidence — the teaching tradition is attested later, though the cult 
context is early.
The worship of Neith at Sais is confirmed from the Naqada II period (c. 3600–3350 BCE) — the 
corridor period, several centuries before unification (Wilkinson 2008; Hendrickx 1996). The 
institution’s focus was gynaecology and obstetrics: the medical domain most directly 
documented in the corridor pharmacopeia (Acacia contraceptive, Artemisia post-partum, 
colocynth labour induction). Sais is in the western Nile Delta, Lower Egypt — the geographic 
destination of the Shemsu Hor migration. Peseshet (c. 2500 BCE, 4th Dynasty), ‘Lady Overseer 
of Female Physicians,’ is the earliest confirmed female physician in documented history; her title 
is attested from the tomb of her son Akhethetep at Giza (Harer and el-Dawakhly 1989, 
‘Peseshet — the first female physician?’, Obstetrics and Gynecology 74(6): 960–961). No direct 
epigraphic link between Peseshet and the Sais institution has been established; the connection 
is inferred from the parallel between her title and the Sais women’s medical tradition, and is 
presented here as contextual, not confirmed.
From Shamans to Priests of Sekhmet
The Edwin Smith source text (c. 3000–2500 BCE) already mentions the parallel roles of the 
physician, the Priest of Sekhmet, and magicians — Sekhmet's physicians are present at the 
oldest surviving moment of the rational medical tradition. The full institutional timeline from 
predynastic shamans to Ni-Ankh-Sekhmet, and the significance of the Sekhmet priesthood's 
animal inspection role, are examined in Section 9 (Bestetti et al. 2024).
6. Reverse Tracking: From Egyptian Pharmacopeia to Corridor Source
This section tracks in the reverse direction: starting from what is documented in the Egyptian 
medical record and tracing each plant backward through the corridor nodes. The methodology is 
identical: E_eco documentation at each node, not E_seq causal claims. The purpose is to test 
whether the reverse direction yields independent corroboration and whether the botanical trail is 
structurally consistent with an origin in the Richat/Mauritanian zone.
Classification Criterion. A taxon is classified as a corridor-distributed plant if its native range or 
earliest documented domestication centre lies primarily within the Saharan–Sahelian ecological 
belt extending west–east across North Africa and connecting the seven corridor nodes 
examined in this paper (Richat Structure through Hierakonpolis), and if that range is distinct 
from Egypt’s indigenous Nilotic, Levantine, or Mediterranean ecological zones. This criterion 
was defined before classifying any taxon; the classification was not derived from the result. 
Each taxon is also evaluated by identification confidence: Confirmed (physical macroremains or 
secure chemical identification at a named site with a peer-reviewed publication); Probable 
(strong circumstantial evidence but no secure physical identification); Uncertain (candidate 
identification in primary literature explicitly noted as unlikely or unresolved). Only Confirmed taxa 
are included in the statistical proportions below.

Four taxa — Balanites aegyptiaca, Senna (Cassia), Commiphora africana, and Moringa oleifera 
— involve contested identifications or documented scholarly disagreement that cannot be 
reduced to a single table cell without losing the argument; they are discussed in full below rather 
than in the table above.
Balanites aegyptiaca. Balanites aegyptiaca is attested in Egypt by c. 3600 BCE, in the HK43 
Burial 333 basket (Fahmy 2005, Veg. Hist. Archaeobot. 14). Burial 333 is one of the richest 
graves in the non-elite HK43 cemetery, belonging to a woman aged 40–50; Friedman (2003) 
proposed on the basis of the full burial assemblage that she was a magico-medical practitioner, 
since the basket contained Balanites fruits, stone amulets with carved faces, an incense mixture 
of partially burned nut grass tubers and coniferous wood fragments, bone awls, and various 
botanical remains recognised for their medicinal and antiseptic applications. The oldest 
confirmed pre-unification physical evidence of Balanites aegyptiaca in the Egyptian medical 
record therefore belongs to a proposed healer’s burial. Balanites aegyptiaca fruits are also 
documented at Uan Muhuggiag, Tadrart Acacus, Libya (Node 3), dated 4450–3942 cal. BCE, 
co-occurring with Citrullus colocynthis seeds in the same pastoral assemblage (Wasylikowa and 
van der Veen 2004, Vegetation History and Archaeobotany 13: 213–217) — corridor Node 3 
presence predates the Egyptian evidence by 400–600 years. Native range: Sahel-wide, from 
Mauritania to Egypt. Status: Confirmed Corridor.
Senna (genus Senna/Cassia). The Egyptian medical record documents primarily Cassia senna 
(Alexandrian senna, syn. Senna alexandrina), native to Upper Egypt and Nubia, while the 
Mauritanian pharmacopeia documents Senna italica (Senegal/Port Royal senna), native to the 
Sahelian belt. Both belong to the same genus, share the same active anthraquinone glycosides 
(sennosides), and are native to the Saharan–Sahelian corridor; the E_eco edge connects the 
genus across the corridor zone. Pottery residue from Abydos Tomb U-j, dated c. 3150 BCE, 
yields a compound-class-level identification — a terpenoid suite consistent with Cassia/Senna 
but not species-specific (McGovern et al. 2009, PNAS); McGovern et al. note the identification is 
‘not yet conclusive,’ so this is treated as probable Senna pending further archaeobotanical 
confirmation (Schmelzer and Gurib-Fakim 2008, PROTA 11(1), PROTA Foundation, 
Wageningen). The Tomb U-j context is elite and contains Levantine wine jars manufactured in 
the Jordan Valley, confirmed by instrumental neutron activation analysis (McGovern et al. 2009, 
PNAS 106(18): 7361–7366); McGovern et al. distinguish explicitly between the Levantine origin 
of the wine jars and the botanical origin of the herbal additives, noting that Cassia is ‘possibly 
native to Egypt’ and that the non-Egyptian plants identified (Artemisia seibeni, Tanacetum 
annuum) are ‘unlikely to have been traded or transplanted to Egypt at this early date.’ Senna’s 
documented native habitat is Egypt, Nubia, Arabia, and Sennar (Schmelzer and Gurib-Fakim 
2008, PROTA 11(1)) — the Nile-to-Sudan corridor zone, not the Mediterranean or southern 
Levant. The botanical evidence in the Tomb U-j jars is therefore consistent with the corridor 
model and inconsistent with a Levantine import origin for the corridor plants specifically. Native 
range: Sahelian belt. Status: Confirmed Corridor.
Commiphora africana (myrrh). Commiphora africana (myrrh) is Chemically Attested in Egypt 
from c. 3000 BCE: general historical consensus places Egyptian use from this date (Nunn 1996; 
general historical record), and an aromatic plant extract consistent with Commiphora at 
compound-class level has been identified in Badarian/Naqada period funerary wrappings at 
Mostagedda, c. 4500–3350 BCE (Jones et al. 2014, PLOS ONE, PMC4132097), though 
species-specific archaeobotanical identification is still pending. The Kahun Papyrus (1825 BCE) 
provides the earliest textual attestation; no species-specific pre-unification physical find has 
been confirmed to date. Native range: Sahel, non-native to Egypt. Status: Confirmed Corridor.
Moringa oleifera. Moringa oleifera is attested from the Old Kingdom, c. 2700–2181 BCE — 
after unification, not before it — via Moringa oil found in pharaonic tombs including the Tomb of 
Maya, and in the Ebers Papyrus (1550 BCE) (Nunn 1996; Manniche 1989; Clapham and 
Rowley-Conwy 2007). Native range: Sahelian / East African. Status: Early Post-Unification 
Corridor (E_eco edge confirmed; Egyptian attestation begins in the Old Kingdom, not pre-
unification).
Statistical Summary. The complete inventory identifies approximately 21 confirmed pre-
unification taxa. Of these, 7 are classified as corridor taxa (Saharan/sub-Saharan distribution, 
non-native to Egypt), using the following three-tier evidential system: Confirmed = macroremain 
physical evidence (actual plant material recovered archaeobotanically); Chemically Attested = 
GC-MS or spectroscopic compound-class identification in a dated archaeological context; 
Probable = general historical consensus or textual attestation, no specific physical or chemical 
find. Under this system: Ricinus communis (Confirmed: seeds in tombs from c. 4000 BCE), 
Balanites aegyptiaca (Confirmed: fruits at HK43 c. 3600 BCE; Takarkori c. 4450–3942 cal. 
BCE), Lawsonia inermis (Confirmed: physical hair specimen from HK43 Burial 16 with 
macroscopic henna-dye identification confirmed by controlled replication, c. 3400 BCE; Fletcher 
1998), Citrullus colocynthis (Confirmed: seeds at Naqada c. 3800 BCE; Takarkori c. 4361–4000 
cal. BCE), Senna italica/Cassia senna (Chemically Attested: compound-class terpenoid 
identification in Abydos Tomb U-j pottery c. 3150 BCE; McGovern et al. 2009 explicitly note the 
identification is not yet conclusive). A note on species and geography: the Senna species 
documented in Egyptian medicine is primarily Senna alexandrina (syn. Cassia acutifolia), known 
as Alexandrian senna, whose native range is confined to Upper Egypt and Nubia — the 
southernmost reach of the Saharan Highland Corridor — rather than the Lower Egyptian Nile 
Valley and Delta where the medical papyri tradition was codified and institutionalised. The 
commercial name “Alexandrian senna” derives not from the plant’s origin but from Alexandria’s 
role as the export port: the leaves were transported from Nubia and Sudan to Alexandria for 
distribution (HerbalGram, American Botanical Council; Kew Plants of the World Online). Its 
presence in the corpus is therefore consistent with introduction from the southern corridor zone 
into the Lower Egyptian institutional medical tradition, regardless of its Upper Nile nativity., 
Hyphaene thebaica (Confirmed: documented in predynastic Egyptian tombs from the earliest 
periods), and Commiphora africana (Chemically Attested: aromatic plant extract consistent with 
Commiphora resin at compound-class level in Badarian/Naqada period funerary wrappings at 
Mostagedda, Jones et al. 2014, PLOS ONE; species-specific identification pending). 
Commiphora africana is thus Chemically Attested Pre-Unification (compound-class level; 
species confirmation pending) — the Jones et al. 2014 GC-MS identification in directly AMS-
dated funerary wrappings from c. 4500–3350 BCE constitutes genuine compound-class 
evidence, not merely historical consensus. 
Two plants are classified as Old Kingdom corridor arrivals with transmission inference: Moringa 
oleifera (Old Kingdom tomb contexts, c. 2700–2181 BCE) and Cymbopogon schoenanthus 
(non-native to Egypt; corridor-distributed Mauritania to Sudan; kyphi named in Pyramid Texts c. 
2400–2300 BCE; camel grass documented as core kyphi ingredient in every surviving recipe; 
physical remains in 21st Dynasty tombs at Deir el-Bahari; most parsimonious inference is arrival 
with the corridor population, awaiting pre-unification physical confirmation). Three late post-
unification trade corridor arrivals (Hibiscus sabdariffa, Acacia senegal, Tamarindus indica) first 
appear in Egypt significantly after unification. One taxon (Artemisia seibeni) was evaluated as a 
corridor candidate but excluded because the primary source (McGovern et al. 2009) explicitly 
notes the identification as unlikely at this early date. Of the approximately 21 plants in the pre-
unification corpus identified to species level with reasonable confidence, seven have specifically 
Saharan or sub-Saharan corridor distribution. The significance of this pattern is illuminated by 
comparison with the other documented stream of non-native plant introduction into predynastic 
Egypt. The predynastic archaeobotanical record at Tell el-Iswid, HK43, and Naqada documents 
a distinct Levantine and Mediterranean agricultural introduction stream: emmer wheat, hulled 
barley, dill, oregano, lentils, and field weeds typical of Near Eastern cultivation (Fahmy 2005; 
Fahmy et al. 2008). McGovern et al. (2009, PNAS 106: 7361–7366) document that southern 
Levantine herbs — rosemary, mint, thyme — accompanied the domesticated grapevine into the 
Nile Delta at c. 3000 BCE. 
None of these Levantine or Mediterranean introductions appear in the pre-unification 
therapeutic medical corpus. The predynastic record therefore documents two geographically 
and functionally distinct introduction streams entering Egypt at overlapping periods: the 
Levantine agricultural stream, which sorted into the food economy; and the Saharan corridor 
stream, which sorted into the therapeutic pharmacopeia. The sorting of non-native plants by 
functional domain — with the therapeutic corpus drawing from the corridor stream rather than 
the Levantine stream — is the pattern this paper proposes requires explanation. This is not a 
claim of statistical over-representation against an unestablished null model; it is a documented 
geographic and functional sorting pattern in the predynastic archaeobotanical record. Whether 
this proportion exceeds what Egypt’s ecological position would produce by chance is a further, 
separate question that would require comparison against the equivalent proportions in other 
ancient pharmacopeias with documented non-native botanical content — Mesopotamian and 
Levantine medical corpora are the most direct comparators — and that comparison has not 
been undertaken here. Denominator methodology note: the denominator of 21 represents the 
subset of the Egyptian medical papyri’s plant inventory that has confirmed or probable pre-
unification archaeobotanical presence in Egypt (physical remains or residue analysis predating 
3100 BCE), drawn from the approximately 30 plant species in the corpus that have been 
identified to species level with reasonable confidence in the peer-reviewed literature (Manniche 
1989; Nunn 1996) out of approximately 160 hieroglyphic plant name entries across the papyri. 
The denominator of 15 is the subset of those 21 that are non-native to Egypt — excluding Nile 
Valley indigenous species (Salix mucronata, Ficus sycomorus, Ziziphus spina-christi, and 
similar Nilotic natives) whose presence in the corpus reflects local ecological availability rather 
than external introduction. The numerator of 7 is the subset of those 15 non-native pre-
unification taxa whose native range is specifically Saharan or sub-Saharan corridor distribution, 
rather than Levantine, Mediterranean, or pan-tropical. Artemisia seibeni was evaluated as a 
corridor candidate and excluded because McGovern et al. (2009) explicitly flag its identification 
as unlikely at this early date. Commiphora africana is counted as Chemically Attested (not 
Confirmed) because the Jones et al. (2014) identification is compound-class level, not species-
specific. These inclusion and exclusion criteria are stated explicitly so that the proportions can 
be reproduced, challenged, or refined by specialist archaeobotanical review. That approximately 
47% of the identifiable external pre-unification taxa have specifically Saharan or sub-Saharan 
corridor distribution, rather than Levantine or Mediterranean origin, is the pattern this paper 
proposes requires explanation. These proportions establish that the Saharan botanical 
component of the pre-unification pharmacopeia is not marginal and is not adequately explained 
by Egypt’s immediate ecological neighbourhood. A clarifying note on indigenous Egyptian 
plants: the willow serves as a control case. 
The willow documented in the Egyptian medical papyri under the hieroglyphic name trt is the 
Nile Valley native Salix mucronata (safsaf willow), confirmed as indigenous to the Nile River 
canal system (Manniche 1989; Al Sherif et al. 2009), not a corridor import. It is attested across 
multiple papyri — the Edwin Smith Papyrus (Case 41, inflamed wounds), the Berlin Papyrus 
(toothache), the Brooklyn Papyrus (venomous bites), and the Ebers Papyrus (inflammation and 
appetite) — precisely because it was locally and abundantly available. Its presence in the 
corpus confirms that Egyptian physicians recorded effective plants regardless of geographic 
origin. The corridor hypothesis predicts exactly this: the Saharan component arrived with the 
Shemsu Hor, but Egypt's own indigenous pharmacopeia was already in use and continued to be 
documented alongside it. The indigenous Nile Valley pharmacopeia and the corridor 
pharmacopeia are complementary, not competing. (If a non-corridor, non-indigenous plant — 
e.g., a Levantine or Mesopotamian native — were confirmed in a pre-unification Egyptian 
medical context, this would weaken the hypothesis, as it would suggest systematic acquisition 
of external pharmacopeia through channels other than the corridor population.)
The seven confirmed/probable pre-unification corridor taxa, the two Old Kingdom arrivals, and 
the three post-unification trade arrivals set out above (Statistical Summary) together complete 
the network. All seven of the confirmed/probable pre-unification taxa are documented in the 
Mauritanian pharmacopeia: six in contemporary clinical surveys (Yebouk 2026; Ehel Adja 2025) 
and Lawsonia inermis in the Adrar Province ethnopharmacological record (Thouzery 1997). The 
evidence density for the Richat connection has now reached the level at which the under-
excavation of Mauritania (Axiom 5) is the primary remaining limitation on confidence, rather than 
absence of any documentary trail.

Negative Cases. A fair test of the transmission hypothesis requires asking not only which 
corridor plants appear in Egyptian medicine but whether there are corridor plants that should 
appear — given their documented medicinal use and corridor presence — but do not. The 
selection filter hypothesis predicts the following criterion, stated in advance of the case analysis: 
plants transmitted to the corpus were those with broad, low-risk, multi-domain therapeutic 
applications manageable by non-specialist practitioners; plants with systemic cardiac or alkaloid 
toxicity requiring specialist dosage knowledge were not transmitted to the institutional corpus; 
food plants whose primary pastoral value is nutritional rather than pharmacological were not 
transmitted as therapeutics. This criterion is pre-specified and applied consistently to the three 
cases below — it is not derived from the cases themselves. The following preliminary analysis 
documents three corridor-zone plants from the Hammiche and Maiza (2006) Tassili N’Ajjer 
pharmacopeia that are absent from the Egyptian medical papyri corpus as documented in 
Manniche (1989) and von Deines, Grapow, and Westendorf (Grundriss der Medizin der Alten 
Ägypter, 1954–1973), alongside the corresponding transmission model interpretation. 
Of the 80+ plants documented by Hammiche and Maiza (2006) at Tassili N’Ajjer, 13 are 
documented in the Egyptian medical corpus. The three negative cases below are drawn from 
the remainder; a full systematic analysis of all 67+ remaining species against the papyri corpus 
is identified as a research agenda item for future comparative ethnobotanical work. (1) 
Calotropis procera (Sodom apple; Arabic: tourha; documented at Tassili for skin diseases, 
dermatosis, and infected sores; Hammiche and Maiza 2006): confirmed present in the Egyptian 
Nile Valley flora by charcoal analysis at the predynastic sites of Adaïma and Elkab (Upper 
Egypt, c. 3500–3100 BCE; Fahmy 2005, Vegetation History and Archaeobotany 14), placing it 
physically in Egypt during the unification window. It is confirmed anti-inflammatory, antifungal, 
and analgesic, with documented use across the corridor arc from Mauritania through North 
Africa. Not identified by botanical name in the Egyptian medical papyri. Interpretation: its toxic 
latex profile (cardenolides; dose-dependent cytotoxicity) makes it a specialist plant with a 
narrow therapeutic window. On the transmission model, the corridor pharmacopeia was not 
transmitted wholesale but selectively — the plants with broad, low-risk, multi-domain pastoral 
applications were transmitted; plants requiring specialist knowledge to apply safely were not. 
Calotropis procera’s confirmed presence in Egypt combined with its absence from the 
institutionalised corpus is the most informative negative case: it demonstrates that the 
transmission was selective, not comprehensive, and that the selection filter operated on risk 
profile rather than geographic availability. (2) Panicum turgidum (desert millet; Arabic: 
merkba/markouba; documented at Tassili for digestive and nutritional conditions; Hammiche 
and Maiza 2006): archaeobotanically documented at Nabta Playa (corridor Node 6 zone) and at 
Farafra Oasis (Egyptian Sahara, mid-Neolithic, 7130–6190 BP), confirming its presence across 
the corridor and in Egypt from early periods. Its common name in Egypt and Arabia is 
‘taman/thaman’, indicating Egyptian familiarity. Not identified in the Egyptian medical papyri as a 
therapeutic agent. Interpretation: Panicum turgidum is a food grass and forage plant; its pastoral 
use is primarily nutritional, not pharmacological. 
The transmission model predicts transmission of the medicinal pharmacopeia, not every 
pastoral resource. A food grass whose pastoral value is caloric rather than therapeutic is not 
predicted to enter an institutionalised medical corpus. Its absence is expected under the model 
and uninformative as a negative case. (3) Ephedra (Ephedra alata subsp. alenda; documented 
at Tassili as Ephedra altissima for respiratory diseases and vascular conditions; Hammiche and 
Maiza 2006): native to Egypt (Sinai), Algeria, Libya, Mauritania, Morocco, Tunisia, and Western 
Sahara — native to Egypt itself and corridor-distributed. Tuareg pastoral communities of the 
Saharan corridor consume its fleshy cones as food and use the green stems, roots, and cones 
medicinally; the active compounds are ephedrine and pseudoephedrine, documented 
vasoconstrictors and bronchodilators. Not identified by botanical name in the Egyptian medical 
papyri despite Egypt’s rich respiratory treatment tradition (Ebers 305–335). Interpretation: 
Ephedra contains ephedrine, a potent alkaloid with a narrow therapeutic window and significant 
cardiovascular toxicity at elevated doses. On the selection filter hypothesis, it falls in the same 
category as Calotropis procera — a pharmacologically active corridor plant excluded from 
institutional encoding because its risk profile made it unsuitable for a standardised corpus. 
Alternatively, the Egyptian corpus may have substituted Nile Valley respiratory plants for 
Ephedra’s applications without preserving the species name. 
The present evidence does not distinguish between these two explanations, and this case is 
therefore not counted as support for the selection-filter hypothesis. Preliminary interpretation: 
the three absent plants are informative in systematically different ways. Calotropis procera is the 
strongest negative case: physically present in predynastic Egypt, pharmacologically active, 
corridor-native, yet absent from the corpus — confirming that the transmission operated a 
selection filter based on risk profile rather than geographic availability. Panicum turgidum is 
uninformative as a negative case: its absence is predicted under the model because it is a food 
grass rather than a therapeutic agent. Ephedra is an inconclusive case: corridor-native, native to 
Egypt, pharmacologically powerful, yet absent from the corpus. Its absence is compatible with 
either a toxicity-based selection filter (as with Calotropis) or silent substitution by a Nile Valley 
respiratory plant, and the present evidence cannot distinguish between them. The hypothesis 
predicts not that every corridor plant was transmitted but that the specific suite with broad, low-
risk, multi-domain pastoral therapeutic applications was. These negative cases are consistent 
with this prediction and are flagged for specialist evaluation.
7. Linguistic Corridor: The adr- Root as a Saharan Pastoral 
Ethnobotanical Marker
The adr- Root Across the Saharan Pastoral Network
Epistemic scope. This section documents the ethnobotanical, ethnomusicological, and 
toponymic record of Commiphora africana and its associated knowledge system across the 
Saharan pastoral corridor. The evidence assembled here — the adr- name trail across six 
corridor nodes, the plant's documented use in pastoral commercial and musical material culture, 
and the cosmological naming cluster — constitutes a documented pattern of pastoral knowledge 
embedded in material culture, commercial practice, and oral tradition across the same 
geographic arc as the corridor migration model. This evidence is presented as what it is: a rich, 
independently documented record of how a specific plant was known, named, used, and 
culturally encoded by pastoral populations across the Saharan corridor. The adr- distribution is 
contextual ethnobotanical data that adds geographic coherence and cultural depth to the 
corridor model. The primary evidential pillars of this paper are the archaeobotanical record 
(Section 6), the chronological inversion of the medical corpus (Section 1), and the One Medicine 
integration at Hierakonpolis (Section 9). This section provides independent corroborating 
context from a different disciplinary domain; it does not independently establish transmission 
and is not claimed to do so.
Three structural limitations govern all inferences here. First, Berber underwent a major levelling 
episode around 100–200 CE that erased earlier linguistic diversity, making reconstruction of 
wild-plant names to the pastoral expansion period (5,000–4,000 BP) unlikely on current 
evidence (Blench 2021). Second, the absence of any adr- term in ancient Egyptian — where the 
plant was ntyw, an import term — is explained by language shift during integration, but this 
explanation is plausible rather than independently evidenced. Third, borrowing direction 
between Berber substrate and Hassaniya Arabic does not establish absolute chronological 
depth.
The adr- distribution. Burkill's authoritative ethnobotanical record (1985) documents the adr- 
name for Commiphora africana across the Saharan pastoral zone: adras (Tamachek, Mali), 
adres (Arabic, Mali), adress (Arabic, Niger), and adras/adress/adriss (Arabic [Maure], Mauritania 
and Senegal). Non-Saharan populations use unrelated names (Fula-Pulaar badadi/badi; 
Bambara badi/barakanté; Hausa dashi/ikitchi), confirming the adr- root as indigenous to the 
Saharan Berber pastoral network specifically — the network this paper proposes as the 
knowledge transmission pathway.
The adr- name trail constitutes four independently documented data points across the corridor: 
(1) the Mauritanian pharmacopeia at the Richat zone (Thouzery 1997; Yebouk et al. 2020; 
Burkill 1985); (2) the Tamachek Tuareg of Mali's Adrar des Ifoghas and Tassili n'Ajjer zone 
(Burkill 1985; Hammiche and Maiza 2006); (3) the Tuareg pastoralists of Illizi (Algeria/Libya 
border, Fezzan approach; Miara et al. 2019), whose pastoral profile (camels, cattle, sheep, 
goats) matches the Haimov-Kochman et al. (2005) knowledge-source identification; and (4) the 
Sudanese traditional pharmacopeia at the corridor's Egyptian terminus (University of Khartoum, 
2025), documenting Commiphora africana resin in active use for inhalation, wound treatment, 
and oral health — precisely the application domains documented in the Egyptian medical 
corpus.
The name does not appear in sub-Saharan Niger-Congo or Chadic languages. It does not 
appear in ancient Egyptian (where the plant was ntyw, an import term). It is a Saharan Berber 
pastoral fossil, documented across the same geographic arc as the corridor migration model, 
carried by populations circumstantially linked, via the two-branch pattern documented in Section 
9, to the deep Saharan lineage Salem et al. (2025) trace to the Takarkori node, and whose 
pastoral profile is structurally consistent with the herder-based knowledge source identified by 
Haimov-Kochman et al. (2005). The linguistic evidence does not prove transmission; it is 
structurally consistent with it at every documented node.
Historical depth. Whether adras/adress can be reconstructed to Proto-Berber or represents a 
later innovation within the Tuareg branch is a question for specialist comparative Berber 
linguistics and is beyond this paper's scope. Blench (2021) argues that the pastoral expansion 
was a primary Berber migration, explicitly rejecting the hypothesis that it was a separate 
vanished population; however, the levelling episode erases earlier linguistic diversity, making 
the inability to reconstruct adr- to Proto-Berber a consequence of levelling, not evidence against 
its antiquity.
A second, methodologically independent linguistic analysis reaches a broadly convergent 
conclusion by different means. Militarev (2021, Études et Documents Berbères 43(1): 133–160, 
DOI: 10.3917/edb.043.0135) uses lexicostatistics and glottochronology, rather than plant-name 
archaeobotany, to date the entry of Proto-Berber-Chadic speakers into North Africa and their 
subsequent spread throughout the Sahara to the onset of the Saharan rock-art “Pastoral 
Period,” beginning approximately 6,000 BCE. This sits within the broader AHP window this 
paper’s corridor model traces (Section 4, c. 15,000–5,000 BP), during which populations 
experienced repeated partial retreats to highland refugia across multiple generations before the 
final 3800–3100 BCE exodus toward the Nile; it does not itself establish presence at that later 
date. Two independent methods — Blench’s plant-name archaeobotany and Militarev’s 
lexicostatistics — therefore converge on the same broad conclusion: Berber-speaking 
populations were present and pastoral-dispersing across the Sahara during the AHP. They 
diverge sharply on geographic direction: Blench argues an eastern, Nile Valley origin dispersing 
west, the opposite of this paper’s corridor model, while Militarev’s Sahara-wide dispersal is 
directionally agnostic. This paper does not attempt to resolve that directional disagreement 
here, and notes only the narrower claim its linguistic evidence actually requires: that a Berber-
speaking pastoral population inhabited the Sahara throughout the AHP.
The adrar toponym (pl. idaren) is documented in Herodotos (c. 500 BC) as a Berber 
geographical term, confirming pre-Islamic Saharan depth for the adr- root family across its 
topographic register.
Toponymic context. The Berber word adrar (Tifinagh: ⴰⴷⵔⴰⵔ) means "mountain" or "highland 
plateau" and names every major highland refugium in the proposed corridor: Adrar Plateau 
(Mauritania, Richat zone), Adrar des Ifoghas (Mali), Adrar Province (Algeria), Adrar Afao (Tassili 
n'Ajjer), and Tadrart Acacus (Libya, Node 3). No peer-reviewed linguistic source establishes a 
shared etymological root between adrar (highland) and adras (Commiphora), and no such claim 
is made here. The co-occurrence is noted as geographic context, not as linguistic evidence of 
transmission.
The cosmological naming: um el barka. The Burkill (1985) ethnobotanical record documents a 
further Arabic name for Commiphora africana in Nigeria: um el barka (Vogel, cited in Burkill 
1985; NIGERIA, ARABIC). The name translates as ‘mother of blessing’ or ‘mother of fortune’ — 
umm = mother; al-barka = blessing, grace, fortune. This is the umm-construction (‘mother of X’) 
applied in Arabic naming to convey that the named thing is the foundational or generative 
source of the quality it names. A plant called ‘mother of blessing’ encodes a cosmological status 
claim: the plant is conceived as the primal source of the healing power it delivers. This naming 
logic is structurally identical to the Salt Papyrus 825 encoding documented in Section 2: when 
Ra’s tears become myrrh, the plant’s healing is attributed to a divine generative source. Both 
the Arabic pastoral tradition and the Egyptian theological tradition independently encode the 
same plant under the same explanatory logic — a convergence consistent with the Heka 
transmission model. The companion name already documented in the adr- trail entry — mbarkat 
(Arabic, Niger) — is a variant of the same barka/baraka root (blessing, divine grace), confirming 
that the cosmological naming cluster is not confined to Nigeria but extends across the Niger-to-
Nigeria arc. Additionally, Burkill (1985) documents that the Tamachek distinguish taghalbas as 
the specific Tamachek name for the gum-resin produced by adras, distinct from the tree itself — 
demonstrating that Tuareg pastoral knowledge encoded a pharmacological distinction between 
the plant and its medicinally active resin, a level of botanical precision that substantially 
predates any formal taxonomy.
Material culture: wood use. Burkill (1985) documents that Commiphora africana is one of two 
woods — the other is Balanites aegyptiaca, the corridor’s other flagship medical plant — used 
across the Saharan pastoral zone to carve standard market measures (nẹfga, mudel) for selling 
fruit, gum, henna leaves, and liquids, and is ‘the wood used to make the Berber violin known as 
tidinit, an instrument of four strings of hair’ (Burkill 1985, The Useful Plants of West Tropical 
Africa, Vol. 1, Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew). Variants of the tidinit are documented across the 
Sahel from Mauritania through Mali, Senegal, and Niger to the Saharawi communities of Algeria 
— the same corridor arc as the adr- name trail (Guignard 2005, Musique, honneur et plaisir au 
Sahara, Geuthner). The wood is documented as soft yet termite-resistant (Orwa et al. 2009) — 
easy to carve, durable in arid conditions. The same two plants this paper identifies as the 
corridor’s flagship medical plants were therefore also selected, across the same pastoral zone, 
for precision-carved commercial and musical instruments, extending Commiphora’s 
documented domains from medicine into craft. Whether Pastoral Period rock art depicts the 
tidinit or its ancestors is untested: the two documented multi-period Saharan rock art 
assemblages richest in potential musical content — the Tassili dance scene (Lhote 1958) and 
the Tadrart Acacus site of Ti-n-Anneuin, catastrophically vandalised in 2009 (di Lernia, Gallinaro 
and Zerboni 2010) — have not been examined specifically for instrument depictions (Prediction 
7).

9. The Integration: Where Pastoral Knowledge Became State Medicine
The timeline traced in Section 5 converges on two institutional endpoints that together define 
where pastoral botanical knowledge was absorbed by the Dynastic state. Hierakonpolis (Node 
7) represents the Upper Egyptian integration point, where Predynastic communities were 
already managing penicillin-producing grain stores, applying mouldy bread to wounds, and 
treating fractures successfully enough that healed breaks are documented throughout the HK43 
skeletal population — a tradition of care consistent with the later, more precisely quantified Old 
Kingdom evidence (93.3% successful alignment at Giza; Section 6). Sais in the western Nile 
Delta, with cult attestation from Naqada II (c. 3600–3350 BCE), represents the Lower Egyptian 
destination for the obstetrics and gynaecology thread of the pastoral pharmacopeia, although its 
institutional role as a dedicated medical training centre is documented only from the Middle 
Kingdom onward (see Table S2). The geographic range between these two endpoints 
encompasses the full corridor destination.
The specific institutional mechanism of absorption was the Per-Ankh — the Houses of Life. 
These temple-integrated institutions, whose oldest surviving references date to two royal 
decrees of the late Old Kingdom (c. 2200 BCE), were simultaneously scriptorium, medical 
school, library, and training centre under the protection of Thoth (Gardiner 1938, ‘The House of 
Life,’ Journal of Egyptian Archaeology 24(2): 157–179; Nunn 1996, Ancient Egyptian Medicine, 
British Museum Press, pp. 121–123). The Edwin Smith Papyrus — the most rational surviving 
medical text — preserves scribal signs in more frequent use in earlier periods than its own c. 
1600 BCE copy date (see Series Context for the full dating debate), indicating that its 
knowledge base predates the institution that codified it. The hypothesis proposes that the 
Houses of Life were not the source of the medical knowledge; they were the institutional vessel 
into which an existing oral pastoral pharmacopeia was absorbed, codified, and distributed. 
Scribes meticulously copied the papyri and distributed them across Egypt (Gardiner 1938: 157–
179). This copying and distribution is the mechanism by which the rational botanical 
pharmacopeia was standardised and spread — while the Heka theological framing developed 
around it across subsequent centuries.
The professional evolution documented by Bestetti et al. (2024, Cureus, DOI: 
10.7759/cureus.67195) provides the institutional timeline with precision: Narmer’s unification (c. 
3100 BCE) converted shamanic healers into Sem priests responsible for the king’s health; the 
Fourth Dynasty elevation of Ra (2613–2494 BCE) restructured the religious framework; the Fifth 
Dynasty (2494–2345 BCE) created the Priests of Sekhmet specifically to inspect bull meat for 
public safety during mass Ra festivities — which Bestetti et al. argue represents the first 
documented recognition of zoonotic transmission (an interpretation the paper notes, while 
acknowledging this reading is not consensus Egyptology). By 2487 BCE, Ni-Ankh-Sekhmet 
performed the first formally documented curative medical procedure. This sequence maps 
directly onto the Heka encoding model: the older the institutional layer, the closer to the original 
pastoral botanical knowledge it sits; the newer the layer, the more fully the theological framing 
has developed around it.
The Priests of Sekhmet represent the most specific evidence of pastoral veterinary knowledge 
institutionalized. Scholarly analysis of the wab-Sekhmet priest describes him as ‘an expert in 
making medical diagnoses by observing blood circulation and the veterinarian responsible for 
the bull stables in the temples’ — ‘the only one with the ability to distinguish the living 
hypostases of the god Apis and enabled to certify the purity of the animals to be sacrificed’ 
(Känel 1984; Torras Benezet 2021). The pure priests ‘inspected the entrails of the beast and its 
blood, smelling and perhaps tasting it, to see if it was pure and free of disease.’ This is precisely 
what pastoral herders do: they observe animal health daily, recognise disease in organs and 
blood, distinguish healthy from infected animals. The wab-Sekhmet did not invent this diagnostic 
practice — he institutionalized it within the state’s ritual apparatus. The pastoral knowledge of 
the herd became the priestly knowledge of the sacrificial bull, encoded within the Sekhmet 
theological system. This is the Heka integration made concrete: the plant-based and animal-
observation knowledge of the corridor pastoralists, preserved in the medical pharmacopeia, and 
administered by priests whose role was defined by the same empirical observation their pastoral 
predecessors had practised for millennia.
The institutional unity of human and animal medicine documented at Hierakonpolis has a formal 
scholarly framework. The ‘One Medicine’ or ‘One Health’ movement — now endorsed by the 
World Health Organization, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, and 
the World Organisation for Animal Health — traces its philosophical roots explicitly to ancient 
Egypt. Lerner (2013, ‘The philosophical roots of the “One Medicine” movement,’ Studia 
Philosophica Estonica 6(2): 97–109, DOI: 10.12697/spe.2013.6.2.07) traces the modern ‘One 
Medicine’ framework through its two principal intellectual founders: Rudolf Virchow and Calvin 
W. Schwabe. Schwabe’s own lecture paper (‘Comparative Medicine in Ancient Egypt: Origins of 
Early Biomedical Theories,’ UC Davis, vetmed.ucdavis.edu) states directly: ‘In virtually all 
ancient healing traditions no distinction was at first made between human and veterinary 
practice and Greek medicine, for example, began to separate into its two branches only in the 
third century BC. That fusion as “one medicine” also remains true of most traditional medical 
systems throughout the world today.’ Schwabe further characterised predynastic Egypt as ‘a 
fused society in which significant disciplinary and institutional separations were not yet evident 
between such currently diverse notions as animal husbandry, religion and healing’ — a 
description structurally identical to the corridor pastoral world documented across Nodes 1–7 of 
this paper’s framework. This was not Egypt’s invention. It was the pastoral world’s inheritance, 
brought by the corridor population and institutionalised into the Dynastic state.
The archival evidence for this unified tradition is preserved in the Kahun excavation itself. The 
Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus — already this paper’s anchor evidence for Prediction 1 as the 
oldest surviving rational medical text — was found in the same physical document cache as the 
Kahun Veterinary Papyrus, a text treating cattle, dogs, birds, and fish with the same empirical, 
case-structured approach as the Edwin Smith. They were stored and published together by 
Griffith (1898, Hieratic Papyri from Kahun and Gurob, Bernard Quaritch, London; 
Gynaecological text: Plates V–VI; Veterinary text: Plate VII). The peer-reviewed translation of 
the Veterinary Papyrus, Walker (1964, ‘The Veterinary Papyrus of Kahun: a revised translation 
and interpretation,’ Veterinary Record 76: 198–200), confirms its rational, empirical character. 
Two medical traditions — one for humans, one for animals — were found together because they 
originated from the same tradition. They were never separated in the knowledge system that 
produced them. Their physical co-location at Kahun is the archival signature of the unified 
pastoral tradition that preceded writing. Schwabe (1978, Cattle, Priests, and Progress in 
Medicine, University of Minnesota Press) explicitly argued that this co-location reflects an 
original One Medicine knowledge system: ‘At least some Egyptian priests were healers of both 
people and cattle,’ and that this dual practice ‘was a fused society in which the underlying 
biological observations which led to these theories’ earliest at least implicit formulations in 
predynastic times, that is from the 4th millennium or earlier BC.’
The material record of bee products in the corridor extends the unified knowledge system back 
into the pre-papyrus pastoral window. Roffet-Salque, Dunne, Evershed et al. (2015, 
‘Widespread exploitation of the honeybee by early Neolithic farmers,’ Nature 527: 226–230, 
DOI: 10.1038/nature15757) used lipid residue analysis of over 6,400 Neolithic pottery vessels 
across Europe, the Near East, and North Africa, identifying beeswax continuously from the 
seventh millennium BCE. North Africa is explicitly in their dataset — placing honey and bee 
product exploitation in the Neolithic North African pastoral zone of the corridor well before any 
Egyptian medical papyrus. Honey is documented in the Edwin Smith and Ebers papyri as a 
wound antiseptic, and its antibacterial mechanism (hydrogen peroxide, osmotic effect, low pH, 
bee defensin-1) is now clinically confirmed with medical-grade honey licensed in Europe and 
Australia (Barazesh et al. 2025, already cited in this paper). The question of when pastoral 
populations first applied honey to wounds cannot be answered by the papyri; it can be bounded: 
the knowledge was available, the material was in use, and the living successor pastoral cultures 
of the upper Nile — the Dinka and Nuer, whom Schwabe identifies as the closest surviving 
parallel to predynastic Egyptian cattle culture — are documented using ash from dung fires as 
an antiseptic wound dressing on cattle (Schwabe 1978, Cattle, Priests, and Progress in 
Medicine, University of Minnesota Press). The knowledge that ash is antiseptic and that honey 
heals wounds was not invented in Egypt. It was documented in Egypt. The distinction matters 
for understanding the inversion: the knowledge was always there; what the papyri provide is its 
first written record. The gap in the corridor archaeozoological record is explicitly acknowledged. 
No systematic palaeopathological study of healed animal fractures has been conducted on the 
faunal assemblages at Takarkori, Messak Settafet, or Nabta Playa comparable to Van Neer et 
al. (2015) at Hierakonpolis. The bones exist at these sites — di Lernia et al. (2013, ‘Inside the 
“African Cattle Complex,”’ PLOS ONE 8(2): e56879, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0056879) 
document extensive faunal assemblages at Messak Settafet; Wendorf, F. and Schild, R. (1998) 
document cattle remains at Nabta Playa; di Lernia et al. (2024, DOI: 
10.1371/journal.pone.0310739) confirm adequate bone preservation conditions at Takarkori. 
They have simply not been examined for palaeopathological evidence of sustained care. 
This is a research agenda item: if the Messak Settafet and Nabta Playa faunal assemblages 
were subjected to systematic palaeopathological analysis comparable to Van Neer et al. 2015, 
healed fractures in domestic cattle consistent with sustained human intervention would be 
expected on the One Medicine model. The current absence of such evidence reflects under-
analysis, not under-excavation. The Hierakonpolis evidence stands as the corridor’s currently 
documented earliest material record of the practice; the corridor gap awaits the fieldwork to 
close it. The Egyptian theological tradition had already unified these domains in a single divine 
figure: Nefertum (Nefertem), son of Sekhmet and Ptah in the Memphite triad, and 
simultaneously god of perfume, god of healing, and divine aromatherapist — attested in the 
Pyramid Texts from c. 2350 BCE (Wilkinson, R.H. 2003, The Complete Gods and Goddesses of 
Ancient Egypt, Thames and Hudson, pp. 184–185). Nefertum embodies the unity the paper 
argues arrived with the corridor population: the god of fragrant aromatic oils is simultaneously 
the god of healing, and his mother is Sekhmet — the goddess whose medical corpus this paper 
analyses. The Pyramid Texts entry for Nefertum (Spell 266) describes him as ‘the lotus blossom 
which is before the nose of Ra’ — an aromatic restorative healing the sun god’s suffering — 
precisely the unification of scent and therapy that the corridor body-knowledge system encoded. 
The Egyptians did not simply practise medicine and perfumery separately. They had a god for 
their unity, and he was the son of the goddess of medicine.
The Unified Body-Knowledge System: Medicine, Gynaecology, Hygiene, 
Perfume, and Embalming as a Single Transmission
The preceding sections have tracked the corridor botanical suite through the Egyptian medical 
papyri. A structurally significant observation emerges when the analysis is widened: the same 
corridor plants do not define one Egyptian institutional domain — they define all of them 
simultaneously. The same botanical set underlies the Egyptian medical corpus, the 
gynaecological corpus, the perfume and aromatic industry, the soap and cleaning agents, the 
hair care and cosmetic tradition, and the embalming and mummification practice. This 
observation is not, by itself, decisive. A plant with genuinely versatile chemical properties — 
antimicrobial, surfactant, aromatic — can be independently discovered and adopted for 
medicine, hygiene, and preservation by any population with access to it, with no transmission 
event required; ethnobotanists have named this pattern ethnobotanical convergence (Garnatje, 
Peñuelas and Vallès 2017, ‘Ethnobotany, phylogeny and ‘omics’ for human health and food 
security,’ Trends in Plant Science 22: 187–191, DOI: 10.1016/j.tplants.2017.01.001). 
Distinguishing convergence from shared ancestry requires more than counting the number of 
domains a plant appears in. Teixidor-Toneu, Jordan and Hawkins (2018, ‘Comparative 
phylogenetic methods and the cultural evolution of medicinal plant use,’ Nature Plants 4: 754–
761, DOI: 10.1038/s41477-018-0226-6) formalise the discriminating tests: a use test (is the 
application shared or divergent between populations), a linguistic test (are the names for the 
plant cognate, indicating shared descent, or independently coined), and an ancestry test (does 
the distribution of use track a genealogical relationship between the populations, rather than 
mere geographic proximity). This paper’s multi-domain evidence is assessed against all three. 
The use test is what the domain map below documents. The linguistic test is partly met: the adr- 
root documented across corridor nodes in Section 7 is a cognate name persisting across six 
corridor populations, establishing that the source tradition was linguistically continuous rather 
than independently coined at each node. It does not persist into ancient Egyptian, where the 
plant is instead ntyw, an import term (Section 7); the account offered there for this gap — 
language shift during integration — is plausible but not independently evidenced. The linguistic 
test therefore supports the coherence of the corridor source population but not, on its own, the 
specific transmission step into Egypt. The ancestry test is partially met, through a two-branch 
pattern that is itself informative. Salem et al. (2025) establish that the Takarkori lineage's closest 
ancient affinity is to the Taforalt population of Morocco (15,000 BP). Among present-day 
populations, this deep ancestral pool shows two distinct modern echoes, running in two different 
directions. Southward, Takarkori itself shows its strongest present-day affinity to Fulani herders 
sampled across eight Sahelian countries — the 'FulaniA' cluster, first identified by D'Atanasio et 
al. (2023, Current Biology 33(24): 5495–5504, DOI: 10.1016/j.cub.2023.10.075) and 
independently corroborated by Fortes-Lima et al. (2025, American Journal of Human Genetics 
112(2): 261–275, DOI: 10.1016/j.ajhg.2024.12.015); Salem et al. note that this Fulani signal 
shares less drift with Taforalt specifically than with Takarkori. Within the Maghreb and western 
Sahara, by contrast, the Taforalt component itself is carried most strongly not by Fulani but by 
Saharawi and other Berber-speaking populations, at statistically significant levels (Serra-Vidal et 
al. 2019, Current Biology 29(22): 3953–3959.e4, DOI: 10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.050; outgroup-f3 = 
0.218–0.222, Mann-Whitney p = 4.52×10 ¹ ). Saharawi communities and the Hassaniya-
⁻ ⁵
speaking Bidhan/Haratine communities of Mauritania whose pharmacopeia this paper 
documents botanically are the same broad ethnolinguistic population, divided by a twentieth-
century colonial border: both descend from the same historical fusion of Beni Ḥassān Arab 
tribes with Sanhaja Berber populations, and both speak Hassaniya Arabic. This is the 
geographically and historically relevant branch for this paper's corridor model, which traces a 
population that remained in the western Sahara/Maghreb rather than moving south into the 
Sahel. No study has yet compared the ancient Takarkori or Taforalt genomes directly against 
modern Saharawi or Mauritanian samples; the chain here runs through Taforalt as an 
intermediate population, not through a direct test, and this link therefore remains circumstantial 
rather than confirmed. On the Teixidor-Toneu framework, a use pattern drawn from a 
linguistically coherent source population is better explained by shared ancestry than by 
independent convergent discovery when the ancestry test is fully met; with the ancestry test 
only partially and indirectly met here, that inference is correspondingly weaker here — though 
convergence cannot be excluded for any single plant considered alone, the linguistic test does 
not itself bridge to Egypt, and the multi-domain pattern is treated in what follows as evidence to 
be weighed alongside the corridor’s other evidentiary pillars, not as proof on its own.
Balanites aegyptiaca — confirmed pre-unification at HK43 Burial 333, c. 3600 BCE — is called 
the ‘soap berry tree’ in English for documented reason. Its bark and roots lather in water and are 
documented as a soap substitute across its entire range from Mauritania to Sudan to Egypt 
(Burkill 1985, The Useful Plants of West Tropical Africa, p. 521). Its saponins — steroidal 
saponins, sapogenins, and diosgenins — are the active surfactant compounds; their broad 
pharmacological profile including surfactant and molluscicidal activity is comprehensively 
documented (Thakkar et al. 2025, Phytomedicine Plus 5(2): 100804; Shinde et al. 2025). In the 
Mauritanian Adrar zone specifically — the zone containing the Richat Structure — Balanites 
aegyptiaca fruit is documented as used in the manufacturing of soap and cosmetic products 
(Abdelaziz et al. 2020, Plants 9(3): 401, DOI: 10.3390/plants9030401). Simultaneously, balanos 
oil from Balanites aegyptiaca was one of only two carrier oils preferred above all others by 
ancient Egyptian perfumers for its neutral odour and resistance to rancidity (Manniche 1999, 
Sacred Luxuries, Cornell University Press). The same tree that the corridor pastoralists used to 
wash themselves and their textiles became the primary carrier oil of the Egyptian perfume 
industry — because it arrived with both functions already established.
Two further dimensions of Balanites aegyptiaca in the Egyptian papyri complete the picture and 
illuminate the pastoral logic most directly. First, the Ebers Papyrus ear section (rubric 764) 
explicitly names ‘fresh balanite oil’ as the primary treatment for impaired hearing: ‘The beginning 
of remedies for an ear whose hearing is poor: red ochre, juice of tamarix, are ground fine with 
fresh balanite oil’ (Ebers Papyrus rubric 764; von Deines, Grapow, and Westendorf 1954–1973, 
Grundriss der Medizin der alten Ägypter). This places Balanites in the ENT section of the oldest 
comprehensive medical text — a seventh institutional domain alongside medicine, perfume, 
soap, hair care, embalming, and pest control. Second, and most significant for the pastoral logic 
argument: Balanites aegyptiaca has confirmed molluscicidal and cercariacidal activity — its 
saponins kill the freshwater snails and copepods that serve as intermediate hosts for 
schistosomiasis and guinea worm; aqueous extracts exhibit molluscicidal activity against 
Biomphalaria pfeifferi and Lymnaea natalensis, and cercariacidal activity against Schistosoma 
mansoni cercariae (Molla et al. 2013; Shinde et al. 2025). Guinea worm (Dracunculus 
medinensis) is already documented in this paper’s Table 1 as treated in the Ebers Papyrus by a 
method unchanged to the present. The Ebers Papyrus itself documents the treatment of guinea 
worm. Balanites aegyptiaca kills the copepod that transmits guinea worm. A pastoral population 
herding cattle, camels, sheep, and goats at Sahelian water sources — where schistosomiasis 
and guinea worm are endemic — would not simply know that Balanites cleans the body. They 
would know that Balanites cleans the water. The saponin emulsion from Balanites fruit or bark is 
toxic to the snail and copepod vectors but does not affect mammals, and fish killed by it remain 
edible (Orwa et al. 2009, World Agroforestry Centre species database; Thakkar et al. 2025). 
This is not a coincidental property. This is the most practically critical knowledge a pastoral 
people in parasite-endemic Sahelian environments could possess: the plant that grows along 
their water sources, that their camels browse extensively, that they use to wash their bodies and 
clean their textiles, simultaneously kills the parasites in the water their animals drink. The fact 
that Balanites is the primary Egyptian perfume carrier oil, the primary soap agent, and the 
specific treatment for guinea worm in the Ebers Papyrus is, on the transmission hypothesis, 
consistent with a population that arrived at the Nile already knowing every property of this plant 
simultaneously, because their survival had depended on that knowledge for generations in the 
environments where the plant grows. Independent discovery of each application within Egypt 
cannot be excluded for Balanites considered alone; the case for transmission rests on this 
pattern recurring across multiple, chemically unrelated plants with the same linguistic and 
ancestry support set out above, not on Balanites in isolation.
Ricinus communis (castor oil) — confirmed pre-unification in Egyptian tombs from c. 4000 BCE 
— is simultaneously documented as a medical oil (Ebers Papyrus rubric 251), a hair growth 
agent (Ebers rubrics 437–476, the dedicated hair chapter: ‘the castor oil plant as promoting hair 
growth in women’), a perfume ingredient, and a primary cleaning agent. An Egyptian text from 
210 BCE instructs the supply of castor oil and mineral natron for the cleaning of flax in bleaching 
houses — and castor oil, unusually among vegetable oils, does not require boiling to be 
saponified by natron, meaning proto-soap was a by-product of every textile-cleaning operation 
using these two substances together (documented in an Egyptian administrative text of c. 210 
BCE specifying castor oil and natron for flax bleaching; cited in the history of soap-making 
literature as the earliest Egyptian cleaning agent record). In the 2023 Saqqara embalming 
workshop analysis, castor oil was confirmed specifically as the treatment agent for the head 
during mummification (Rageot et al. 2023, Nature 614: 287–293, DOI: 10.1038/s41586-022-
05663-4). One plant, present in Egypt before writing existed, deployed across medicine, hair 
care, perfumery, textile cleaning, and embalming — a pattern consistent, on the same combined 
evidence, with these uses arriving together rather than being independently discovered, though 
as with Balanites this case does not stand alone. A further dimension: Ricinus communis is 
documented as a traditional anti-fertility agent, its seed extract showing anti-implantation and 
estrogenic activity in animal studies (Chouhan et al. 2021, ‘Medicinal Properties of Ricinus 
communis: A Review,’ International Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences and Research 12(7): 
3632–3642, DOI: 10.13040/IJPSR.0975-8232.12(7).3632-42; Brahmi et al. 2025, Diseases 
13(5): 160, DOI: 10.3390/diseases13050160). Balanites aegyptiaca is likewise recorded as a 
traditional contraceptive (Chothani and Vaghasiya 2011, Pharmacognosy Reviews 5(9): 55–62, 
DOI: 10.4103/0973-7847.79100). These are two confirmed pre-unification corridor plants 
independently used in reproductive-health management by corridor communities today — the 
pastoral population that understood both as soap and medicine also understood their 
gynaecological applications.
Lawsonia inermis (henna) — confirmed pre-unification at HK43 via a physical hair specimen 
with macroscopic dye identification and controlled replication (Fletcher 1998), c. 3400 BCE — 
carries the same multi-domain profile. It is documented in the Ebers Papyrus as an astringent 
that closes wounds and stops diarrhoea. It was historically applied to the hands and feet to 
protect against fungal pathogens and to hair to combat lice and dandruff (Semwal, R.B.K. et al. 
2014, ‘Lawsonia inermis: ethnobotanical, phytochemical and pharmacological aspects,’ Journal 
of Ethnopharmacology 155(1): 80–103, DOI: 10.1016/j.jep.2014.05.035). The Cyprinum 
perfume — one of the most widely documented ancient Egyptian perfume formulas — was 
based upon the scent of henna (Lawsonia inermis) combined with cardamom, cinnamon, myrrh, 
and southernwood (Manniche 1999, Sacred Luxuries, Cornell University Press). Henna was 
used as a mummification material: one 11th Dynasty mummy showed fingernails tinted with 
henna; an 18th Dynasty mummy displayed reddish-dyed hair (Lucas, A. and Harris, J.R. 1962, 
Ancient Egyptian Materials and Industries, 4th ed., Edward Arnold, London). For a pastoral 
population living in close proximity to animals in hot desert conditions, henna’s antimicrobial 
properties — confirmed against fungal pathogens, lice, and bacteria — would have been among 
its most practically important applications, predating any cosmetic or ritual use.
Commiphora africana (myrrh resin) — Chemically Attested pre-unification, with the Jones et al. 
2014 (PLOS ONE, PMC4132097) aromatic plant extract at Mostagedda representing the 
earliest possible evidence — is confirmed across the same six domains. It is the bridge plant 
that crosses from medical to ritual in the Salt Papyrus 825 (Ra’s tears becoming myrrh). It is the 
core of the Mendesian perfume formula. It is confirmed in the Saqqara mummification workshop 
vessels. And the Jones et al. 2014 Mostagedda study — already cited in this paper for the 
Commiphora compound-class identification — contains a finding whose full significance has not 
previously been noted: the embalming agents identified in the Badarian and Predynastic 
funerary wrappings (c. 4500–3350 BCE) constitute ‘complex, processed recipes of the same 
natural products, in similar proportions, as those utilized at the zenith of Pharaonic 
mummification some 3,000 years later’ (Jones et al. 2014, PLOS ONE 9: e103608, DOI: 
10.1371/journal.pone.0103608). The evidence is consistent with the hypothesis that this recipe 
arrived as an integrated system rather than being assembled incrementally within Egypt. A pre-
literate pastoral population was already wrapping its dead in complex multi-ingredient aromatic 
preservative recipes before Egypt was Egypt, and those recipes were transmitted with sufficient 
fidelity that 3,000 years of Pharaonic practice did not substantially alter them. On the 
transmission model, this is the corridor visible in the funerary domain: not a gradual 
development, but an inherited practice carried intact to the Nile. Jones et al. (2018, Journal of 
Archaeological Science 100: 191–200, DOI: 10.1016/j.jas.2018.07.011) confirmed this finding 
on a second independent site and an intact mummy. Turin Mummy S. 293 (Egyptian Museum 
Turin), radiocarbon-dated to 3635–3380 cal BCE (Naqada IA–IIB, at 95.4% confidence), was 
chemically analysed by GC-MS and found to contain the same embalming recipe: plant oil as 
the dominant component, with conifer resin (heated, confirmed by methyl esters indicating 
processing), aromatic plant extract, and plant gum/sugar. The Turin mummy originates from 
Upper Egypt (Gebelein/Qena/Luxor area) — a different region from Mostagedda in Middle 
Egypt. The same recipe, at the same proportions, in two independent Upper Egyptian sites, at 
3700–3350 BCE. The paper’s conclusion states explicitly: ‘this recipe contained antibacterial 
agents, used in similar proportions to those employed by the Egyptian embalmers when their 
skill was at its peak, some 2500 years later’ (Buckley, in Jones et al. 2018). The geographic 
spread confirms what the Mostagedda evidence alone could not: the formative embalming 
recipe was not a localised practice but pan-Upper Egyptian at a time when, as Jones et al. 2018 
note, ‘the concept of a pan-Egyptian identity was supposedly still developing.’ The recipe 
preceded the state that institutionalised it. Critically, Jones et al. (2018, p. 198) flag an 
unanalysed parallel at the corridor’s integration node: ‘This may reflect the reported early use of 
layers of linen impregnated with a “resinous” substance wrapped around the back of the head, 
jaw and hands in particular at Hierakonpolis, Cemetery HK43. Biochemical analyses have not 
been carried out, hence the nature and composition of these residues at Hierakonpolis remain 
unknown (Friedman et al. 2002; Jones 2007).’ The corridor plants already physically 
documented at HK43 — castor oil (4000 BCE), henna (3400 BCE), Balanites (Burial 333 
basket) — are the candidate organic residues for exactly this unanalysed resinous substance. 
GC-MS analysis of the HK43 resin-soaked linen would test whether the embalming recipe 
documented at Mostagedda and confirmed at Turin was also operating at the corridor’s 
destination node before any of the surviving papyri were written.
Moringa oleifera (Old Kingdom corridor arrival, c. 2700–2181 BCE) completes the picture. It 
became the second primary perfume carrier oil alongside Balanites, and was valued as a luxury 
hair conditioning and skin treatment oil — documented in New Kingdom contexts as a luxury oil 
— residue analysis of New Kingdom jars has identified Moringa (baq oil) alongside balanos and 
castor oil, and it appears in royal perfume and unguent inventories as a prestige product 
(Manniche 1999, Sacred Luxuries). Its arrival in the Old Kingdom, immediately after unification, 
follows the same pattern as the pre-unification corridor plants: non-native to Egypt, corridor-
distributed, arriving with established multi-domain applications. The gynaecological corpus of 
the Egyptian medical papyri — the Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus (c. 1825 BCE), the Ebers 
Papyrus gynaecology section (rubric 783), and the Ramesseum Papyrus IV (c. 1700 BCE) — 
constitutes a seventh institutional domain for the corridor botanical suite, one not previously 
identified in this paper. The Ebers Papyrus rubric 783 prescribes a contraceptive suppository of 
acacia tips, dates, and honey: the acacia ferments to produce lactic acid anhydride, a confirmed 
spermicidal agent still used in modern contraceptive preparations (Riddle 1992; Thomas et al. 
2020, Contraception: X 2: 100031). Citrullus colocynthis — confirmed pre-unification corridor 
plant from c. 3800 BCE, physically present in the same pastoral assemblage at Uan Muhuggiag 
alongside Balanites — is the active abortifacient ingredient in the Ebers contraceptive formula; 
its abortifacient properties are documented in the pharmacological literature and still observed in 
traditional Arabic medicine (Dehghani et al. 2008, ‘Toxic effects of hydroalcoholic extract of 
Citrullus colocynthis on pregnant mice,’ Iranian Journal of Veterinary Research 9(1): 42–45). 
The pastoral logic is direct: a population managing reproductive health in remote field conditions 
without institutional medicine would carry gynaecological plant knowledge with the same 
urgency as wound care, parasite control, and water purification. The same Citrullus colocynthis 
seeds physically present in Egyptian contexts from 3800 BCE were carrying both their 
documented purgative and anti-inflammatory properties AND their abortifacient and anti-fertility 
properties. Both arrived at the Nile simultaneously, in the same seeds, with the same 
population. The Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus — the oldest surviving intact medical papyrus 
and the most purely rational of all the corpus — deploys corridor plants for their most intimate 
pastoral applications. On this hypothesis, the gynaecological corpus was transmitted alongside 
the medical, aromatic, and hygienic corpus because it came from the same source.
The structural argument this evidence supports is the following. A pastoral population living with 
cattle, camels, sheep, and goats in hot Sahelian desert conditions requires an integrated body-
maintenance system covering wound care, parasite control, skin protection, odour 
management, hair hygiene, textile cleaning, and funerary preservation. Every one of these 
functions maps onto the corridor botanical suite. Balanites cleaned. Henna protected against 
parasites and fungi. Castor oil conditioned hair and treated skin. Commiphora preserved bodies 
and masked odour. Considered plant by plant, each of these applications could in principle have 
been discovered independently within Egypt — ethnobotanical convergence, not transmission, 
is the default explanation for any single multi-use plant. What favours transmission here is the 
combination of the three lines of evidence set out at the start of this section: the use pattern 
documented plant by plant above and mapped in Table 5 below; the linguistic pattern, the 
cognate adr- root persisting across six corridor populations rather than being independently 
coined at each (a fossil of source-population coherence, though it does not itself persist into 
ancient Egyptian; see Section 7); and the ancestry pattern, partially met: Salem et al. (2025) 
document deep Saharan lineage continuity from Taforalt through Takarkori, whose modern 
echoes split between a southward Fulani branch (D'Atanasio et al. 2023) and a 
Maghreb/western-Sahara branch carried by Saharawi populations (Serra-Vidal et al. 2019) 
ethnolinguistically continuous with the Hassaniya-speaking population that uses these plants 
today — the geographically relevant branch, though not yet directly tested against it. On the 
transmission hypothesis, when that population arrived at the Nile and was absorbed into the 
Dynastic state, the unified knowledge system was disaggregated into separate institutional 
domains: the medical papyri, the perfume industry, the embalming workshops, the cosmetic 
traditions. Modern scholars have studied each of these domains independently, which is 
precisely what this disaggregation would produce if it occurred 5,000 years ago: the disciplines 
that study each domain today reflect the same separation. 
The Heka encoding model proposed in this paper explains not just the medical corpus inversion 
but the simultaneous appearance of the same plants across every Egyptian personal care 
domain: on the combined linguistic, ancestry, and use evidence, this pattern is consistent with a 
unified knowledge system arriving intact, more so than with convergent independent discovery 
in each domain — though the latter cannot be excluded for any single plant considered alone, 
and the supporting evidence has real limits: the genetic continuity Salem et al. (2025) document 
connects Takarkori to Taforalt, whose modern echo in the Maghreb/western Sahara (Saharawi 
populations; Serra-Vidal et al. 2019) is ethnolinguistically continuous with, but not yet directly 
tested against, the specific Mauritanian population documented botanically here, and the adr- 
root does not itself bridge into ancient Egyptian (Section 7). The multi-domain pattern is offered 
as evidence to be weighed alongside the paper’s other pillars, not as decisive on its own. The 
six broad institutional categories — medicine, gynaecology, hair care, perfumery, 
soap/hygiene/textile care, and embalming — spanning sixteen distinct clinical and cultural 
subdomains mapped in Table 5 below (medicine alone accounting for eleven of the sixteen), 
are, on this model, the disaggregated fragments of a single pastoral body-knowledge tradition. 
The Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus (Section 1) is gynaecological in focus because reproductive 
health management was the most urgent priority for a pastoral population whose survival 
depended on birth spacing, obstetric safety, and post-partum recovery in remote field 
conditions. On this hypothesis, it deploys corridor plants because those were the plants the 
population brought with them, already understood in their full gynaecological applications, 
before writing existed, before the state existed, and before any institutional mechanism could 
account for their presence in the Nile Valley.

The Book of Hearts and the Pre-Unification Timestamp. The most significant single finding to 
emerge from the domain analysis is the date of the source material for the Ebers Papyrus Book 
of Hearts. The Ebers Papyrus is not a unified composition: it is explicitly a compilation from 
earlier temple sources. The papyrus itself opens with the statement “I came forth from On 
(Heliopolis)” followed immediately by “I came forth from Sais,” indicating that the compiler 
extracted material from the medical libraries of multiple temple institutions (Withington 1892; 
Nunn 1996). The papyrus contains an internal self-dating statement of particular significance: 
paragraph 856a states that “the book of driving wekhedu from all the limbs of a man was found 
in writings under the two feet of Anubis in Letopolis and was brought to the majesty of the king 
of Upper and Lower Egypt Den” — a reference to the First Dynasty king Den (c. 2950 BCE), 
which Egyptologists identify as a historic anachronism placing the origin of at least some 
sections of the Ebers material closer to c. 3000 BCE (Nunn 1996, Ancient Egyptian Medicine, 
British Museum Press, p. 31, citing the wekhedu-text passage directly; Grundriss der Medizin 
der alten Ägypter, von Deines, Grapow, and Westendorf 1954–1973). The American Journal of 
Psychiatry citation of the Ebers Papyrus (Bou Khalil and Richa 2014, American Journal of 
Psychiatry 171) also explicitly states that the Book of Hearts constitutes “a collection of extracts 
from different existing sources dating as far back as 3400 BCE.” This broader dating — which 
extends the compilation window to 3400 BCE — reflects the general scholarly recognition that 
the Ebers sources predate the c. 1550 BCE copy date by a substantial margin, possibly 
reaching into the predynastic period. The Ebers Papyrus is in this respect directly analogous to 
the Edfu Building Texts: a New Kingdom document that explicitly preserves, copies, and 
attributes its content to much older sources, with internal referential markers that allow partial 
reconstruction of those earlier source dates. 
The Book of Hearts covers cardiovascular function, depression, and dementia — and its 
corridor botanical prescriptions include Commiphora (headache and mood), castor oil, and 
henna. At the 3400 BCE horizon, henna is physically confirmed at Hierakonpolis (HK43 infrared 
identification, c. 3400 BCE). Colocynth is in the Naqada assemblages from 3800 BCE. Castor 
oil is in Egyptian tombs from 4000 BCE. The knowledge in the Book of Hearts and the physical 
presence of its botanical ingredients are contemporaneous at the pre-unification level. On this 
hypothesis, the written Book of Hearts is a 1550 BCE transcription of a knowledge system that 
was already operating when the corridor population reached the Nile — a reading consistent 
with both the internal self-dating evidence of the papyrus itself and the archaeobotanical record 
of the corridor plants it documents.

10. Predictions
The following predictions test the corridor-transmission hypothesis against evidence, existing or 
not yet gathered. Most draw on findings already established elsewhere in this paper, restated 
here as discrete, individually falsifiable claims rather than as part of the continuous argument; a 
smaller number are genuine forecasts — specified precisely enough that a future test could 
return an answer this paper’s own model did not assume. Each prediction states its 
discriminating weight: how much a positive or negative result would actually move confidence in 
the corridor model, as distinct from merely being consistent with it either way.
Prediction 1
Knowledge source identified as pastoral. Confirmed: Haimov-Kochman et al. (2005, EJOG 
123(1)): ‘obtained probably by herders of domesticated animals.’ This is independent 
confirmation: a reproductive-medicine paper, unconnected to this hypothesis or to Egyptology, 
reaches the same conclusion from its own evidence. The underlying mechanism and the 
pastoralist knowledge-pattern are independently corroborated by 2018–2022 veterinary-science 
and ethnoveterinary literature (Section 4). High discriminating weight.
Prediction 2
Systematic palaeopathological analysis of the Messak Settafet and Nabta Playa faunal 
assemblages, using the methodology of Van Neer et al. (2015) at Hierakonpolis, would identify 
healed fractures in domestic cattle consistent with sustained veterinary intervention. The 
assemblages exist and are confirmed adequately preserved (di Lernia et al. 2013, PLOS ONE, 
e56879, for Messak Settafet; Wendorf and Schild 1998 for Nabta Playa cattle remains; di Lernia 
et al. 2024, PLOS ONE, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0310739, confirms comparable bone 
preservation at Takarkori) but have not yet been examined for this specific evidence. This test 
requires no new excavation, only laboratory analysis of existing specimens. High discriminating 
weight.
Prediction 3
Corridor plants with pre-unification physical or chemical evidence in Egypt. Confirmed physical 
or chemical evidence (7): Castor oil (Egyptian tombs 4000 BCE), Henna/Lawsonia (HK43 Burial 
16 physical hair specimen, Fletcher 1998, c. 3400 BCE), Colocynth (Naqada seeds 3800 BCE), 
Balanites (HK43 Burial 333, 3600 BCE), Doum palm (Predynastic tombs), Senna (Abydos 
herbal wine residue 3150 BCE, compound-class level, McGovern et al. 2009 note identification 
‘not yet conclusive’), Commiphora africana (aromatic plant extract consistent with Commiphora 
identified at compound-class level in Badarian/Naqada period wrappings at Mostagedda, Jones 
et al. 2014, PLOS ONE; Chemically Attested, not Confirmed, since species-specific 
archaeobotanical identification at a named pre-unification site is still pending). Old Kingdom 
corridor arrivals with transmission inference (2) and late post-unification trade arrivals (3) 
complete the inventory (full plant names, dates, and evidentiary detail in Section 6). These are 
small counts drawn from a denominator of 21 pre-unification taxa, and no null expectation for 
corridor-distributed representation has been independently calculated; the discriminating weight 
below reflects direct evidentiary strength, not a statistically tested departure from chance (see 
Section 6 for the full caveat). High discriminating weight.
Prediction 4
Medical evidence at Hierakonpolis in predynastic window. Confirmed: HK43 fracture healing; 
Mills (1992) antibiotic grain storage. High discriminating weight.
Prediction 5
Formal physician tradition begins at unification. Confirmed: Bestetti et al. (2024): shamans → 
Sem priests → Priests of Sekhmet. Moderate discriminating weight.
Prediction 6
Cult presence at Sais in the predynastic period. Confirmed: Naqada II epigraphic attestation (c. 
3600–3350 BCE) of Neith worship at Sais, western Nile Delta. The later tradition of Sais as a 
dedicated medical teaching centre for obstetrics and gynaecology is well documented from the 
Middle Kingdom onward but is not independently confirmed for the predynastic period itself (see 
Table S2); this prediction is therefore scoped to the confirmed cult presence, which establishes 
Sais as the Lower Egyptian destination point of the corridor's geographic range. Moderate 
discriminating weight.
Prediction 7
A systematic survey of surviving Pastoral Period rock art panels across the corridor, using the 
player-posture identification methodology of Kumbani and Díaz-Andreu (2024, ‘The art of music: 
the representation of musical instruments in the rock art of Zimbabwe,’ Azania: Archaeological 
Research in Africa, DOI: 10.1080/0067270X.2024.2415211), would identify lute-type instrument 
depictions at one or more corridor nodes. No such survey has been conducted to date; the 
existing record — the Tassili dance scene (Lhote 1958) and the multi-period Ti-n-Anneuin 
assemblage before its 2009 destruction — has not been examined specifically for instrument 
content. Moderate discriminating weight.
Prediction 8
If the cosmological naming logic documented for Commiphora africana (um el barka, ‘mother of 
blessing’) reflects a genuine transmitted pattern rather than an isolated case, the same 
umm-/mother-construction should appear in the corridor vernacular names of other corridor 
plants — Balanites aegyptiaca, Ricinus communis, or Lawsonia inermis — documented in the 
existing ethnobotanical literature (Burkill 1985 and successors), and where present, should 
correspond to an independent divine-origin narrative for that plant in Egyptian religious texts. 
This is desk-testable, requiring no new fieldwork, only systematic cross-referencing of existing 
vernacular-name catalogues against Egyptian mythological sources. Low–Moderate 
discriminating weight (contextual, not load-bearing; see Section 7 epistemic scope).
Prediction 9
If the corridor's shared application-domain pattern — the same plant used for the same 
conditions across the corridor and in Egypt — reflects genuine knowledge transmission rather 
than convergent independent discovery, the specific-application matching documented for 
Commiphora africana between the Sudanese pharmacopeia and the Egyptian medical corpus 
(inhalation, wound treatment, oral health) should extend to the other six corridor plants when 
checked systematically against existing Sudanese ethnobotanical surveys (University of 
Khartoum 2025 and comparable sources). This is desk-testable using existing literature. Low–
Moderate discriminating weight (contextual; see Section 7 epistemic scope).
Prediction 10
Sekhmet associated with Lower Egypt. Confirmed: Coffin Texts. Moderate discriminating 
weight.
Outstanding Testable Prediction
The following prediction specifies the fieldwork that would most directly test the corridor model 
at its western terminus.
Prediction 11
If future palynological analysis or phytolith extraction from the Holocene playa lake deposits of 
the Richat Structure’s central depression identifies Commiphora pollen or plant silica bodies 
dated to 6,000–3,000 BCE, this would confirm Commiphora’s presence in the Richat zone 
during the proposed pastoral migration window. If organic residue analysis (GC-MS) of 
Holocene pastoral pottery from the Richat area identifies Commiphora terpenoid signatures, this 
would constitute direct evidence of harvesting and use by pastoralists. A positive result on either 
test would establish the Mauritanian confidence class for pre-3000 BCE knowledge as High, 
resolving its current Untested status; a negative result, replicated across multiple sampling sites, 
would weaken the case for a Richat-specific origin without falsifying the broader corridor model 
(Section 11). This is the paper’s highest-priority recommendation for future fieldwork. Moderate 
discriminating weight.
This prediction identifies the fieldwork target at the corridor’s western terminus. Analogous 
palynological and archaeobotanical survey predictions apply across all corridor nodes during the 
proposed migration window (3800–3100 BCE): at Tassili n’Ajjer, direct archaeobotanical 
sampling of the pastoral occupation layers; at Takarkori, targeted residue analysis of additional 
pastoral pottery contemporaneous with the Salem et al. 2025 genome individuals; and at Nabta 
Playa and Gilf Kebir, survey for botanical macro-remains at documented pastoral campsites. 
The Richat zone is currently the highest-priority target due to the absence of any existing 
botanical survey, but the prediction framework applies westward to eastward across the full 
corridor.
11. Alternative Explanations
The state-invention model does not straightforwardly account for the inversion. If medicine were 
invented by the unified Dynastic state, the expected trajectory would show increasing 
sophistication over time; the documented trajectory runs in the opposite direction. The Ebers 
Papyrus’s own internal evidence (copied from older rational sources) contradicts this account 
from within.
Independent Nilotic development is possible but does not fully account for the botanical 
distribution under current evidence. Thirteen plants with North African corridor-zone distribution 
appear in the oldest Egyptian medical texts. Six are documented in the contemporary 
Mauritanian pharmacopeia. Commiphora was not native to Egypt. The doum palm’s distribution 
runs from Mauritania to Egypt. On its own, ecological co-occurrence does not distinguish 
independent discovery from transmission — discovering a locally available plant’s uses is a 
normal process available to any population living in its range, not evidence unique to this 
hypothesis. What discriminates between them here is the discriminating-test framework applied 
in Section 9: the cognate adr- root persisting across six corridor populations (Section 7) and the 
deep Saharan lineage continuity Salem et al. (2025) document from Taforalt through Takarkori, 
whose Maghreb/western-Sahara branch (Saharawi populations; Serra-Vidal et al. 2019) is 
ethnolinguistically continuous with the Mauritanian population documented botanically here, 
though not yet directly tested against it (Section 9). Independent Nilotic development offers no 
account of why the corridor population itself is linguistically coherent as a single group along this 
exact route, or why an isolated Saharan genetic lineage persisted along that same route at all 
— a coherence that does not bear on Egypt directly, but is the necessary premise for a 
population able to carry knowledge there at all, and one that a purely local Nilotic development 
has no reason to predict.
Mesopotamian transmission is not consistent with the North African botanical distribution 
documented in the oldest Egyptian texts. The plants in the oldest Egyptian texts are African in 
distribution. Current documentation of the Kahun, Ramesseum, and Edwin Smith plant lists 
shows no Mesopotamian botanical correspondence; this absence reflects the current state of 
the comparative record and is not itself treated as falsification (Axiom 5).
The pre-3150 BCE archaeobotanical evidence — plants documented before any state-level 
trade network — creates a chronological problem for the trade-route model, although absence 
of documented networks before that date does not rule out informal exchange mechanisms. 
Castor oil in tombs at 4000 BCE and colocynth seeds at Naqada at 3800 BCE predate any 
documented North African trade network at the required scale.
The Equifinality Matrix below makes these alternatives explicit for each class of evidence and 
specifies what discriminating evidence would look like. Quantitative thresholds and specific test 
conditions for the discriminating evidence are detailed in Section 10 (Predictions); the matrix 
summarises the types of evidence that would favour each explanation. The purpose is to 
demonstrate that the transmission hypothesis is preferred not merely because it is consistent 
with the evidence, but because the alternatives face specific evidential problems that 
transmission does not.
1. Pre-papyrus plants in Egypt 4000–3000 BCE (castor, colocynth, probable senna)

A. Independent invention: Plausible. Nile valley populations could have independently 
identified medicinal uses.

B. Saharan trade network: Possible for some. Predynastic long-distance exchange 
networks are archaeologically documented: baboons from the Horn of Africa appear at 
Hierakonpolis by c. 3700 BCE (Van Neer et al. 2004); the glyptic continuum documents 
elite exchange across Egypt, A-Group Nubia, and southern Palestine from Naqada IId 
onward (Hill 2016). These networks moved high-value prestige goods through elite 
acquisition channels. However, a multi-plant medicinal suite of pre-unification corridor 
species with consistent pastoral applications across the corridor is not a prestige import 
pattern. Trade is not ruled out for individual species but is less parsimonious than 
population movement for the full suite.

C. Corridor transmission: Consistent. Plants arrive with population during 3800–3100 
BCE Shemsu Hor window, preceding state formation.

Discriminating evidence: Naqada I–III sites with multiple corridor plants together in a 
single context (e.g., three or more taxa in the same tomb or occupational layer). Multi-
plant suites support C; single-plant finds or dispersed occurrences across different sites 
and periods support A or B.
2. Commiphora africana: non-native to Egypt, Sahel-zone origin, in oldest rational texts

A. Independent invention: Weak. Commiphora does not grow in Egypt; Egyptian 
populations could not independently discover a plant unavailable locally.

B. Saharan trade network: Possible. Long-distance resin trade is documented in the 
ancient Near East.

C. Corridor transmission: Consistent and strong. Non-native plant in oldest rational 
texts; carried by a population traversing its habitat zone over generations.

Discriminating evidence: Pre-3000 BCE resin trade documentation in the Nile Valley 
(e.g., Commiphora resin in a securely dated pre-3100 BCE context outside the corridor's 
known pastoral sites). If trade predates the corridor integration window, B gains support 
over C.
3. Seven corridor plants in Mauritanian pharmacopeia (Yebouk 2020, 2026; Boune 2025; 
Thouzery 1997)

A. Independent invention: Consistent. Saharan plants used across a wide range by 
many populations independently.

B. Saharan trade network: Consistent. Trading populations would retain knowledge of 
traded goods.

C. Corridor transmission: Consistent. TEK continuity in a pastoral population that did 
not migrate east retains the original corridor pharmacopeia.

Discriminating evidence: Palynological or residue evidence of the same plant suite at 
Holocene Richat-zone sites (Prediction 11). Shared suite across western and eastern 
corridor nodes discriminates C from A.
4. Chronological inversion: oldest texts most rational

A. Independent invention: Unexplained. State-invention model predicts increasing 
sophistication; documented trajectory runs opposite.

B. Saharan trade network: Does not address. Trade transmits goods, not knowledge 
systems; cannot explain the inversion.

C. Corridor transmission: Explains. Inherited pre-state empirical knowledge acquired 
ritual framing as state theology absorbed it over centuries.

D. Differential preservation: Partially addresses. Reeves (2008) proposes that rational 
texts were preserved as scribal curriculum while magical texts survived in less 
accessible temple contexts, which could produce an apparent inversion without any real 
change in the underlying tradition over time. This does not fully account for the pattern: 
Chester Beatty VI (c. 1200 BCE) is purely rational, showing rational-register composition 
continued into the Ramesside period rather than being confined to the earliest texts, 
which a pure preservation-bias account does not predict. A single counterexample does 
not disprove a general preservation bias, but it weakens the claim that the surviving 
rational texts are simply an early-period sample. This is consistent with a broader pattern 
documented independently of the rational/magical prescription count used here: 
Pommerening’s (2020) genre-wide classification of Egyptian medical “teaching texts” 
(Lehrtexte), based on internal patterns of reasoning rather than the rational/magical 
distinction, traces this rational-register composition tradition from the 5th/6th Dynasty (c. 
2400 BCE) through the Second Intermediate Period and early New Kingdom (c. 1550 
BCE), with later instances of the genre — including Roman Period copies — tracing 
back to earlier compositional dates: evidence that rational-register composition was an 
ongoing scribal tradition across more than 2,000 years, not an artefact confined to the 
earliest surviving texts. This is a different genre classification than the prescription-level 
rational/magical count central to this paper’s argument, so it does not itself settle the 
inversion question, but it independently weakens the premise that rational composition 
was a one-off early phenomenon later overtaken by magical framing.

Discriminating evidence: Systematic magical-content classification using Grapow, 
Ritner, and this paper's count across the full papyrus corpus. If inversion disappears 
under alternative classification, the anomaly is reduced. A systematic survey of rational-
register composition dates across the full corpus, rather than reliance on single 
counterexamples like Chester Beatty VI, would further discriminate C from D. 
Pommerening’s (2014, 2020) teaching-text genre classification is a step in this direction, 
though it classifies compositional reasoning patterns rather than the rational/magical 
prescription distinction itself, so a corpus-wide survey using that specific distinction 
remains the more direct test.
5. Pastoral knowledge source: Haimov-Kochman (2005) independently identifies herders as 
probable source

A. Independent invention: Does not address. The study identifies herders as source, 
not indigenous Egyptian inventors.

B. Saharan trade network: Partial. Trade implies herder contact, but trade does not 
require herder migration into Egypt.

C. Corridor transmission: Consistent and convergent. Pastoral population is both the 
knowledge source (Haimov-Kochman) and the proposed migration vector (this paper).

Discriminating evidence: aDNA evidence of Saharan corridor ancestry at 
Hierakonpolis pastoral contexts. Salem et al. (2025) provides the Takarkori anchor; 
Hierakonpolis aDNA would close the corridor-to-destination link.
Conclusion: For each class of evidence, the matrix shows what alternative explanations 
predict, where they face evidential problems, and what discriminating evidence would decide 
between them. The transmission hypothesis (C) is preferred not because it is merely consistent 
with the evidence, but because it faces fewer unresolved evidential problems across the full 
evidence set. No single column defeats the others; the case is cumulative.
12. Falsification Conditions
1. Evidence that the rational medical tradition first appears in Egypt after unification (c. 3100 
BCE) with no archaeobotanical or textual antecedents in the Naqada period. If systematic 
survey of Naqada I–III contexts (c. 4400–3100 BCE) yields no medicinal plant residues and no 
pre-unification medical instruments or contexts, the hypothesis that the pharmacopeia predates 
the state is weakened. The current archaeobotanical record (castor oil 4000 BCE, colocynth 
3800 BCE, senna 3150 BCE) constitutes the evidence to be overturned.
2. Egyptian magical medical texts predating the Edwin Smith source text (c. 3000 BCE). If such 
texts were discovered, they would directly contradict the claim that the oldest surviving medical 
texts are the most rational, breaking the inversion pattern documented in Table 1 at its oldest 
point. The absence of such texts is not claimed as confirmation of the hypothesis—it is a 
boundary condition: the hypothesis predicts that the oldest written record would prioritise 
botanical content over ritual framing, but the oral incantation tradition is expected to have 
existed alongside it. The falsification condition tests the written record only.
3. Systematic palaeobotanical evidence demonstrating that the pre-unification corridor plants 
documented in this paper were not available to predynastic Naqada populations via ecological 
continuity with the corridor zone. The Tassili and Takarkori evidence already contradicts this for 
three of the confirmed pre-unification corridor taxa.
4. Evidence that Neith cult presence at Sais was established substantially after the Naqada II 
period (c. 3600–3350 BCE), which would undermine Sais's role as the corridor's Lower Egyptian 
destination point. This condition tests cult presence only; the later medical-institution tradition at 
Sais is not claimed as a predynastic fact and is therefore not a target of this falsification 
condition (see Table S2).
5. Ancient DNA evidence demonstrating that the predynastic Hierakonpolis population had no 
Saharan corridor ancestry component — specifically, Fst differentiation greater than 0.05 
between Hierakonpolis samples and the Takarkori-anchored Saharan population (Salem et al. 
2025), or evidence that Hierakonpolis derives entirely from Levantine/Nilotic sources with no 
Saharan ancestry component at the 95% confidence threshold of available aDNA methods. This 
condition is testable with future archaeogenomic analysis of predynastic Hierakonpolis skeletal 
material. 
6. Evidence that the corridor plants documented here were present in Egypt before the Shemsu 
Hor window (c. 3800 BCE) in non-pastoral, non-corridor contexts — for example, settled Nile 
Valley agricultural sites with no pastoralist material culture. Specifically: if Ricinus communis, 
Balanites aegyptiaca, Citrullus colocynthis, Lawsonia inermis, or Senna italica are confirmed in 
such contexts before 3800 BCE, the hypothesis requires revision. (Hyphaene thebaica is 
excluded, since its eastern Sahelian range already extends into Egypt; its early presence would 
not falsify the hypothesis for that plant.) A separate, timing-independent condition: if any corridor 
plant is confirmed native to Lower Egypt or the Nile Delta specifically — not Upper Egypt or 
Nubia, the corridor's own southern terminus — its presence is adequately explained by local 
availability and needs no transmission account. Senna demonstrates the principle already: 
Senna alexandrina is native to Upper Egypt and Nubia, not the Delta, which is why it keeps its 
corridor classification. This would weaken, not globally falsify, the hypothesis — proportionally, 
depending on how many plants turned out to be Delta-native.
7. Systematic palaeopathological analysis of the Messak Settafet and Nabta Playa faunal 
assemblages, conducted using the methodology of Van Neer et al. (2015), finding no healed 
fractures or other evidence of sustained veterinary care in the cattle remains. This would 
weaken the claim that corridor pastoralists practised the same sustained-care tradition later 
institutionalised at Hierakonpolis (Prediction 2), though it would not by itself falsify the broader 
corridor model, since absence of healed fractures in a sample is also consistent with a smaller 
or differently composed herd.
8. A systematic survey of surviving Pastoral Period rock art across multiple corridor nodes, 
using the methodology of Kumbani and Díaz-Andreu (2024), finding no lute-type instrument 
depictions despite adequate preservation and coverage. This would weaken the claim of deep 
pastoral musical-transmission continuity (Prediction 7); it would not fully falsify the claim given 
the documented destruction of the Ti-n-Anneuin assemblage and the general under-survey of 
the corpus for this specific content (Section 7).
9. Systematic cross-referencing of existing corridor ethnobotanical literature finding that the 
umm-/mother-naming construction and the Sudan–Egypt application-domain matching 
documented for Commiphora africana do not extend to the other corridor plants (Predictions 8 
and 9). This would weaken the reading of these patterns as evidence of a shared cultural-
cosmological framework, in favour of Commiphora being an isolated case; given this evidence is 
already scoped as contextual rather than load-bearing (Section 7), a negative result would not 
affect the paper’s primary evidentiary pillars.
10. Evidence that the same multi-domain pattern documented in Section 9 — a single plant’s 
simultaneous use across medicine, perfumery, hygiene, and embalming — is equally present in 
other ancient pharmacopeias (Levantine, Mesopotamian, or other non-corridor traditions) for 
plants with no corridor connection. If multi-domain use is common wherever a chemically 
versatile plant is available, regardless of transmission history, this would favour the 
ethnobotanical convergence explanation (Garnatje et al. 2017; Teixidor-Toneu et al. 2018) over 
the ‘unified knowledge system arriving intact’ reading, weakening Section 9’s central argument 
specifically.
This paper opened with an anomaly — Egypt's oldest medical texts are its most rational, and 
every later century adds more ritual, not less. The explanation proposed here is that Egypt did 
not grow more superstitious over time; it grew further from a pastoral pharmacopeia it inherited 
rather than invented, re-narrating an old knowledge system as its origin receded from memory. 
The evidence for where that knowledge came from converges across botany, genetics, 
language, and institution — but convergence is not proof. What would move this from 
hypothesis to finding is a single, specific, unglamorous act: a soil sample from the Richat 
Structure's own wadi beds, tested for pollen that has never been looked for. That sample has 
not yet been taken. Until it is, this remains what it was on its first page — a hypothesis, held to 
the same standard it asks of everyone else, and open to being proven wrong by exactly the kind 
of evidence it invites.
