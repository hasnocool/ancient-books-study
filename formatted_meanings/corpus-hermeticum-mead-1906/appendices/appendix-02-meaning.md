# The Encomium of Kings — Section-by-Section Meaning Guide

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), supplementary text, “About the Soul’s Being Hindered by the Passion of the Body.”
> **Coverage:** §§1–16 in the repository transcription.
> **Status:** Provisional explanation; not a new translation, facsimile-verified edition, or independent human review.

## Central argument

The text uses musical performance to distinguish an artist's skill from the limitations of an instrument. It applies this analogy to divine activity and bodily weakness, then turns toward praise of God and earthly kings. The encomium presents kingship as a source of peace and frames praise of rulers as subordinate to, and modeled on, praise of the highest divine King.

The political claims are the rhetoric of this particular praise discourse. They should not be treated as neutral evidence that every ruler brings peace or that royal authority is inherently just.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph explanation

### §1 — The musician and the inadequate instrument

A musician's performance can be obstructed by instruments too weak to express the music. The audience may laugh at the result, although the artist has offered the art without weariness. God is compared to the unwearied musician whose harmony reaches individual instruments.

**Meaning:** The paragraph separates the perfection of the source from the limitations of its material means. The musical analogy prepares for the text's treatment of bodily weakness.

### §2 — Do not blame the composer

The example of an ensemble shows that when an instrument fails, blame belongs to the faulty instrument rather than the composer or inspiration behind the music.

**Meaning:** The argument reinforces the distinction between originating skill and imperfect execution. It is an analogy, not a technical account of musical performance.

### §3 — The body as instrument

The audience is asked not to blame the divine Race for weakness inherent in the body. God is described as unwearied Spirit, continually giving the same benefits.

**Meaning:** Bodily limitation is distinguished from divine activity. The phrase “our Race” and the exact referent of “body” need contextual and textual checking; do not silently replace them with a modern anthropological category.

### §4 — Pheidias and the lack of matter

The Demiurge is compared to Pheidias, the sculptor, whose skill cannot be fully realized if suitable matter is unavailable. The failure is attributed to the material or instrument, not the artist.

**Meaning:** The analogy extends from music to sculpture: a maker's intention may exceed what resistant or inadequate material can express.

### §5 — Weakness can magnify the artist

The worse the instrument, the more the artist's repeated success in producing the right note displays skill. The speaker applies this to the inner lyre and the divine Musician.

**Meaning:** Limitation becomes the setting in which excellence is revealed. This is a rhetorical and spiritual analogy, not a claim that suffering or disability should be romanticized.

### §6 — The harper and the grasshopper

A story tells of a harper whose string breaks during a contest. A grasshopper settles on the lyre and supplies the missing sound, allowing the performance to continue and the harper to win.

**Meaning:** The episode dramatizes assistance arriving from beyond the performer's own resources. The “Better One” is presented as the source of the timely aid.

**Textual watchlist:** Check “necessitude,” “president of music-work,” and the bracketed “[broken]” string. The mythic allusion should not be assigned a more specific identity without evidence from the source or reliable commentary.

### §7 — The speaker resumes his song

The speaker compares his earlier confession of weakness with renewed strength received from a Superior One. He now feels able to praise the King and to celebrate royal victories.

**Meaning:** The musical metaphor becomes a description of the speaker's own rhetorical performance. Divine assistance enables the encomium to proceed.

### §8 — Praise descends from the highest King

The singer first raises himself to the highest King, the God of all, and then descends to earthly rulers who hold sceptres.

**Meaning:** The hierarchy of praise is deliberate: divine sovereignty is primary, and earthly kingship is represented as derivative. The passage also reflects the conventions of royal encomium.

### §9 — God as source of victory

God is praised as the greatest King, deathless and everlasting, from whom victories descend to later victors.

**Meaning:** Earthly victory is placed within a divine order. The paragraph establishes the theological premise for the subsequent praise of rulers.

### §10 — The earthly kings

The discourse turns to kings as presidents of common welfare and peace. Their victories are described as prepared before battle, and their presence is said to make foreign lands tremble. Five asterisks precede a parenthetical heading about blessing the Better One and praising the King.

**Meaning:** The rhetoric magnifies royal power through anticipatory victory and public order. The asterisks and parenthetical heading may indicate a division, omission, or editorial device; their exact function cannot be settled from the transcription alone.

**Textual watchlist:** Verify the five asterisks, the heading “ABOUT THE BLESSING OF THE BETTER [ONE] AND PRAISING OF THE KING,” and whether the heading is printed as part of the text or supplied editorially.

### §11 — The discourse returns to its beginning

The speaker says the discourse will join its end to its beginning by blessing the Better One. The Sun gathering the first fruits of plants with its rays becomes an image for receiving divine wisdom and directing blessings back toward their source.

**Meaning:** The paragraph describes a circular movement of gift and gratitude. Blessing comes from the divine source and is returned through the soul's cultivated response.

**Textual watchlist:** Preserve “ambrosial [essences],” “garden of our souls above the heavens,” and the bracketed “[direct and]” pending collation.

### §12 — Praise according to one's capacity

The speaker says ten thousand tongues would be fitting for God's praise, yet humans cannot express what is fully due. Newborn children provide the analogy: they cannot sing their father's glory adequately, but their limited thanks is accepted.

**Meaning:** The inability to praise God perfectly does not make praise worthless. The appropriate response is gratitude proportionate to one's capacity, while acknowledging divine greatness beyond human expression.

**Textual watchlist:** Check the bracketed “[far],” “[yet will we say what best we can],” and “[one].” Preserve the unusual phrase “what a limit means.”

### §13 — Human praise and Gnosis

People are described as children of the King and naturally inclined to praise him, while asking pardon for their insufficiency. The text then speaks of Gnosis as distributing life and the power to return blessing to God.

**Meaning:** Praise is presented as a response made possible by the very gift being praised. Gnosis is linked to life and the capacity for gratitude.

**Textual watchlist:** The phrase “the Gnosis of the all [rejoice]” is grammatically uncertain. Preserve the bracketed material and check the subject and verb against the printed page.

### §14 — Divine goodness and shared harmony

God is described as Good, deathless, and continually active. The passage says there is no partiality or difference among those united in Thought, Mind, and active Sense; Love works the harmony of all.

**Meaning:** Divine goodness is portrayed as an inexhaustible source whose gifts call forth praise. Unity is expressed through shared Mind and Love.

**Textual watchlist:** Check “that lot of that inheritance,” “for ever ever-flowing,” and the punctuation around “One is the Sense that’s active through them.” These may reflect transcription or translation difficulties.

### §15 — Praise of kings as preparation for piety

The speaker proposes descending from praise of God to praise of rulers, beginning with kings as an exercise that trains people in pious service to the Better One.

**Meaning:** Royal praise is framed as a subordinate practice that prepares the speaker and audience for divine praise. The passage reveals the encomium's political and religious purpose.

### §16 — The king as guarantor of peace

The final paragraph thanks kings for extending peace. It interprets the title “King” through the idea of leveling dissension and governing by reason in the service of peace. The king's name and statues are described as protection for those in danger.

**Meaning:** The text uses idealized royal imagery: the king's name and likeness are treated as symbols that can calm conflict and reassure citizens. This is a normative encomiastic claim, not proof of the actual conduct or effects of historical rulers.

**Textual watchlist:** The isolated “101” at the end is likely a printed page number or layout remnant, but must be checked against the page image before removal from the diplomatic transcription. Also inspect “the lord of reason (logos) that [makes] for peace” and the bracketed “[is made].”

## Textual and interpretive cautions

- Keep all bracketed readings visible until facsimile collation.
- Check the five asterisks and parenthetical heading in §10.
- Check the isolated “101” after §16 as a possible page-number artifact.
- The “Better One,” God, and the King are related through the text's hierarchy of praise; do not collapse their distinct roles.
- The musical and sculptural analogies concern the relation between source, instrument, and material limitation. They do not justify blaming bodies or treating disability as moral failure.
- The encomium's claims about kings and peace should be attributed to the speaker and genre.
