# Tractate V — Though Unmanifest, God Is Most Manifest
## Section-by-section meaning guide

**Source:** G. R. S. Mead's 1906 English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 5.
**Status:** Provisional explanation based on the repository transcription; not independently verified against the 1906 facsimile.

## Reading the tractate as a whole

The discourse asks how a reality that is not itself a visible, made object can nevertheless be known through the things it brings into manifestation. Hermes moves through three principal fields of consideration: the distinction between the Unmanifest and manifested things; the order of the heavens and the formation of the human body as signs of a maker; and a hymn that describes God as beyond names while encompassing all things.

The argument is theological and philosophical. Its references to astronomy, anatomy, and the elements belong to the conceptual language of the translated text and should not be treated as statements of modern science.

## Section guide

### 1. The Unmanifest and manifestation

Hermes distinguishes the Unmanifest, which always is, from things that become manifest. In this passage, manifestation is associated with becoming and making; the divine source is not itself a product that has come into being. It is the source that makes other things manifest.

The parenthetical gloss “thinking into manifestation” is part of the source transcription's explanatory wording. It should not be silently removed or treated as an independently established Greek equivalent. The section's central distinction is between the unmade source and the things made manifest through it.

### 2. Thought as the means of apprehension

The unmade God is beyond the process of making-manifest, yet manifests through and in all things, especially where He wills. Hermes tells Tat to pray for the ability to receive a ray of understanding. The Unmanifest is apprehended by thought rather than ordinary eyesight.

The passage also turns the inquiry inward: if Tat cannot perceive what is within himself, how can he expect to see the divine within himself by means of external eyes? “Seeing” here is contemplative or intellectual, not simply visual. The text does not say that every thought is automatically divine knowledge; Tat must become capable of apprehension.

### 3. The courses of the heavenly bodies

Hermes asks Tat to consider the sun, moon, and stars, whose courses follow distinct limits of place and number. Even the sun, described in the passage as the greatest of the heavenly gods, moves within an order that it does not itself establish.

The question “Who” marks the passage's argument from ordered motion to an ordering source. The celestial hierarchy and its language of divine beings should be read as the cosmological framework of this text, not as a modern astronomical model.

### 4. Cosmic boundaries and the maker

The Bear constellation, the turning cosmos, the sea's boundaries, and the earth's position extend the argument. Number, place, and measure imply an ordering agency. Hermes also argues that what lacks order cannot be sovereign over order; it too would be subordinate to the one who has not yet ordained it.

The transcription reads “It cound not be” and contains other archaic or potentially irregular forms. These are source-text issues for later collation, not grounds for silently rewriting the derivative. The philosophical point is that order is not self-explanatory within Hermes' argument.

### 5. The imagined view from above

Hermes invites Tat to imagine rising into the air and surveying earth, water, air, fire, the stars, and heaven's circuit together. The imagined vantage point makes the coordination of diverse motions easier to contemplate.

The key phrase is the relation between “the motionless in motion” and “the unmanifest made manifest.” The source presents the cosmos as an ordered display of a source that is not itself simply another moving object within the display. The ascent is a thought experiment, not a report of physical flight.

### 6. Formation of the human body

The focus shifts from the heavens to the developing human body. Hermes lists bodily structures and organs, asking who shapes and arranges them. The list emphasizes differentiation and coordination: many parts, each with its own form and function, are brought together in one living body.

The section uses ancient anatomical language and assumptions. It is not a reliable modern anatomical description, and the catalogue should not be used as medical or biological instruction. Its role in the argument is to direct attention to the complexity of embodied life.

### 7. Many arts applied to one material

Hermes summarizes the preceding catalogue: many arts and labors are involved in one material, producing a coordinated and varied result. He asks what mother or father could account for this work except God, described as unmanifest and acting by will.

The claim is theological: bodily formation is interpreted as evidence of divine agency. The section does not independently demonstrate the mechanism of embryological development in modern scientific terms.

### 8. The maker and the work

Hermes compares the human body with a statue or painting. People recognize that an artwork requires a sculptor or painter; he argues that a still more intricate work should not be regarded as existing without a worker.

The rebuke against blindness and ignorance expresses the urgency of the argument. God is said to exceed all names and to be the Father of all. Fatherhood here functions as a statement about divine source and production, not merely biological parenthood.

### 9. God as continual source and maker

Hermes describes God's being in terms of conceiving and making all things. He argues that nothing in the world is outside God: what exists is made manifest, while what does not exist is held in God.

This is a sweeping metaphysical claim about divine presence and dependence. It should not be flattened into the claim that every object is identical in every respect, nor should “things that are not” be assigned a precise technical meaning without further textual and scholarly investigation.

The transcription contains the phrase “its is impossible,” an apparent grammatical irregularity. It is retained here as a note about the source rather than silently corrected.

### 10. God beyond names and present through all

God is called unmanifest and most manifest, beyond bodily limitation yet visible through bodies, and both the one of no body and the one of every body. The passage says that all things are in God and from God, while no place or external standpoint can contain or surround Him.

The paradox of many names and no name is central. God has all names because all things derive from the one Father; no name is sufficient because none can encompass the source of all. The bracketed phrases “[alone]” and “[as well]” belong to the transcription and should be checked against the printed edition before any editorial decision is made.

### 11. The concluding hymn

Tat asks when and how the Father can be praised. No time, place, or standpoint lies outside the divine reality. The speaker cannot claim an independent possession or identity from which to praise God as though God were merely another being.

The sequence “Mind when Thou thinkest,” “Father when Thou makest,” and “God when Thou dost energize” describes different ways the divine activity is named. The final progression—“the subtler part of matter” as air, of air as soul, of soul as mind, and of mind as God—presents a hierarchy of refinement in the tractate's own conceptual vocabulary. It should not be recast as a modern physical theory.

## Important distinctions

- **Unmanifest is not simply absent:** the text distinguishes the source that does not become a made object from the things it brings into manifestation.
- **Manifestation is not ordinary visibility alone:** thought and contemplative apprehension are essential to the discourse.
- **Cosmic and bodily order serve as arguments:** Hermes asks Tat to infer a maker from coordination and measure.
- **The hymn uses paradox deliberately:** God is beyond names yet receives all names; beyond bodies yet present through every body.
- **The text's cosmology is not modern science:** celestial hierarchy, elemental descriptions, and anatomical language must remain in their historical textual context.

## Source-text cautions

The repository transcription has not been checked against the 1906 facsimile. Apparent irregularities include “cound” in section 4, “its is impossible” in section 9, “nome” in section 10, and the wording and punctuation of several bracketed phrases. These are recorded as collation targets, not silently emended. The phrase “thinking-manifest” and its gloss in section 1 also deserve comparison with the printed page.
