# Tractate XIV — A Letter of Thrice-Greatest Hermes to Asclepius
## Section-by-section meaning guide

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate XIV.
>
> **Editorial status:** Provisional explanation based on the repository's formatted Mead translation. It is not a new translation, facsimile-verified transcription, or independently human-reviewed edition.

## Central argument

The letter argues from the existence and diversity of made things to a source that is not itself made. Hermes names this source God, Maker, and Father, associating the names respectively with Power, Energy, and Good. The Maker and the made are presented as inseparable, although not identical in role: one makes and the other is made. The world's change and imperfections do not, in Hermes' view, disprove divine goodness. The closing image of a farmer sowing different seeds illustrates differentiated processes proceeding from one maker.

This is a theological and metaphysical argument within the text. It should not be recast as a modern scientific account of cosmology, biology, or physical causation.

## Explanation by numbered section

### §1 — Why Hermes writes

Hermes explains the occasion of the letter. Tat had pressed him for further teaching while Asclepius was absent. Hermes now condenses the chief points for Asclepius, adapting the explanation to someone he regards as older and more experienced in the study of Nature.

**Meaning:** The tractate presents itself as a selective written summary of an earlier teaching conversation. Its compressed and “more mystic” style is intentional within the framing.

**Textual note:** The source includes bracketed editorial wording such as “[natural to]” and “[teachings],” as well as “[him]” and “[of them].” These brackets should be retained unless checked against the printed edition. “mystic-ly” is an unusual hyphenated form in the transcription; do not normalize it without facsimile evidence.

### §2 — Made things point to a maker

Hermes argues that things which come into being do not make themselves. Since the visible world contains many different made things, their existence implies a maker distinct from the things made. The ultimate maker cannot itself be made, or the explanation would simply be pushed back to another maker.

**Meaning:** This is an argument about dependence and first cause. The source of the chain of made things must not belong to the same dependent category as its products.

**Interpretive caution:** The passage states a metaphysical premise; it is not an empirical demonstration in the modern scientific sense. The repeated bracketed “[if]” and “[than themselves]” are part of the transmitted wording and should be checked, not silently removed.

### §3 — The unseen source and continuous making

The Maker is described as supreme, one, and the only one with nothing more ancient than itself. It governs number, size, difference, and the continuity of making. Made things are visible, whereas the Maker is not; Hermes connects this invisibility with the Maker's perpetual activity.

**Meaning:** The text contrasts visible products with their invisible source. The learner is invited to move from observing what is made to contemplating the source that makes it.

**Interpretive caution:** “He maketh,—that He may not be able to be seen” is a striking formulation. It should be explained in the context of the passage, not converted into a claim that God deliberately hides in a literal physical sense.

### §4 — God, Maker, and Father

Hermes joins three names: God, Maker, and Father. He associates God with Power, Maker with Energy, and Father with Good. Power is distinguished from the products of making; Energy is said to consist in all things being made. The section concludes that there is no third or intermediate term between Maker and made.

**Meaning:** The three names emphasize different aspects of one divine source: capacity, activity, and goodness. Hermes wants the reader to understand the relation between source and product without multiplying independent ultimate principles.

**Textual note:** The phrase “or rather [all] the three” contains a bracketed editorial insertion. Preserve it pending comparison with the printed page.

### §5 — Maker and made are inseparable

Hermes asks the reader to remember Maker and made in everything perceived or heard. All things, whether divine, changing, obscure, above, or below, belong to this pair. Neither can exist as the term it is without the other: “Maker” implies what is made, and “made” implies a maker.

**Meaning:** The argument is relational. The names describe two sides of the act of making, not two unrelated realities. The passage's language of inseparability should not erase the distinction between the maker and what is made.

**Interpretive caution:** The claim that each is “one and the same thing” is stronger than merely saying they are related. It belongs to Hermes' particular formulation and should not be softened or systematized without textual and philosophical context.

### §6 — The Maker's self-relation and the unity of the pair

Hermes considers the Maker as Alone, Simple, and Uncompound. Its making is described as being “to Itself,” with the paradoxical formulation that the Maker's making is its being made. Created things, by contrast, cannot make themselves; they depend on another. The section then names the Maker as that which leads and the made as that which follows, while declaring the two one in union.

**Meaning:** This section explores the asymmetry between the unmade source and dependent things, while also insisting that making and what is made form one inseparable relation. Its language is deliberately paradoxical.

**Interpretive caution:** Do not turn the passage into a claim that ordinary created things literally create themselves. Hermes explicitly distinguishes the Maker's unique self-relation from the dependence of made things.

### §7 — Variety, imperfection, and change

Hermes warns against treating the variety of created things as evidence that God is low or lacking in glory. God's glory is to make all things. He compares defects that accompany becoming to rust on brass and filth on a body, which are not made by the metalworker or by those who generate a body. God, likewise, is not the maker of evil. Change is presented as a cleansing of the process of becoming.

**Meaning:** The section addresses the problem of imperfection: if God makes all things, why do decay and undesirable conditions appear? Hermes distinguishes the act of making from defects or passions that arise in the continuing process of becoming.

**Interpretive caution:** This is the tractate's theological explanation of evil and change, not a complete treatment of the problem of evil. The analogies should not be extended beyond the point they serve.

### §8 — The painter analogy and the charge of impiety

Hermes asks why a human painter can depict an enormous range of things while God would supposedly be unable to make all things. He calls it ignorance not to attribute universal making to God. To deny that capacity, he argues, implies arrogance or inability in God.

**Meaning:** The comparison is an argument from the lesser to the greater: if a limited human maker can produce representations of many kinds of things, it is incoherent, within Hermes' theology, to deny the divine Maker's universal creative power.

**Textual note:** The transcription reads “monstraus” in “what monstraus lack of understanding.” This is suspicious and likely requires checking against the 1906 page image. Do not silently replace it. The isolated “1” after “God!” may be a footnote marker or OCR artifact; verify its function before editing. The punctuation and spacing around “impiety [to think]” should also be checked.

### §9 — The Good as God's sole passion

Hermes says that God has one passion only: Good. The Good is neither arrogant nor impotent and has the power to make all things. Everything capable of being made is made by God, identified here with the Good. Hermes offers to explain the manner of this making.

**Meaning:** The section reinforces the connection between divine goodness and creative power. It rejects the idea that inability or arrogance could explain a limit on God's making.

**Interpretive caution:** “Passion” here belongs to the source's philosophical vocabulary. It should not automatically be read as an ordinary emotional impulse or as a modern psychological category.

### §10 — The farmer, the seeds, and Genesis

Hermes compares divine making to a farmer who sows different seeds and plants different trees. In the same way, God sows Immortality in Heaven, Change on Earth, and Life and Motion in the universe. The section then says these are few and easy to number, and concludes with the wording “four in all are they,—and God Himself and Genesis, in whom are all that are.”

**Meaning:** The agricultural analogy illustrates how one maker can bring about different kinds of processes in different domains. Immortality, change, life, and motion are differentiated expressions within the ordered whole.

**Textual and interpretive caution:** The final enumeration is compressed and potentially difficult: the sentence names Immortality, Change, Life, and Motion, then refers to “four in all” and adds God and Genesis. Do not silently rewrite the count or impose a fully specified cosmological diagram. Compare the punctuation and wording against the facsimile before deciding how the clauses relate. “Genesis” should be retained as Mead's term here; do not assume without further evidence that it means only the biblical book of Genesis.

## What this tractate does—and does not—establish

- It presents a theological argument that the many made things depend upon one unmade Maker.
- It associates the divine names God, Maker, and Father with Power, Energy, and Good.
- It treats Maker and made as inseparable while preserving their different roles.
- It explains imperfection and change through the distinction between making and the conditions accompanying becoming.
- It uses a farmer's sowing as an analogy for differentiated divine activity.
- It does not provide a modern physical theory, a scientific account of the universe, or evidence for a technological interpretation.
