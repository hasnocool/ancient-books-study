# Tractate VII — The Greatest Ill Among Men Is Ignorance of God
## Section-by-section meaning guide

**Source:** G. R. S. Mead's 1906 English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 7.
**Status:** Provisional explanation based on the repository transcription; not independently verified against the 1906 facsimile.

## Reading the tractate as a whole

This short tractate is an exhortation to awaken from ignorance and seek Gnosis. It uses a connected set of images: intoxication, a flood, a harbor, a guide, light, and a cloak that must be removed. These images describe a movement from confusion and captivity toward clear-minded contemplation of the divine.

The discourse's language about the body, sensation, and materiality belongs to its ascetic and philosophical rhetoric. It should not be treated as a literal account of the body or as a reason to neglect bodily care.

## Section guide

### 1. Ignorance as intoxication and flood

Hermes addresses people as though they were drunk on ignorance and unable to contain or manage its effects. His command to become sober and look upward with the “true” eyes of the heart establishes the central contrast: ordinary perception and unexamined attachment are inadequate for recognizing the divine.

Ignorance is pictured as a flood that overwhelms the soul held within the body and prevents it from reaching the harbors of Salvation. The harbor image suggests safety and arrival; the flood represents a condition that carries the person away from that destination.

The bracketed “[true]” and the unusual apostrophe in “o`er” are transcription features to compare with the printed page. They should not be silently regularized.

### 2. The counter-current, the guide, and the cloak

Hermes urges those capable of doing so to resist the flood by taking the shore-current—glossed in the transcription as “back-current” or “up-current”—toward Salvation's harbor. The seeker then needs a guide who can lead them to the gates of Gnosis.

At that destination, Light is free of darkness and the souls are sober. The One is apprehended not through hearing, sight, or speech, but through mind and heart. This is a claim about contemplative or intellectual knowledge, not a denial that ordinary senses have practical functions.

The seeker must first remove a “cloak” made up of ignorance, corruption, darkness, sensation, and deathlike confinement. The striking image of a robber in one's own house describes how attachment can become hostile: the things a person loves can be used to keep that person captive, while aversions can also be turned against them.

### 3. The cloak's deception and the Beauty of Truth

Hermes says the cloak prevents the soul from looking upward and seeing the Beauty of Truth and the Good. It also makes people misjudge their own sensory capacities, filling them with material concerns and disordered desire.

The result is a failure of discernment: people do not hear or see what the discourse says they ought to apprehend. The section's rhetoric distinguishes mere sensation from the attentive, inward perception associated with Gnosis.

The wording “having found out the plot that it hath schemed against thee” personifies ignorance as an active deceiver. The adaptation should retain that metaphor without turning it into a literal external enemy.

## Important distinctions

- **Ignorance is more than lack of information:** the tractate portrays it as a condition that distorts perception, desire, and judgment.
- **Salvation is pictured as arrival:** the harbor metaphor expresses release from the current that carries the soul away.
- **Gnosis requires guidance and transformation:** the seeker is led to its gates and must remove the cloak of ignorance.
- **Mind and heart are modes of inward apprehension:** the passage contrasts them with the limits of ordinary sensory perception.
- **The cloak is metaphorical:** its catalogue of corruption, sensation, and darkness describes spiritual captivity, not a literal garment or a medical diagnosis.
