# Tractate XII — About the Common Mind
## Section-by-section meaning guide

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), Tractate XII.
> **Coverage:** Sections 1–23.
> **Status:** Provisional explanatory guide based on the repository's formatted transcription. It is not a translation, critical edition, or facsimile-verified commentary.

## How to read this guide

The notes distinguish what the tractate says in Mead's English from a plain-language explanation and interpretive cautions. Terms such as **Mind**, **Soul**, **Reason/Logos**, **Aeon**, **Fate**, **Providence**, and **Nature** have technical roles here; they should not automatically be equated with modern psychological, scientific, or theological categories.

## Section guide

### 1. Divine Mind and the human capacity for divinity

**Text:** Mind belongs to God's essence and is united to God as light is to the sun. The Mind in human beings is called God, and the Good Daimon is quoted as saying that gods are immortal men and men mortal gods.

**Meaning:** Human rational or spiritual Mind has a divine origin and creates a special kinship between humanity and the divine. The saying about gods and men is a paradox expressing shared qualities alongside the difference between immortal and mortal conditions.

**Caution:** Mind is not merely everyday intelligence or brain activity. The passage makes a metaphysical claim within the tractate's religious philosophy.

### 2. Mind in rational and irrational life

**Text:** Soul accompanies life, and Mind accompanies soul, but irrational creatures are described as having life and soul without Mind's specifically beneficial guidance. In human beings, Mind works against harmful impulses.

**Meaning:** The passage distinguishes the presence of life from reflective or divine guidance that can govern human conduct. Embodied pleasure and pain are portrayed as forces that can overwhelm the soul.

**Caution:** This is the author's philosophical hierarchy, not a modern biological classification of animal cognition.

### 3. Mind as physician to the soul

**Text:** Mind acts against a soul's harmful preconceptions as a physician treats illness, even through painful treatment. Godlessness is called the soul's greatest illness.

**Meaning:** Moral and spiritual correction may feel uncomfortable because it opposes habits and desires. The physician analogy makes discipline a form of healing rather than punishment for its own sake.

### 4. Human beings who lack Mind's guidance

**Text:** Human souls without Mind as pilot follow desires and irrational impulses in a way compared to irrational animals. Mind is appointed as judge over passions and desires.

**Meaning:** Merely being human does not guarantee that a person lives according to the higher capacity described in section 1. The tractate distinguishes possessing the possibility of Mind from allowing it to govern conduct.

**Caution:** The bracketed phrases in the source are editorially marked and should not be treated as unambiguous authorial wording until the printed edition is checked.

### 5. Fate and responsibility

**Text:** Tat asks why someone should be punished for wrongdoing if the act was fated. Hermes answers that bodily events occur through Fate and that suffering the consequence is also fated.

**Meaning:** The dialogue raises a tension between determinism and moral accountability. Hermes' answer places both action and consequence within Fate, but does not fully resolve every philosophical difficulty this creates.

**Caution:** Do not paraphrase this as a simple doctrine that people have no responsibility, or as a complete solution to free will. The text moves on to its main subject—Mind—while acknowledging that Fate and vice have been discussed elsewhere.

### 6. The tractate's focus: what Mind does

**Text:** Hermes brackets the previous discussion and returns to Mind's differing operation in human and irrational lives. Human beings may be reason-led or unreasoning.

**Meaning:** This is a transition. The question is no longer only whether events are fated, but what inner guidance is active in a person and how it changes the person's response to impulse.

### 7. Fated change and freedom from vice

**Text:** All people undergo Fate, birth, and change. The Mind-led person still experiences change but can be freed from vice. Hermes clarifies that such a person may undergo the same kinds of external changes without being morally defined by the corresponding wrongdoing.

**Meaning:** The passage distinguishes unavoidable conditions of embodied existence from moral character. Mind does not make a person exempt from change; it offers a way to resist vice.

**Caution:** The examples involving fornication and murder are deliberately provocative. They do not mean that committing these acts is morally irrelevant.

### 8. The Good Daimon's teaching: unity and divine activity

**Text:** Hermes quotes the Good Daimon: all things are one, especially bodies perceived by Mind; life depends on God's Energy, Power, and Aeon; intelligible things are not separated; divine Mind rules all.

**Meaning:** The tractate grounds its account of Mind in a larger vision of unity. Visible multiplicity does not mean that ultimate reality is divided into independent, unrelated powers.

**Caution:** “All things are one” is a metaphysical statement in the dialogue, not a modern physical claim about matter or energy.

### 9. Mind's relation to Fate and Law

**Text:** Hermes concludes that Mind, described as the Soul of God, rules Fate, Law, and all things. It can set a human soul above Fate or under Fate.

**Meaning:** Divine Mind is presented as superior to the order of necessity governing ordinary events. The human soul's relation to Mind determines whether it is guided by a higher principle or remains subject to Fate.

**Caution:** The precise mechanics of being above or under Fate are not fully explained. Preserve the distinction without inventing a detailed system.

### 10. Does Mind become passion?

**Text:** Tat reasons that if Mind cooperates with the impulses of irrational creatures, and those impulses are passions, Mind might itself become passionate.

**Meaning:** Tat tests the consistency of Hermes' earlier account. If Mind works through impulses, does it become like the impulses it governs?

**Caution:** This is a question, not yet the tractate's conclusion.

### 11. Incorporeal things, bodies, action, and passibility

**Text:** Hermes discusses incorporeal movers and moved bodies, describing both as subject to passion in different senses. He distinguishes active action from passive passibility and warns that terminology can mislead.

**Meaning:** The section explores how change or movement affects different kinds of being. Hermes argues that incorporeal things and bodies do not participate in movement in the same way, even though the vocabulary of passion may be applied to both.

**Caution:** The passage is conceptually dense and its wording is potentially unstable. The source has the apparent typo “invaribly” and a difficult sentence equating action and passion. Do not silently regularize the philosophical terminology before facsimile comparison.

### 12. Mind and speech as gifts to humanity

**Text:** God gives humanity Mind and speech (Logos): Mind for knowing God and speech for praising God. Proper use guides a person toward the company of gods and blessed ones.

**Meaning:** Human distinction lies not simply in vocal communication but in paired capacities for divine knowledge and meaningful expression. Speech has a religious and ethical purpose.

**Caution:** Mead's “speech” and “Logos” should be tracked carefully; Logos can carry a wider meaning than spoken language.

### 13. Voice, language, and universal Reason

**Text:** Other creatures use voice, while human speech differs. Human languages vary, but Hermes argues that humanity and its underlying speech or Reason are one. He quotes a chain: Soul in Body, Mind in Soul, Logos in Mind, Mind in God.

**Meaning:** The passage distinguishes the many outward forms of language from a common rational principle. The hierarchy also places human Reason within Mind and Mind within God.

**Caution:** This is a philosophical claim about common human rationality, not a linguistic argument that all human languages are identical.

### 14. A hierarchy of images and cosmic ordering

**Text:** Reason is the image of Mind, Mind the image of God; Body, Form, and Soul are related in an ordered sequence. The passage describes Matter, Air or vital spirit, Soul, Mind, and God, and names Necessity, Providence, and Nature as instruments of cosmic order.

**Meaning:** Reality is presented as layered, with higher principles giving form or order to lower ones. The tractate distinguishes the intelligible realm's Sameness from the changing multiplicity of bodies.

**Caution:** The source contains irregularities including “Mind Surroundeth,” “possessiong,” and “do natheless.” These require source checking; do not silently repair them in the transcription.

### 15. Number, composition, and the living Cosmos

**Text:** Number makes structure and composition possible. Matter and the Cosmos are described as filled with Life; nothing in the Cosmos is truly dead, because the Father willed it to have life throughout Aeon.

**Meaning:** The tractate sees the Cosmos as a living, ordered whole. Number explains the arrangement of composite things, while divine will sustains cosmic life.

**Caution:** “The mighty God” and “image of the mightier One” are theological descriptions of the Cosmos. They should not be flattened into a claim that the material universe is identical in every respect with the highest God.

### 16. Death as dissolution, not annihilation

**Text:** Tat asks whether living parts of the Cosmos die. Hermes says they are dissolved as compounds and renewed, not destroyed. Motion is identified as the activity of life.

**Meaning:** Death is reinterpreted as the breakdown and re-formation of composite bodies. The underlying cosmic life is not annihilated when a particular bodily arrangement dissolves.

**Caution:** This is a metaphysical account of life and death, not an empirical claim that individual organisms or bodies do not die.

### 17. Earth's apparent stillness

**Text:** Tat asks whether Earth is motionless. Hermes replies that Earth is stable while also moving rapidly, because it nourishes, generates, and brings forth things.

**Meaning:** Apparent stillness does not mean inactivity. Earth is understood through its productive and sustaining function.

**Caution:** Do not reinterpret the passage as a precise astronomical or geophysical statement. Its argument is framed in the tractate's ancient cosmology.

### 18. Motion, change, and the language of life and death

**Text:** Everything in the Cosmos moves through increase or decrease. The whole Cosmos is not subject to change in the same way as its parts. The passage distinguishes life from genesis and death from change, associating death with forgetfulness.

**Meaning:** The text separates the enduring cosmic whole from transformations within it. Its vocabulary challenges ordinary assumptions: birth and change do not necessarily mean ultimate coming-into-being or destruction.

**Caution:** The claim that Matter, Life, Spirit, Mind, and Soul are immortal belongs to this cosmological argument; it is not modern scientific consensus.

### 19. Humanity as recipient of God

**Text:** Living beings owe their immortality to Mind, especially humanity, which is described as recipient of God and co-essential with Him. God communicates through dreams, signs, birds, inward parts, wind, and trees.

**Meaning:** Human beings are given a privileged capacity to interpret signs and seek knowledge beyond immediate sensation. The text places divination and symbolic reading within its religious worldview.

**Caution:** This reports ancient religious beliefs about signs and foreknowledge. It does not establish that divination reliably predicts future events.

### 20. Humanity's relation to the elements and heavens

**Text:** Other creatures inhabit particular regions—water, earth, or air—while humans use earth, water, air, and fire, and perceive the heavens. God surrounds and permeates all.

**Meaning:** Humanity is portrayed as having a broad relationship to the Cosmos, engaging several elements and contemplating the heavens. This supports the tractate's account of human beings as uniquely positioned to seek divine knowledge.

**Caution:** The list is part of an ancient elemental cosmology, not a modern taxonomy of matter.

### 21. Contemplating God through cosmic order

**Text:** Hermes tells Tat to contemplate the ordered Cosmos, Necessity, Providence, living Matter, and the activity of gods, daimones, and humans. These are energies, ultimately energized by God. Nothing that has become or is becoming is outside God.

**Meaning:** The Cosmos itself becomes a route to contemplation. Its order and activity point beyond themselves to the divine source that sustains them.

**Caution:** The phrase “God's parts” should be read within the tractate's account of divine powers and energies, not assumed to mean that God is a spatially divisible object.

### 22. Is God in Matter?

**Text:** Tat asks whether God is in Matter. Hermes says Matter is separate from God so that it can be assigned the quality of space, but also says Matter, Body, and Essence are energies of God.

**Meaning:** The passage distinguishes God from Matter as a condition or spatial substrate while maintaining that material existence depends on divine activity. It attempts to explain how God can permeate all without being reduced to one material object among others.

**Caution:** The wording is difficult and contains the apparent grammatical anomaly “doth constituteth.” The relation between separation, space, and divine energy should be revisited against the printed edition and relevant scholarship.

### 23. God as All and the ethical form of worship

**Text:** Nothing in the All is outside God. Size, space, quality, form, and time do not surround God; God is All and permeates all. Hermes says there is one way to worship God: not to be bad.

**Meaning:** The tractate concludes by combining a sweeping metaphysical claim with a concise ethical demand. True worship is not reduced to ritual or verbal praise; it is expressed in moral conduct.

**Caution:** Keep the conclusion connected to the tractate's earlier emphasis on Mind guiding the soul away from vice.

## Major themes across the tractate

1. **Mind as divine kinship:** Human Mind is presented as a gift with a divine source, not merely as ordinary cleverness.
2. **Ethical self-government:** Mind heals the soul by resisting destructive impulses and habits.
3. **Fate and moral agency:** The dialogue acknowledges Fate while preserving a meaningful distinction between being governed by vice and being guided by Mind.
4. **Logos and speech:** Human speech is treated as a vehicle for reason, knowledge, and praise of God.
5. **Cosmic hierarchy and unity:** Body, Soul, Mind, Reason, and God are arranged in an ordered relation, while the Cosmos remains dependent on divine activity.
6. **Life, motion, and dissolution:** The tractate interprets bodily death as dissolution of compounds within a living Cosmos, not annihilation of the whole.
7. **Contemplation and ethics:** The Cosmos is a means of contemplating God, and the final test of worship is not to be bad.
