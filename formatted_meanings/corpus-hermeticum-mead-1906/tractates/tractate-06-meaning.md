# Tractate VI — In God Alone Is Good, and Elsewhere Nowhere
## Section-by-section meaning guide

**Source:** G. R. S. Mead's 1906 English translation, *Corpus Hermeticum*, Tractate 6.
**Status:** Provisional explanation based on the repository transcription; not independently verified against the 1906 facsimile.

## Reading the tractate as a whole

The tractate distinguishes the perfect Good, identified with God, from the changing world and from ordinary human judgments about what is good. It does not simply say that nothing in the world has value. Rather, it argues that created things are mixed with change, need, passion, and limitation, whereas the divine Good is complete and dependent on nothing.

The closing sections connect knowledge of God with the Beautiful and the Good, and name Devotion joined with Gnosis as the path toward them. These are claims within the tractate's philosophical and theological framework, not modern empirical propositions.

## Section guide

### 1. Good as the complete and self-sufficient divine reality

Hermes identifies Good with God eternally. Good is not subject to motion or becoming and lacks nothing. Because God needs no external thing, cannot be deprived, and has no superior or equal who could injure Him, the conditions associated with deficiency—pain, envy, anger, and dependence—do not apply.

The passage presents Good as both complete in itself and the source that supplies all things. Its language of stable energy and inexhaustible fullness describes divine sufficiency. The bracketed “[Good]” and “[Though]” are editorial features in the transcription and should be checked against the printed page.

### 2. Why Good is not found in created things in the same way

Hermes contrasts God's transcendent being with the rest of the cosmos. Created things are subject to birth and passion; they change and can suffer. The text uses the contrast between day and night to illustrate the mutual exclusion of opposites.

The cosmos is called Good in a qualified sense: insofar as it makes things, it participates in Good. Yet it is also passible, moving, and productive of passible things. The argument therefore distinguishes the source's perfect Good from the limited goodness attributed to a changing world.

The source includes explanatory bracketed glosses identifying “the other things” and the “living one” with the cosmos. Those glosses should remain visible as editorial aids rather than be mistaken for unmediated wording of an ancient original.

### 3. Relative human goodness and the danger of appetite

Hermes says that human beings determine good by comparison: what is less bad may be called good. But a good mixed with bad cannot be perfectly good and may change into its opposite. He then distinguishes the name “Good” from the reality itself, which he says no material body can contain.

The catalogue of labor, pain, desire, passion, error, and foolish thought describes the limitations of embodied human life in the tractate's terms. “Belly-lust” is singled out as a leading error because appetite can make people turn away from the highest Good.

This is a moral and metaphysical argument in the text. It should not be treated as a condemnation of all bodily needs, nor as practical health advice. The passage's sharp rhetoric needs to be understood in its philosophical context.

### 4. The Beautiful and the Good beyond visible images

Hermes gives thanks for the insight that the perfect Good is not an ordinary object in the world. He associates the Beautiful with the essence of the Good and suggests that God's essence, if one may speak of it in such terms, is Beautiful.

Visible things are described as images or pictures; the realities that do not meet the eye are treated as more fundamental, especially the Beautiful and the Good. The point is not that visible objects are unreal in every sense, but that sight alone cannot reach the highest reality.

The transcription's bracketed phrases, including “[essence]” and “[the eye are the realities],” are text-critical cautions. They must be compared with the 1906 printed page before being treated as settled wording.

### 5. Conceiving God, the Beautiful, and the path

Hermes argues that conceiving God also leads the mind toward the Beautiful and the Good, which cannot be separated from God. The search for God is therefore also a search for the Beautiful.

The section names the path as “Devotion joined with Gnosis.” In this context, Devotion indicates a committed orientation toward the divine, while Gnosis means a kind of knowing or insight. The wording does not justify reducing the path to belief alone or to the accumulation of ordinary information.

The claims that the Beautiful and Good cannot be joined with other living things are part of the tractate's account of divine transcendence. They should be read within that argument rather than expanded into unsupported claims about the nature of all earthly beauty.

### 6. Mistaking dependent things for the highest Good

Hermes criticizes people who do not know or follow the path of Devotion and Gnosis but nevertheless call human beings and worldly conditions beautiful and good in the highest sense. He says they mistake bad for good and fear losing the things to which they are attached.

The passage also acknowledges a tension: people cannot simply flee or hate all such things, because they need them to live. The warning is therefore about confusing necessary or desired things with the absolute Good, not about pretending that embodied life can be lived without material needs.

## Important distinctions

- **Absolute Good and relative good:** God is identified with Good itself; worldly goods are limited and mixed with change.
- **Participation is not identity:** the cosmos is called Good insofar as it makes, but it is not the unchanging Good in the same sense as God.
- **The Beautiful is more than visual attractiveness:** the tractate treats the Beautiful as a divine reality not exhausted by visible images.
- **Devotion and Gnosis belong together:** the stated path combines orientation toward God with insight.
- **Critique of appetite is textual, not medical advice:** the passage's moral vocabulary should not be turned into modern health guidance.

## Source-text cautions

The repository transcription has not been checked against the 1906 facsimile. Several features merit collation, including bracketed additions in sections 1, 2, and 4; the punctuation and wording of “the Good can never be, but only be in the ingenerate” in section 2; “the name alone of Good” and the catalogue of human conditions in section 3; and the bracketed phrases around the Beautiful in section 4. These are review targets, not grounds for silent emendation.
