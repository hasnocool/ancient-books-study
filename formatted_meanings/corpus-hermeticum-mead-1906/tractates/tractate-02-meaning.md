## Short summary

Tractate II is a philosophical dialogue about motion, space, the bodiless, Mind, and God. Hermes argues that moving bodies require a containing space and an ordering cause that is not itself simply another moving body. He then distinguishes space from God, describes Mind and Word as bodiless principles, and identifies God with the Good and the source of all things. The dialogue closes with a teaching about divine fatherhood and human generation.

## Explanation

### Sections 1–4 — Motion, the cosmos, and containing space

- **What the text says:** Hermes asks whether everything moved is moved in something and by something. He argues that the containing space must be greater than the moving body. Since the cosmos is both vast and moving, the space in which it moves must be greater still. He concludes that this space is bodiless.
- **What it means:** The dialogue uses a chain of questions to move from ordinary experience to a metaphysical problem: what makes motion possible, and what contains the totality of moving bodies? The argument depends on distinguishing the material cosmos from the condition that allows it to move.
- **Textual evidence:** Sections 1–4 establish the relation between mover, moved object, containing space, and the bodiless.
- **Uncertainty or alternatives:** The argument is philosophical rather than a measured cosmological model. The passage does not define space using modern mathematical or physical concepts.

### Sections 5–8 — God, stability, and contrary motion

- **What the text says:** Hermes distinguishes space from God, describing God as the power able to contain all space. He argues that motion depends on something stable and explains the movements of heavenly spheres through opposition or contrariety. A swimmer moving through water illustrates how resistance can provide stability.
- **What it means:** The dialogue presents stability not as the absence of all movement but as a condition that makes ordered movement possible. The swimmer example makes the abstract argument accessible: movement can be guided or sustained through resistance.
- **Textual evidence:** Sections 5–7 distinguish space and God and discuss the spheres; section 8 gives the swimmer analogy and identifies an incorporeal principle as the source of cosmic motion.
- **Uncertainty or alternatives:** Mead's text contains likely OCR corruption in section 7 (“spable one”). The exact reading must be checked against the 1906 print. The account of the Bears and the spheres should be read in its ancient cosmological setting, not mapped uncritically onto current astronomy.

### Sections 9–11 — The mover and the question of emptiness

- **What the text says:** Asclepius asks whether bodies move in void space and points to apparently empty containers. Hermes answers that no existing thing is truly void: containers are filled with air and spirit. The truly void belongs only to what does not exist.
- **What it means:** The text challenges the everyday use of “empty.” A vessel that appears empty still contains air; therefore, apparent emptiness is not absolute non-being. The argument supports the tractate's larger claim that the cosmos is not surrounded or sustained by a simple material nothingness.
- **Textual evidence:** Sections 9–11 discuss animate movers, apparently empty vessels, air, and the distinction between void and hollow.
- **Uncertainty or alternatives:** This is an ancient philosophical argument, not an empirical account of vacuum physics. Its claims should not be treated as equivalent to modern experiments or definitions of vacuum.

### Sections 12–16 — The bodiless, Mind, and the Good

- **What the text says:** Hermes identifies the bodiless with Mind and Word, describing it as self-containing and preserving what exists. He then says that God is not Mind, Spirit, or Light in the same sense, but the cause that makes them be. God is to be honoured as the Good and Father; the Good gives all things and receives nothing.
- **What it means:** The dialogue distinguishes the ultimate divine source from the principles through which reality is understood and ordered. Mind, Word, Spirit, and Light are meaningful descriptions, but God is presented as their cause rather than merely one item within that set. The Good is defined by its giving, not by receiving or needing something beyond itself.
- **Textual evidence:** Section 12 describes the bodiless as Mind and Word. Sections 13–16 distinguish God from Mind, Spirit, and Light and identify God with the Good.
- **Uncertainty or alternatives:** Mead's translation choices for “Mind,” “Word,” “substance,” and “Good” need comparison with the Greek text and specialist scholarship. The English rendering alone cannot settle every philosophical nuance.

### Section 17 — Fatherhood and generation

- **What the text says:** Hermes calls God Father because God is the maker of all. He then praises human childbearing and describes childlessness in strongly condemnatory terms, including a post-mortem punishment.
- **What it means:** The tractate extends its account of divine generation into a social and ethical teaching about human reproduction. The analogy links the divine role as source of life with human procreation.
- **Textual evidence:** Section 17 explicitly connects the name “Father” with making and applies the principle to having children.
- **Uncertainty or alternatives:** This passage reflects a particular ancient social and religious value system. It should not be universalized as medical, moral, or spiritual advice. Its historical context and relation to other ancient views of marriage, kinship, and childlessness require independent research.

## Key themes and concepts

- **Motion and stability:** Movement is explained through a relation between what moves, what is moved, and a stable condition that makes motion possible.
- **Space:** The dialogue distinguishes containing space from the material cosmos and from God.
- **The bodiless:** Mind and Word are described as incorporeal principles that contain and preserve what exists.
- **God as cause:** God is not reduced to Mind, Spirit, or Light but is described as the source that makes them possible.
- **The Good:** God's goodness is expressed as the source that gives all things and receives nothing.
- **Fatherhood and generation:** Divine creation is connected rhetorically with human procreation, in terms shaped by the tractate's ancient social assumptions.

## Interpretive limits and open questions

- The source is Mead's English translation, not the Greek text. Key philosophical terms require comparison with the original language and reputable scholarship.
- The formatted transcription has not been collated against the 1906 facsimile. Apparent OCR errors must remain unresolved until checked.
- The ancient account of space and motion should not be represented as a precursor that straightforwardly proves modern physics.
- The passage's description of the heavenly spheres belongs to its historical cosmological framework.
- Section 17's claims about childlessness are historically situated and should not be treated as contemporary guidance.
- The tractate's date, compositional context, and relationship to other philosophical traditions require external scholarship.
