# Tractate XIII — The Secret Sermon on the Mountain
## Section-by-section meaning guide

> **Source:** G. R. S. Mead, *Thrice-Greatest Hermes*, Volume II (1906), Tractate XIII, represented by the repository's formatted transcription.
>
> **Status:** Provisional explanatory guide. It follows the source's **22 numbered sections**. It is not a translation, a facsimile collation, or a claim that the text's metaphysical assertions are empirical facts.

## Reading framework

This tractate presents Rebirth as a transformation in the mode of knowing and being, not ordinary physical birth. Its language is initiatory and symbolic: the womb and seed are Wisdom and the Good; the sower is God's Will; Mind is the field of transformed perception; and the Twelve and Ten describe a moral-spiritual struggle and its overcoming. The text's own distinctions should be retained rather than converted into modern psychology, biology, or physics.

## Section guide

### 1 — The request for the teaching of Rebirth
Tat recalls Hermes' earlier statement that salvation requires Rebirth and that the teaching would be disclosed when Tat became a stranger to the world. Tat has prepared himself by turning thought away from the world's illusion, but still asks what matter, womb, and seed produce the new human being.

**Meaning:** Tat's preparation is a change in orientation. The question is not about biological conception but about the origin of a transformed spiritual person. The parenthetical “[Now]” and the dialogue's unusual capitalisation belong to the supplied transcription and should be checked against the printed page.

### 2 — Wisdom, the Good, and God's Will
Hermes identifies silent Wisdom as the womb, the True Good as the seed, and God's Will as the sower. The begotten one is “all in all” and composed from all powers. Hermes says this spiritual race is not taught in the ordinary way; God restores its memory when willing.

**Meaning:** Rebirth is represented as an awakening to a divine origin already latent or remembered, rather than information acquired only from an external instructor. “All in all” and “all powers” are theological formulations; their exact force should not be narrowed without textual and historical evidence.

### 3 — Hermes describes his own transformation
Tat asks for direct answers. Hermes says that, through God's mercy, the Simple Vision arises within him and he passes into an undying body. He is “born in Mind”; his former composite form has been dismembered, and bodily sight cannot comprehend what he now is.

**Meaning:** The contrast is between composite, sense-dependent existence and a mode of being apprehended through Mind. The passage's “body” language is paradoxical and should not automatically be read as a claim about a second physical organism. The source's lower-case “i” in “i am not as I was before” is a transcription watchpoint, not a correction to make silently.

### 4 — Tat's disorientation and the agent of Rebirth
Tat says he is plunged into frenzy and can no longer see himself. Hermes compares the desired passage beyond the ordinary self to being awake while dreaming. He identifies the Son of God, the One Man, acting by God's Will, as the author of Rebirth.

**Meaning:** The loss of the familiar self-image is presented as part of the transition. “Awake” within sleep expresses a paradoxical state of awareness beyond ordinary sensory consciousness. The identity and theological role of the “One Man” deserve contextual study; this guide does not impose a later doctrinal system upon the phrase.

### 5 — The mortal form changes
Tat says his former senses have been arrested and that he sees Hermes' greatness as identical with his distinctive form. A lacuna interrupts the text. Hermes corrects Tat: the mortal form changes daily through growth and decline and is therefore not the true, stable reality.

**Meaning:** The passage distinguishes a changing bodily appearance from the unchanging reality sought in Rebirth. The lacuna is explicit and must not be reconstructed from another translation without documenting that source separately.

### 6 — The characteristics of what is true
Hermes describes the true as untroubled, indefinable, without colour or figure, unchanging, luminous, self-comprehending, and beyond bodily containment. Tat struggles to understand. Hermes asks how the senses could grasp what is not simply fire, earth, water, or air and is known only through power and energy.

**Meaning:** Ordinary perception classifies things through bodily qualities. The object of Rebirth is described as transcending those categories. The elemental comparisons are rhetorical contrasts, not a physical recipe or a scientific account of matter.

### 7 — Withdrawal into oneself and the Twelve tormentors
Hermes tells Tat to withdraw into himself, will the transformation, and put bodily senses out of work. He lists twelve tormentors: Not-knowing, Grief, Intemperance, Concupiscence, Unrighteousness, Avarice, Error, Envy, Guile, Anger, Rashness, and Malice. They trouble the person imprisoned in the body. They depart through divine mercy, though not all at once. The source then contains a lacuna before a reference to the Logos.

**Meaning:** The Twelve personify destructive conditions that obstruct spiritual understanding. They are not presented here as twelve literal beings or a clinical diagnostic system. Their gradual departure is linked to divine mercy and moral transformation. Preserve the lacuna; do not complete the missing transition by guesswork.

### 8 — The first powers displace the torments
Hermes calls for silence and announces purification through divine powers. Gnosis displaces Not-knowing; Joy drives out Sorrow; Self-control expels Intemperance. The powers are welcomed as they arrive.

**Meaning:** The text pairs destructive conditions with countervailing virtues or states of knowledge. Gnosis is more than accumulating facts: it changes the person's orientation and experience. The source's line break and hyphenation in “Not- knowing” should be treated as layout/transcription detail pending collation.

### 9 — The remaining powers and the flight of darkness
Hermes names Continence against Desire, Righteousness against Unrighteousness, Sharing-with-all against Avarice, and Truth against Error. With Truth come the Good, Life, and Light; Envy and the torments of Darkness flee. A lacuna appears after the reference to Desire. The source includes a translator comparison: “without judgement” and another reading, “without effort.”

**Meaning:** The sequence presents Rebirth as ethical as well as intellectual. The powers are not merely abstract ideas: they are active transformations of conduct and perception. The variant “without judgement / without effort” must remain visible as a textual issue until the edition is checked; do not choose one reading silently.

### 10 — The Ten, the Twelve, and intellectual birth
Hermes says that when the Ten arrives and drives out the Twelve, birth in understanding—glossed as “intellectual birth”—is complete. Those who receive this birth are made gods. The recipient knows himself as belonging to Light and Life and is filled with bliss.

**Meaning:** The Ten represents the powers that overcome the Twelve destructive conditions. The arithmetic and symbolism are central to the tractate, but the source does not provide a full systematic exposition here. “Made into Gods” belongs to the text's language of divine transformation and should not be flattened into a literal claim about biological or political status.

### 11 — Tat's expanded perception
Tat says he now sees through the energy of Mind rather than ordinary eyesight. He experiences himself as present in the heavens, elements, animals, plants, and before, within, and after the womb. He asks how the Twelve are driven out by the Ten.

**Meaning:** Tat describes an enlarged participation in the All through Mind. The catalogue expresses a changed relation to the cosmos, not necessarily physical travel or literal bodily ubiquity. His question leads Hermes to explain the relation between the Twelve and Ten.

### 12 — The twelvefold constitution and the unity of the Ten
Hermes compares the human dwelling-place to a circle of twelve types of life, composed of elements and forming one nature. Human delusion sees divisions where action is one; Rashness and Wrath cannot be separated. The Ten drives out the Twelve and gives birth to souls. Life and Light are unified where the One has being from Spirit; the One contains the Ten, and the Ten the One.

**Meaning:** The passage uses a symbolic account of human constitution to explain how apparent multiplicity can conceal unity. The Twelve are differentiated in the deluded experience but operate as a connected whole. The Ten is associated with the generation of souls and the restoration of unity. The phrase “circle of the twelve types-of-life” should be checked against the printed text; do not assume it maps neatly onto a later zodiacal or astrological system without evidence.

### 13 — Seeing the All in Mind
Tat declares that he sees the All and himself in Mind. Hermes identifies this as Rebirth: no longer seeing from the body's spatial viewpoint. A lacuna interrupts the sentence. Hermes says he withheld part of the sermon to avoid speaking against the All before the multitude, since God does not will indiscriminate disclosure.

**Meaning:** Rebirth is defined as a transformation of perspective: Mind becomes the mode through which the All is apprehended. The source's parenthetical description of the body's viewpoint and the lacuna should be retained. The restriction on disclosure introduces an initiatory boundary between the prepared hearer and the wider public.

### 14 — The essential birth and the natural body
Tat asks whether the body made from the powers can be dissolved. Hermes rebukes the question and distinguishes the natural body perceived by sense from the essential birth. The first dissolves and dies; the latter cannot be touched by death. Hermes tells Tat he has been born a god, a son of the One.

**Meaning:** The passage distinguishes mortal embodiment from the spiritual identity attained through Rebirth. It does not establish that the ordinary physical body becomes indestructible. The distinction between “natural body” and “essential birth” is crucial to avoid misreading the claim.

### 15 — The Shepherd and the Eight
Tat asks to hear the hymn Hermes heard when he reached the Eight, identified by the editor as the Ogdoad of Powers. Hermes recalls the Shepherd, Mind of all masterhood, who foretold the event but left Hermes to learn and perceive further things himself. The powers within him begin to sing.

**Meaning:** The Eight/Ogdoad marks a stage in the tractate's spiritual ascent. Hermes' account emphasizes both instruction and direct realization: the Shepherd provides a foundation, but Hermes must perceive and learn. The wording “e'en as they are in all” and the punctuation around the dialogue deserve checking.

### 16 — Preparation for the Secret Hymnody
Tat asks to hear the hymn. Hermes says it is kept hidden in silence and is not taught indiscriminately. He instructs Tat to stand in an open place beneath the sky, facing the southern wind at sunset and the eastern wind at sunrise, and to worship. The heading “The Secret Hymnody” closes the section.

**Meaning:** The hymn is framed as a disciplined ritual act within the narrative. The directions are part of Mead's translated text; their precise wording and punctuation should be checked before drawing conclusions about ritual practice. The heading marks a clear structural transition from dialogue to hymn.

### 17 — The cosmos is summoned to praise its maker
The hymn calls on the world's natures: Earth, the Abyss, trees, heavens, and winds. The speaker praises the Lord who founded all, fixed Earth, suspended Heaven, gave Ocean its waters for inhabited and uninhabited regions, and made Fire shine for gods and humans. God is praised as the Eye of Mind.

**Meaning:** The hymn portrays the cosmos as a participant in praise. Its catalogue of Earth, Heaven, Ocean, Fire, and the winds echoes the tractate's cosmic language and presents the world as ordered by a single Lord. The invocation is poetic and liturgical, not a technical cosmological description.

### 18 — The inner powers join the hymn
The speaker calls the powers within to hymn the One and All. Gnosis illumines; Mind brings joy; Self-control, Righteousness, Sharing-all, Truth, the Good, Life, and Light each contribute praise. The speaker thanks God as the Energy and Power of all the powers and energies.

**Meaning:** The virtues and powers that displaced the Twelve now become the voices of worship. The transformed person is represented as an ordered chorus. The source's “mond alone can see” is an apparent transcription anomaly and must be flagged, not silently changed to “mind” without checking the facsimile.

### 19 — The Logos as reasonable offering
The speaker asks that the All be taken back through the Logos as a reasonable oblation. Life is asked to preserve what is within, Light to illumine it, and God to inspire it. God's Mind is described as shepherding the Word, and God as Creator and giver of Spirit.

**Meaning:** Logos is both the medium of articulate praise and part of the relation between divine Mind and creation. The language of offering is intellectual and spiritual as well as liturgical. The source has conspicuous punctuation and wording defects—“It it Thy Mind,” angle-bracketed commas, and “in-spirit”—which need page-level verification.

### 20 — Praise through the elements and the mind-seen Cosmos
The speaker praises God through Fire, Air, Earth, Water, Spirit, and creatures, and says that rest has been found in God's Will. Tat says he has placed the hymn in his own Cosmos. Hermes clarifies that this is the Cosmos seen by Mind alone. Tat agrees that the hymn has illumined his mind and asks to praise God from his natural mind as well.

**Meaning:** The passage distinguishes the mind-perceived Cosmos from the ordinary sensory world. Tat's participation is an inward appropriation of the hymn, not the creation of a second physical universe. The shift between the speaker's hymn and Tat's dialogue should be preserved.

### 21 — Tat offers praise through the Word
Hermes cautions Tat against offering praise carelessly. Tat says he speaks what he beholds in Mind and addresses God as Parent, Lord, and Mind. Hermes tells him to add “through the Word” (Logos). Tat thanks Hermes for teaching him to sing.

**Meaning:** Proper praise is aligned with Mind and Logos rather than being an unconsidered utterance. The instruction “through the Word” connects Tat's offering to the tractate's larger account of divine articulation and order. The initial lower-case “o God” is a source watchpoint.

### 22 — Secrecy and the completion of instruction
Hermes rejoices that Tat has brought forth imperishable fruits of Truth. He asks Tat to keep silent about his virtue and not disclose the manner of Rebirth to others, lest they be accused of speaking against the All. Teacher and hearer have both attended to the lesson; Tat has become, in Mind, a Knower of himself and their common Sire.

**Meaning:** The conclusion presents knowledge as a transformed state that carries obligations of discretion. The secrecy motif is part of the tractate's initiatory framing; it should not be mistaken for proof of a specific historical secret society. The source's “though hast brought” appears defective and requires facsimile checking.

## Conceptual cautions

1. **Rebirth:** The tractate describes spiritual or intellectual transformation. Do not conflate it with reincarnation or ordinary biological birth without separate evidence.
2. **Body:** “Body that can never die” and “essential birth” must be read alongside the explicit contrast with the natural, dissolving body.
3. **The Twelve and the Ten:** They function as symbolic structures in the text. Avoid asserting an exact astrological, numerological, or psychological system beyond what the passage supports.
4. **The One, Mind, Logos, and God:** These terms are related but not interchangeable. Preserve their distinctions and the translation's own uncertainty.
5. **The hymn:** Its elemental and cosmic imagery is devotional poetry, not a modern scientific description.
6. **Secrecy:** The instruction to keep silent belongs to the tractate's initiatory rhetoric; it does not by itself establish a particular historical institution.
