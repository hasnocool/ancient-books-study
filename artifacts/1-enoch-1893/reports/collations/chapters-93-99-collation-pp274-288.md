## 2. Translation witness: repository comparison

The repository retains the 1893 printed chapter sequence and broadly follows its translation. The following distinctions should be preserved:

- **XCIII.11–14:** Charles prints these four verses in square brackets. He explicitly judges them out of place and foreign in character to XCI–CIV, associating them instead with the Book of Celestial Physics. The repository marks all four as interpolations, consistent with the 1893 presentation.
- **XCIV.1–11:** The repository's Chapter 94 follows the 1893 text in substance. Keep Charles's “a (future) generation” in XCIV.2 as a parenthetical qualification rather than silently integrating it into the base wording. XCIV.7 begins “Woe to those that build their houses with sin”; retain the verse boundary and distinguish Charles's note from translation.
- **XCV.1–7:** The repository has the expected lament and warnings. At XCV.3, the 1893 sentence continues across the OCR page break; do not treat the OCR's displaced line order as a different verse sequence.
- **XCVI.2:** The 1893 translation brackets this verse; the repository marks it as an interpolation. Charles argues it is contextually inappropriate because it has the righteous hiding in caves, whereas the surrounding context describes the wicked fleeing. Preserve the bracket and label Charles's judgment as commentary.
- **XCVII.1–10:** The repository's chapter corresponds to the printed text. Note that the OCR at p.280 contains the transition from the first textual witness to Charles's discussion of G and G1; the apparatus should not be mistaken for part of the running translation.
- **XCVIII.1–16:** The repository's “furnace of lire” at XCVIII.3 is an obvious transcription error for printed “furnace of fire.” This is supported by the readable 1893 OCR and should be corrected in the formatted text. Other phrases needing careful future facsimile check include XCVIII.4–5 and XCVIII.15, where syntax and punctuation can be distorted by OCR.
- **XCIX.1–16:** The repository generally follows the printed translation. At XCIX.2 the repository's “mean, into sinners” is an OCR/transcription corruption; the 1893 text reads “i.e. into sinners.” Correct this obvious artifact. XCIX.3's opening “In those days” is the start of a new sentence, not a continuation of the preceding apparatus.

## 4. Charles's commentary, separately attributed

- **XCIII.11–14:** Charles regards the four verses as foreign to XCI–CIV and more appropriate to the Book of Celestial Physics. His argument is literary and contextual; the printed edition nevertheless includes them in brackets.
- **XCIV:** Charles describes this as the practical portion of the work, immediately following XCI.12–19 in his reconstructed original order. He sees its warning against the wicked as directed partly against Sadducean or Greek influences.
- **XCIV.5 and XCIX.2:** Charles connects “make wisdom wicked” and the perversion of the eternal law with opponents who distort inherited teaching and adopt foreign customs. This is his historical interpretation.
- **XCV:** Charles relates the opening lament to Jeremiah 9:1 and sees the warning in XCV.3 as anticipating the righteous' coming period of supremacy.
- **XCVI.2:** Charles rejects the verse as an interpolation because it depicts the righteous hiding in caves; he argues the context instead has the wicked fleeing from judgment. He links the cave imagery to Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21.
- **XCVII:** Charles describes the chapter as largely threats against the wicked. He reads the prayer of the righteous as a cry for judgment and vengeance, and identifies the strong separation from sinners as Pharisaic.
- **XCVIII–CII:** Charles marks a fresh division within XCI–CIV. In his outline, XCVIII–CII address the foolish, while CII.4–CIV.2 address the wise. He reads XCVIII.4–5 as rejecting the view that sin is innate or unavoidable: sin is of human devising and therefore culpable.
- **XCIX.3:** Charles explains the angels' presentation of prayers as a mediatorial function rooted in the Hebrew Bible, with a later parallel in Revelation 8:3–4.
- **XCIX.4–6:** He associates the coming conflict with the “period of the Sword” and interprets the references to family and national conflict as eschatological violence.
- **XCIX.7–10:** Charles reads the passage as a denunciation of idolatry and superstition, and notes that Tertullian's *De Idololatria* IV preserves a Latin citation supporting the “not according to knowledge” reading.
- **XCIX.13–16:** Charles connects the false measures, oppressive building practices, rejection of ancestral inheritance, and persecution with the Mosaic law and the final judgment.
- **C.1–5 (opening on p.287):** Charles interprets the fratricidal violence as an expansion of XCIX.6. He tentatively relates C.2 to the killing of Antigonus by Aristobulus I (Josephus, *Antiquities* 13.11.1–2), and interprets the “secret places” of C.4 as the earthly abysses where fallen angels are confined. He argues the guarded righteous of C.5 are departed souls, not living people, and reads their long sleep as extending until the final judgment at the close of the tenth week. These are Charles's exegetical proposals, not direct textual readings.
